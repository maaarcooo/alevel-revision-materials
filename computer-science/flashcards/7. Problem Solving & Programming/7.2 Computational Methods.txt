What are computational methods? | Problem-solving techniques that use algorithms and mathematical models to analyse, simulate, and solve complex problems using a computer
What is a computable problem? | A problem that can be solved using an algorithm within a finite, realistic amount of time
A problem that can be solved using an algorithm within a finite, realistic amount of time — what is this? | A computable problem
What do computable problems typically consist of? | Inputs, outputs, and calculations
Name three real-world constraints that affect whether a problem can be solved computationally | Processing power, speed, and memory
Why might a theoretically computable problem be impractical to solve? | The amount of resources or length of time required may be too great
What makes a problem unsuitable for a computational solution? | It requires human judgment, creativity, or cultural/ethical input that algorithms could oversimplify or misinterpret
What is problem recognition? | Determining whether a problem exists, clearly identifying what it is, and defining the system requirements
Who states the requirements during problem recognition? | Stakeholders
Name two ways system requirements may be defined during problem recognition | Analysing strengths and weaknesses of the current solution, and considering the types of data involved (inputs, outputs, stored data)
What is problem decomposition? | Continually breaking a large problem into smaller sub-problems until each can be represented as a self-contained subroutine
Why might decomposition allow reuse of existing code? | Sub-problems may match tasks already solved by pre-coded modules or libraries
Give two benefits of problem decomposition for team-based development | Different teams can work on separate sections in parallel, and individual modules can be independently designed, developed, and tested
Explain how problem decomposition simplifies debugging | Errors can be identified, located, and fixed within individual modules rather than searching the entire application
Why is abstraction often applied before decomposition? | Removing non-essential elements first allows programmers to focus on the critical aspects when breaking the problem down
What are the three stages of divide and conquer? | Divide (halve the problem into sub-problems), conquer (solve each sub-problem, often recursively), and merge (recombine solutions into the overall solution)
Name two algorithms that use divide and conquer | Binary search and merge sort (or quick sort)
What is the time complexity of divide and conquer algorithms? | $O(\log n)$
Give one benefit of using divide and conquer | The problem size halves with each iteration, so as the problem grows the time to solve it does not grow as significantly
Give one drawback of divide and conquer | It uses recursion, which risks stack overflow and makes large programs difficult to trace
What is 'decrease and conquer'? | A variant of divide and conquer where the problem is reduced by less than half in every iteration
How does divide and conquer differ from general problem decomposition? | Divide and conquer specifically halves the problem with each iteration, solves sub-problems recursively, and recombines solutions. Decomposition does not require halving or recombination
What is task parallelism? | When several tasks or sub-tasks are carried out concurrently to speed up overall completion time
What is abstraction in problem solving? | The removal of unnecessary details from a problem to simplify it and allow focus on the core aspects of the solution
The removal of unnecessary details from a problem to simplify it and focus on core aspects — what technique is this? | Abstraction (representational abstraction)
How do levels of abstraction help manage a large project? | The project is split into simpler component parts that can be dealt with by different teams, with details about other layers hidden
What is abstraction by generalisation? | Grouping together different sections of a problem with similar underlying functionality, allowing segments to be coded together and reused
Give an example of abstraction in a computer game | Complex algorithms controlling NPCs and game physics are hidden from the user, who only interacts with visual elements and controls
What is backtracking? | A problem-solving technique that builds a solution incrementally by exploring paths; if a path is invalid, the algorithm returns to the last valid point and tries an alternate path
A problem-solving technique that explores paths incrementally, returning to the last valid point when a dead end is reached — what is this? | Backtracking
Give an example of backtracking in data structures | Depth-first graph or tree traversal
Give one benefit of backtracking | It is guaranteed to find a solution if one exists
Give one drawback of backtracking | It can have high time complexity and may not find the most optimal solution
Why is backtracking not ideal for solving strategic problems? | It systematically explores all paths without evaluating which is most likely to lead to the best solution
What is data mining? | A technique for identifying patterns, trends, or correlations in large datasets (big data) that are not immediately obvious, using algorithms and statistical methods
A technique for identifying patterns and correlations in large datasets that are not immediately obvious — what is this? | Data mining
Give two uses of data mining in business | Predicting future trends based on past data, and identifying customer purchasing patterns to inform marketing decisions
Why must data mining comply with GDPR? | It often involves processing personal data, so organisations must follow data protection legislation
Give one benefit of data mining | It can identify patterns and trends not immediately obvious to humans, enabling better predictions and informed decisions
Give one limitation of data mining | It can identify patterns but may not explain the reasons why they exist
What are heuristics? | A non-optimal, 'rule-of-thumb' approach to problem-solving that finds an approximate, 'good enough' solution quickly rather than the optimal one
A non-optimal approach using rules of thumb to find an approximate solution quickly — what is this? | Heuristics
What is the key trade-off when using heuristics? | Speed vs accuracy: heuristics find solutions faster but do not guarantee the best or most accurate result
What is an intractable problem? | A problem for which the optimal solution takes an unreasonably long time to find
A problem for which the optimal solution takes an unreasonably long time to find — what is this called? | An intractable problem
Name two problems where heuristics are used | The Travelling Salesman Problem and the A* pathfinding algorithm
What is a local optimum in the context of heuristics? | A point where the algorithm appears near the best solution in its local area, but this is not the globally best solution
What is performance modelling? | Using mathematical methods to simulate and predict the behaviour and performance of a system before it is used in the real world
Using mathematical methods to simulate system behaviour before real-world deployment — what technique is this? | Performance modelling
Why is performance modelling useful for safety-critical systems? | It allows testing without real-world risk, as systems such as those on airplanes cannot safely undergo real trial runs
Give one benefit of performance modelling over real-world testing | It is cheaper and less time-consuming than setting up and running real-world tests
Give one limitation of performance modelling | Results are only as accurate as the data and rules fed into the model; incorrect assumptions produce incorrect results
In which two phases of software development can performance modelling be used? | The design phase (for architectural decisions) and the testing phase (to simulate real-world scenarios)
What is pipelining as a computational method? | Carrying out multiple tasks concurrently, where the output of one process becomes the input of the next, improving overall efficiency
Carrying out multiple tasks concurrently where each task's output feeds into the next — what computational method is this? | Pipelining
What three steps are involved in applying pipelining to a task? | Break the task into individual sub-tasks, arrange them in sequential order, and allow multiple tasks to operate at the same time
What does the pipe symbol (vertical bar) do in Unix? | Connects the output of one command to the input of the next, allowing commands to be chained together
What is visualisation as a computational method? | Presenting data or concepts in graphical or visual form that is easier for humans to understand, making it possible to identify otherwise hidden trends and patterns
Name three types of visual representation used in problem solving | Flowcharts, UML diagrams, and wireframes
What are wireframes? | Low-fidelity design plans representing the skeletal framework of a program or website, used for layout planning and user experience design
Give one benefit of visualisation | It simplifies complex concepts, making it easier to spot trends and patterns that may not have been noticed before
Give one limitation of visualisation | It can show data visually but cannot explain why something is the way it is, and different people may interpret visualisations differently
