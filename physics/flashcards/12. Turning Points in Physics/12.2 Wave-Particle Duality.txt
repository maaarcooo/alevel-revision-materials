What is Newton's corpuscular theory of light? | Light is made of tiny particles (corpuscles) emitted by luminous objects
What did corpuscular theory predict about objects that emit light? | They slowly lose mass
How did corpuscular theory explain reflection? | A repulsive force from the surface reverses the velocity component perpendicular to it and leaves the parallel component unchanged
How did corpuscular theory explain light bending towards the normal on entering a denser medium? | A short-range attraction at the boundary increases the velocity component perpendicular to it, while the parallel component is unchanged
Which two phenomena of light could corpuscular theory not explain? | Diffraction and interference
How did corpuscular theory explain sharp shadows? | Corpuscles travel in straight lines
What is Huygens' theory of light? | Light is a wave that travels as a series of wavefronts
What medium did Huygens propose light travels through? | The luminiferous aether, a massless medium filling the Universe
What is Huygens' principle? | Every point on a wavefront acts as a secondary source of wavelets, which combine to form the next wavefront
How did Huygens' wave theory explain reflection? | Different parts of the wavefront reach the surface at different times, and each point of reflection emits new wavelets, so the new wavefront forms in a new direction
How did Huygens' wave theory explain refraction? | The part of the wavefront that reaches the boundary first slows down before the rest, so the wavefront changes direction
How do corpuscular and wave theory predictions differ for the speed of light entering a denser medium? | Corpuscular theory: light speeds up. Wave theory: light slows down
Which two phenomena of light could both corpuscular and wave theory explain? | Reflection and refraction
What factor about Newton himself favoured his theory of light over Huygens'? | Newton was already highly respected for his work on motion and gravity
What experimental limitation stopped the corpuscular and wave theories being tested against each other at the time? | The speed of light in a dense medium could not be measured, and diffraction of light had not been observed
What did Young's double slit experiment produce on the screen? | Alternating bright and dark interference fringes
What pattern does corpuscular theory predict for light passing through two slits? | Only two bright regions, one behind each slit
Why is a single slit placed before the double slit in Young's experiment? | Light diffracting from the single slit reaches both slits in phase, so the two slits act as coherent sources
Explain how wave theory accounts for the fringes in Young's double slit experiment | Wavelets from the two slits overlap. Bright fringes form where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths (in phase), dark fringes where it is an odd number of half wavelengths (antiphase)
Which theory of light did Young's double slit experiment provide evidence for? | Huygens' wave theory
Whose measurement of the speed of light in water supported wave theory over corpuscular theory? | Foucault's
What did Foucault find about the speed of light in water compared with air? | It is slower in water, as wave theory predicted
What did Maxwell's equations predict the existence of? | Electromagnetic waves
What is an electromagnetic wave? | Oscillating electric and magnetic fields in phase, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel
What produces an electromagnetic wave? | An accelerating charge
Why can electromagnetic waves travel without a medium? | The changing electric field generates a changing magnetic field and vice versa, so the wave is self-propagating
What is Maxwell's equation for the speed of electromagnetic waves in a vacuum? | $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}}$
What is $\mu_0$? | The permeability of free space, which relates magnetic flux density to the current producing it in free space
What is $\varepsilon_0$? | The permittivity of free space, which relates electric field strength to the charged object producing it in free space
What speed do $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{H m}^{-1}$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F m}^{-1}$ give for electromagnetic waves? | $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12}}} = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
How did Hertz produce radio waves? | A high voltage across a short air gap made sparks jump the gap, and the sparks emitted radio waves
What showed that Hertz's wire loop detector was receiving radio waves? | Sparks across a small gap in the loop
Besides a wire loop, what detector did Hertz use for radio waves? | Two parallel metal rods at the centre of a concave metal sheet, across which an oscillating pd was induced
How did Hertz show that radio waves can be reflected? | A metal screen placed behind the transmitter gave a stronger signal at the detector
How did Hertz show that radio waves pass through insulators? | Placing an insulator between transmitter and detector made no difference to the signal
What did Hertz show by rotating his detector through 90° about the line to the transmitter? | The radio waves were plane polarised (the sparks stopped)
Why were the radio waves from Hertz's transmitter polarised? | Charges in the transmitter were accelerated in one direction only
How did Hertz set up a standing wave to measure the speed of radio waves? | He reflected the waves off a flat metal sheet so the incident and reflected waves interfered
How did Hertz find the wavelength of his standing radio waves? | He moved the detector to locate antinodes (strongest signal). Adjacent antinodes are $\frac{\lambda}{2}$ apart
How did Hertz know the frequency of his radio waves? | From the properties of the transmitter circuit
What did Hertz's measured speed of radio waves show? | It matched the speed Maxwell predicted for electromagnetic waves, confirming Maxwell's theory
What did scientists believe about the speed of light before it was measured? | It was infinite (light travelled instantaneously)
What was Fizeau's apparatus for measuring the speed of light? | A light beam passed through a gap in a rapidly rotating toothed wheel to a mirror several kilometres away, then back through the wheel to an observer
What was the purpose of the toothed wheel in Fizeau's experiment? | To chop the continuous beam into regular pulses of light
At what condition did Fizeau's observer first see no returning light? | Light that passed through a gap returned to find the adjacent tooth in its place
In Fizeau's experiment, what is the round-trip time of the light? | $t = \frac{2d}{c}$, where $d$ is the distance from wheel to mirror
For a toothed wheel with $n$ teeth rotating at frequency $f$, how long does a gap take to be replaced by the adjacent tooth? | $t = \frac{1}{2nf}$
What is Fizeau's equation for the speed of light? | $c = 4dnf$, where $d$ is the wheel-to-mirror distance, $n$ the number of teeth and $f$ the wheel's rotation frequency
What speed of light is found with a mirror 8.6 km away and a 720-tooth wheel that first blocks the returning light at 12.6 revolutions per second? | $c = 4dnf = 4 \times 8600 \times 720 \times 12.6 = 3.1 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
In Fizeau's experiment, at what rotation frequency does the returning light first reappear at maximum brightness? | Twice the frequency at which it first disappeared
In Fizeau's experiment, at what rotation frequency is the returning light next blocked after $f_0$? | $3f_0$, when the light passes a gap and meets the following tooth
What did the match between Fizeau's speed of light and Maxwell's predicted speed suggest? | Light is an electromagnetic wave
What is a black body? | An object that absorbs all the radiation incident on it, reflecting and transmitting none
An object that absorbs all the radiation incident on it, reflecting and transmitting none - what is this? | A black body
Why would a perfect black body also be the best possible emitter? | A good absorber is also a good emitter
What is black-body radiation? | Electromagnetic radiation emitted by a black body, with a spectrum whose peak depends only on its temperature
How does the wavelength of peak intensity of a black-body spectrum change as temperature increases? | It decreases
In which region of the spectrum does an object at room temperature emit most intensely? | Infrared
What was the ultraviolet catastrophe? | Classical wave theory predicted black-body intensity rising without limit as wavelength decreased into the ultraviolet, contradicting experiment
How did experimental black-body curves differ from the classical prediction? | Experiment showed a peak, with intensity falling to zero at short wavelengths. Theory agreed only at long wavelengths
What did Planck propose to resolve the ultraviolet catastrophe? | Electromagnetic radiation is emitted in quanta, each with energy proportional to its frequency
What is Planck's equation for the energy of an oscillator of frequency $f$? | $E = nhf$, where $n$ is an integer
What did classical wave theory predict for photoelectric emission using high-intensity radiation below the threshold frequency? | Electrons would eventually be emitted, since waves transfer energy continuously
What did classical wave theory predict about the delay before photoelectric emission with weak radiation? | A delay while electrons absorbed enough energy, as the wave's energy is spread over the surface
What did classical wave theory predict about how intensity affects photoelectron kinetic energy? | Greater intensity would give each electron more kinetic energy
What is observed when low-intensity radiation above the threshold frequency falls on a metal? | Photoelectrons are emitted immediately
What did Einstein propose electromagnetic radiation consists of? | Discrete packets of energy (photons), each of energy $E = hf$
Whose work did Einstein's explanation of photoelectricity build on? | Planck's quantisation of energy in black-body radiation
What is the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated from rest through a pd $V$? | $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$
What accelerating pd gives an electron a de Broglie wavelength of $1.5 \times 10^{-10}$ m, about the size of an atom? | $V = \frac{h^2}{2me\lambda^2} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34})^2}{2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times (1.5 \times 10^{-10})^2} = 67\ \text{V}$
By what factor does an electron's de Broglie wavelength change when its accelerating pd is multiplied by 25? | It falls to $\frac{1}{5}$ of its original value
How does electron diffraction support de Broglie's hypothesis as the anode pd is raised? | A higher pd gives the electrons more momentum and the rings shrink, showing a shorter wavelength, as $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$ predicts
How is the electron diffraction pattern made visible? | A fluorescent (phosphor) screen glows where electrons strike
What is the resolving power of a microscope? | Its ability to distinguish between two nearby points on an object
Its ability to distinguish between two nearby points on an object - what property of a microscope is this? | Resolving power
How does the wavelength used affect a microscope's resolving power? | Shorter wavelength gives higher resolving power (finer detail)
Why can electron microscopes resolve more detail than light microscopes? | Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than visible light
What focuses the electron beam in a transmission electron microscope (TEM)? | Magnetic lenses
What is the function of the condenser lens in a TEM? | It deflects the electrons into a wide parallel beam that is uniformly incident on the sample
What is the function of the objective lens in a TEM? | It forms an image of the sample
What is the function of the projector lens in a TEM? | It casts a magnified second image onto the fluorescent screen
Why does passing through the sample reduce a TEM's resolution? | The electrons slow down, so their wavelength increases
Why do electrons in a TEM beam have a range of speeds? | They leave the gun at different speeds and are slowed by different amounts in the sample
Why does a range of electron speeds blur a TEM image? | Magnetic lenses deflect electrons of different speeds by different amounts, so electrons from one point on the sample land at a range of positions
What is the probe of a scanning tunnelling microscope (STM)? | A very fine tip, only a few atoms across
What moves the tip of an STM? | Piezoelectric transducers, in tiny increments in any direction
How is an STM tip arranged relative to the sample surface? | Held very close to the surface at a constant pd
What is the tunnelling current in an STM? | The current produced by electrons tunnelling across the gap between the sample surface and the tip
What happens to the tunnelling current when the gap between STM tip and surface decreases? | It increases
In constant current mode, how does an STM keep the tunnelling current constant? | The tip moves up or down to keep the gap the same
How do constant height and constant current STM modes differ in what they record to map the surface? | Constant height: changes in tunnelling current. Constant current: the vertical movement of the tip
Explain how electrons can tunnel across the gap in an STM | They lack the energy to cross the gap, a potential barrier, but their wave nature gives a small probability of being found on the other side
What condition must a barrier meet for quantum tunnelling to occur? | It must be very narrow (a small enough gap)
Why does an STM have very high resolving power? | The tunnelling current is very sensitive to changes in gap width
What can STM images show? | Individual atoms on a surface
