What was the luminiferous aether? | A hypothetical medium thought to fill space and carry light waves
Why did physicists assume light needed a medium to travel through? | All other known waves, such as sound and water waves, travel through a medium
What was the aim of the Michelson-Morley experiment? | To detect the Earth's absolute motion through the aether
What is meant by absolute motion? | Motion measured relative to a single fixed frame (the aether) that applies to every body in the Universe
What is the aether wind? | The apparent flow of aether past the Earth caused by the Earth's motion through it
According to aether theory, along which interferometer arm should a beam take longer to travel there and back? | The arm parallel to the Earth's motion through the aether
What were the main parts of the Michelson-Morley interferometer? | Two equal-length arms at right angles, each with a mirror at its end, and a beam splitter where they meet
What is a beam splitter? | A semi-silvered mirror that transmits part of the light and reflects the rest
Why do the two beams in the Michelson-Morley interferometer form an interference pattern? | They come from the same source, so they are coherent
What was the purpose of the extra glass plate in one arm of the Michelson-Morley interferometer? | To make both beams pass through the same thickness of glass and air
Why was the Michelson-Morley interferometer floated on mercury? | So it could be rotated smoothly with minimal friction
Why did Michelson and Morley expect the fringes to shift when the interferometer was rotated by 90°? | Rotation changes which arm is parallel to the aether wind, so the time difference between the beams would change
How large a fringe shift did aether theory predict, and what could the apparatus detect? | About 0.4 of a fringe was predicted, and shifts of 0.05 of a fringe could be detected
Why would fringes be seen whatever the interferometer's orientation? | The two beams always interfere. Aether theory predicted only that the pattern would shift as the apparatus was rotated
What result did the Michelson-Morley experiment give? | A null result: no significant fringe shift at any orientation or time of day
How was the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment interpreted? | Absolute motion cannot be detected, refuting the idea of a fixed aether frame
What did the Michelson-Morley null result show about the speed of light? | It is invariant: unaffected by the Earth's motion
What speed does a stationary observer measure for light emitted by a moving source? | $c$, regardless of the source's speed
What is a frame of reference? | A set of coordinates used to record the position and time of events
What is an inertial frame of reference? | A frame that is not accelerating, in which Newton's first law holds
A frame that is not accelerating, in which Newton's first law holds - what term is this? | Inertial frame of reference
How do any two inertial frames move relative to each other? | At constant velocity
Why can special relativity not be applied to a spacecraft whose path is bent by the Sun's gravity? | Special relativity applies only in inertial frames, and the spacecraft is accelerating
What does special relativity say about an absolutely stationary frame of reference? | None exists: all motion is relative
What is Galilean relativity? | Newton's laws are the same in all inertial frames, with space and time treated as absolute
Why does Galilean velocity addition fail near the speed of light? | It can give speeds above $c$, e.g. $0.7c + 0.5c = 1.2c$, which is impossible
State Einstein's first postulate of special relativity | The laws of physics have the same form in all inertial frames of reference
State Einstein's second postulate of special relativity | The speed of light in free space is the same in all inertial frames of reference
Two spaceships approach each other at $0.5c$ and $0.3c$. What speed does one measure for a radio signal from the other? | $c$, because the speed of light in free space is the same for every observer
How is the second postulate consistent with $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}}$? | The equation contains only constants that do not depend on the frame of reference, so $c$ in free space is the same in every frame
How do the results of an experiment on a train moving at constant velocity compare with the same experiment on the platform? | They are identical, so uniform motion cannot be detected
What is time dilation? | A clock moving relative to an observer is measured to run slow, so the time interval it records is shorter than the observer measures
What is proper time? | The time interval between two events measured by an observer at rest relative to them, so both events occur at the same position
The time interval between two events measured by an observer at rest relative to them - what term is this? | Proper time
What is the time dilation equation? | $t = \frac{t_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$, where $t_0$ is the proper time
What is the gamma factor? | $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$
What range of values can the gamma factor take? | $\gamma \geq 1$: equal to 1 when $v = 0$, tending to infinity as $v \to c$
Two observers move at high speed relative to each other. How does each measure the other's clock? | Each measures the other's clock running slow
Explain why a light clock moving relative to an observer is measured to tick slower | The observer sees the light follow a longer diagonal path at the same speed $c$ (second postulate), so each tick takes longer
A clock moving at $0.6c$ records $8.0\ \text{s}$. What time interval does a stationary observer measure? | $t = \frac{8.0}{\sqrt{1 - 0.6^2}} = \frac{8.0}{0.8} = 10\ \text{s}$
What is length contraction? | An object moving relative to an observer is measured to be shorter than its proper length
What is proper length? | The length of an object measured by an observer at rest relative to it
The length of an object measured by an observer at rest relative to it - what term is this? | Proper length
What is the length contraction equation? | $l = l_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}$, where $l_0$ is the proper length
Along which direction does length contraction occur? | Only along the direction of relative motion
How must an observer measure the length of a moving object? | Record the positions of both ends at the same time in their own frame
A rod of proper length $5.0\ \text{m}$ moves at $0.8c$. What length does a stationary observer measure? | $l = 5.0\sqrt{1 - 0.8^2} = 5.0 \times 0.6 = 3.0\ \text{m}$
Where are muons produced? | In the upper atmosphere, from the decay of pions produced by cosmic rays
Muons with a half-life of $1.5\ \mu\text{s}$ travel 10 km at $0.98c$. Using Newtonian physics, about how many half-lives pass? | $t = \frac{1.0 \times 10^4}{0.98 \times 3.0 \times 10^8} = 3.4 \times 10^{-5}$ s, about 23 half-lives, so very few muons would reach the ground
Explain, in the Earth's frame, why many more muons reach the ground than Newtonian physics predicts | The muons' half-life is dilated by $\gamma$, so far fewer half-lives pass during the journey
Explain, in the muons' frame, why many more muons reach the ground than Newtonian physics predicts | The distance to the ground is length-contracted by $\gamma$, so the journey takes far fewer half-lives
In the muon experiment, in which frame is the proper time of the muon half-life measured? | The muon's rest frame
In the muon experiment, in which frame is the proper length of the journey measured? | The Earth's frame
What measurements give evidence of time dilation from muons? | Muon count rates at high altitude and at ground level. Far more muons reach the ground than expected without time dilation
What is rest mass? | The mass of an object measured by an observer at rest relative to it, $m_0$
What is the equation for relativistic mass? | $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$
Describe how the relativistic mass of an object varies with its speed | Close to $m_0$ at low speeds, then rising steeply towards infinity as $v \to c$
What is the equation for the total energy of an object moving at speed $v$? | $E = mc^2 = \frac{m_0c^2}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$
What is the equation for the rest energy of an object? | $E_0 = m_0c^2$
What is the equation for relativistic kinetic energy? | $E_k = mc^2 - m_0c^2$
Why is $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ with the relativistic mass wrong for relativistic kinetic energy? | Kinetic energy is total energy minus rest energy, $E_k = mc^2 - m_0c^2$, which does not equal $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$
How does relativistic kinetic energy vary with speed compared with Newtonian kinetic energy? | Almost equal at low speeds, but at high speeds relativistic $E_k$ is greater and rises towards infinity as $v \to c$
Why can no object with mass be accelerated to the speed of light? | It would need an infinite amount of energy
When must relativistic rather than Newtonian kinetic energy be used? | When the speed is a significant fraction of $c$
Electrons pass through several equal accelerating pds. How do the gains in kinetic energy and speed compare between the first and last stages? | Each stage adds the same kinetic energy, $eV$, but the speed gain is far smaller in the last stage as the speed approaches $c$
A particle with rest energy $938\ \text{MeV}$ moves at $0.6c$. What is its total energy? | $E = \frac{938}{\sqrt{1 - 0.6^2}} = \frac{938}{0.8} \approx 1170\ \text{MeV}$
An electron with rest energy $0.511\ \text{MeV}$ has $\gamma = 3.0$. What is its kinetic energy? | $E_k = (\gamma - 1)m_0c^2 = 2.0 \times 0.511 = 1.02\ \text{MeV}$
What did Bertozzi's experiment provide direct evidence for? | The relativistic variation of kinetic energy with speed
How did Bertozzi give electrons different kinetic energies? | By accelerating them from rest through different potential differences
How did Bertozzi measure the speed of the electrons? | Timed their flight between two signal points a known distance apart using an oscilloscope, then speed = distance / time
Why did the electrons in Bertozzi's experiment travel at constant speed after leaving the electric field? | No forces acted on them in the evacuated tube
What graph did Bertozzi plot? | Speed squared ($v^2$) against kinetic energy
How do the Newtonian and relativistic predictions differ for Bertozzi's graph of $v^2$ against $E_k$? | Newtonian: straight line through the origin, with $v^2$ exceeding $c^2$. Relativistic: $v^2$ levels off, approaching $c^2$
Which prediction did Bertozzi's results support? | The relativistic one: electron speeds approached but never exceeded $c$
How did Bertozzi independently check the electrons' kinetic energy? | Measured the energy gained by the aluminium target using $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$, then divided by the number of electrons
In Bertozzi's experiment, how was the number of electrons hitting the target found? | Total charge collected divided by the electron charge, $n = \frac{Q}{e}$
A target collects $3.2\ \mu\text{C}$ of charge from electrons. How many electrons hit it? | $n = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-6}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 2.0 \times 10^{13}$
