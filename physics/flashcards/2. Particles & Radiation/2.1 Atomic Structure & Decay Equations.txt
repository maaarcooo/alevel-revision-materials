What three types of particle make up an atom? | Protons, neutrons, and electrons
Where are protons and neutrons located within an atom? | In the nucleus, with electrons orbiting around it
What is the elementary charge, $e$? | $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C
What is the mass of a proton (or neutron) in SI units? | Approximately $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
What is the mass of an electron in SI units? | $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg
What are the relative charge and relative mass of a proton? | Relative charge: $+1$, relative mass: $1$
What are the relative charge and relative mass of a neutron? | Relative charge: $0$, relative mass: $1$
What are the relative charge and relative mass of an electron? | Relative charge: $-1$, relative mass: $\frac{1}{2000}$ (negligible)
Why is a stable atom electrically neutral? | It has equal numbers of protons and electrons, whose equal and opposite charges cancel
Define specific charge | The ratio of the total charge of a particle or ion to its mass, given by $\frac{Q}{m}$
The ratio of the total charge of a particle to its mass — what quantity is this? | Specific charge
What is the SI unit of specific charge? | C kg$^{-1}$
What is the specific charge of the electron? | $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C kg$^{-1}$
What is the specific charge of the proton? | $9.58 \times 10^{7}$ C kg$^{-1}$
How is the total charge of an ion determined for a specific charge calculation? | Charge = number of electrons gained or lost $\times$ $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C (negative if gained, positive if lost)
How is the total charge of a nucleus determined for a specific charge calculation? | Charge = number of protons $\times$ $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C
How is the mass determined for a specific charge calculation of an ion or nucleus? | Mass = nucleon number (A) $\times$ $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
How does calculating specific charge differ for an ion versus a nucleus? | For an ion, charge comes from the excess or deficit of electrons. For a nucleus, charge comes from the protons only
In $^A_Z X$ notation, what does A represent? | The nucleon number (mass number): total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus
In $^A_Z X$ notation, what does Z represent? | The proton number (atomic number): total number of protons in the nucleus
Define isotope | Nuclei that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons
Nuclei that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons — what term describes them? | Isotopes
For isotopes of the same element, which number is fixed and which changes? | The proton number (Z) is fixed; the nucleon number (A) is different
Why are some isotopes unstable? | They have an imbalance of neutrons and protons, causing them to decay and emit radiation to reach a more stable form
Define isotopic data | The relative amounts of different isotopes of an element present within a substance
How is the relative atomic mass of an element calculated from isotopic data? | By summing the products of each isotope's mass number and its fractional (or percentage) abundance
What instrument is used to determine the percentage abundance of different isotopes in a sample? | A mass spectrometer
How is carbon dating used to determine the age of archaeological artefacts? | By comparing the ratio of stable carbon-12 to unstable carbon-14 in a dead sample with the ratio found in living tissue
Why would electrostatic and gravitational forces alone be insufficient to hold the nucleus together? | Electrostatic repulsion between protons would push the nucleus apart, and gravitational attraction between nucleons is far too weak (negligible) to overcome it
What is the strong nuclear force? | An attractive force that acts between all nucleons, holding the nucleus together by overcoming electrostatic repulsion
The strong nuclear force acts between particles called [...] | quarks (which make up protons and neutrons)
At what nucleon separation is the strong nuclear force repulsive? | Below approximately 0.5 fm
Over what range of separations is the strong nuclear force attractive? | From approximately 0.5 fm to 3.0 fm
At what nucleon separation does the strong nuclear force reach its maximum attractive value? | Approximately 1.0 fm, which is a typical nucleon separation
At what separation is the equilibrium position of the strong nuclear force between two nucleons (resultant force = 0)? | Approximately 0.5 fm
How does the strength of the strong nuclear force differ between proton-proton, neutron-neutron, and proton-neutron pairs? | It is roughly the same between all nucleon pairs, because the strong force is not influenced by charge
How does the range of the strong nuclear force compare to the electrostatic force? | The strong force has a much smaller range (0.5 to 3.0 fm) than the electrostatic force, which becomes significant beyond about 2.5 fm separation
At what separation is the equilibrium position for two protons, where the strong attractive and electrostatic repulsive forces balance? | Approximately 0.7 fm
What is 1 femtometre (fm) in metres? | $1 \times 10^{-15}$ m
What is an alpha particle? | Two protons and two neutrons, equivalent to a helium-4 nucleus ($^4_2\text{He}$)
What type of nucleus commonly undergoes alpha decay? | Large, unstable nuclei with too many nucleons
In alpha decay, how do the proton number and nucleon number change? | Proton number decreases by 2; nucleon number decreases by 4
Write the general equation for alpha decay | $^A_Z X \rightarrow \;^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y + \;^4_2 \alpha$
In radioactive decay, what are the parent nucleus and daughter nucleus? | The parent is the original unstable nucleus; the daughter is the new nucleus formed after decay
What is a beta-minus ($\beta^-$) particle? | A high-energy electron emitted from the nucleus during radioactive decay
What happens at the nucleon level during beta-minus decay? | A neutron turns into a proton, emitting an electron ($\beta^-$) and an anti-electron neutrino ($\bar{\nu}_e$)
In beta-minus decay, how do the proton number and nucleon number change? | Proton number increases by 1; nucleon number remains the same
Write the general equation for beta-minus decay | $^A_Z X \rightarrow \;^A_{Z+1} Y + \;^{\;0}_{-1} \beta + \;^0_0 \bar{\nu}_e$
What is a beta-plus ($\beta^+$) particle? | A high-energy positron emitted from the nucleus during radioactive decay
What happens at the nucleon level during beta-plus decay? | A proton turns into a neutron, emitting a positron ($\beta^+$) and an electron neutrino ($\nu_e$)
In beta-plus decay, how do the proton number and nucleon number change? | Proton number decreases by 1; nucleon number remains the same
Write the general equation for beta-plus decay | $^A_Z X \rightarrow \;^A_{Z-1} Y + \;^0_{+1} \beta + \;^0_0 \nu_e$
How does beta-minus decay differ from beta-plus decay at the nucleon level? | In $\beta^-$ decay a neutron becomes a proton (emitting an electron and $\bar{\nu}_e$). In $\beta^+$ decay a proton becomes a neutron (emitting a positron and $\nu_e$)
Which type of neutrino is produced in beta-minus decay? | An anti-electron neutrino ($\bar{\nu}_e$)
Which type of neutrino is produced in beta-plus decay? | An electron neutrino ($\nu_e$)
Why was the neutrino hypothesised to exist? | To account for the conservation of energy in beta decay, since beta particles were observed with a continuous range of energies rather than a single fixed value
What type of energy spectrum do alpha particles have? | Discrete energies (specific fixed values, shown as spikes on a number-versus-energy graph)
Why do beta particles have a continuous range of energies? | The energy released in beta decay is shared between the beta particle and the neutrino (or anti-neutrino), in varying proportions
How do the energy spectra of alpha and beta particles differ? | Alpha particles have discrete energies (fixed values). Beta particles have a continuous range of energies because the decay energy is shared with a neutrino
What is an antiparticle? | The antimatter counterpart of a particle, with the same mass and rest mass-energy but opposite charge
A particle's counterpart with the same mass and rest mass-energy but opposite charge — what is this called? | An antiparticle
What is the antiparticle of the electron? | The positron ($e^+$)
How are antiparticles named and symbolised (apart from the positron)? | Named with the prefix 'anti-' and symbolised with a bar above the particle symbol
What is rest mass-energy? | The energy equivalent to the mass of a particle when it is at rest
What is the rest mass-energy of a proton (or antiproton)? | 938.257 MeV
What is the rest mass-energy of a neutron (or antineutron)? | 939.551 MeV
What is the rest mass-energy of an electron (or positron)? | 0.511 MeV
What is the rest mass-energy of a neutrino (or antineutrino)? | Zero
Define a photon | A massless packet (quantum) of electromagnetic energy
A massless packet (quantum) of electromagnetic energy — what is this called? | A photon
What is the equation for the energy of a photon in terms of frequency? | $E = hf$
What is the equation for the energy of a photon in terms of wavelength? | $E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$
How is photon energy related to wavelength? | Inversely proportional — a longer wavelength means a lower photon energy
Define annihilation | The destruction of a particle-antiparticle pair when they collide, converting their mass into two gamma-ray photons emitted in opposite directions
The destruction of a particle-antiparticle pair when they collide, converting their mass into two gamma-ray photons — what process is this? | Annihilation
Why are two photons produced in annihilation, emitted in opposite directions? | To conserve momentum
What is the minimum energy of each photon produced in annihilation? | The rest mass-energy of one of the annihilating particles: $E_{min} = hf_{min} = E_0$
Define pair production | The creation of a particle-antiparticle pair when a high-energy photon spontaneously converts its energy into mass near a nucleus
The creation of a particle-antiparticle pair from a high-energy photon near a nucleus — what process is this? | Pair production
What is the minimum photon energy required for pair production? | Twice the rest mass-energy of one of the particles produced: $E_{min} = hf_{min} = 2E_0$
Why do the particle and antiparticle produced in pair production move in opposite directions? | To conserve momentum
How does the minimum energy condition differ between annihilation and pair production? | In annihilation, each of the two photons produced has minimum energy equal to one particle's rest mass-energy ($E_0$). In pair production, the single incoming photon must have minimum energy equal to $2E_0$
