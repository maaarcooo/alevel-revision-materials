What is a stationary (standing) wave? | A wave formed by the superposition of two waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions, producing a pattern where the peaks and troughs do not move
A wave formed by superposition of two waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions, where the peaks and troughs do not move — what type of wave is this? | Stationary (standing) wave
How is a stationary wave usually produced? | By a travelling wave superposing with its own reflection
How do stationary waves and progressive waves differ in terms of energy? | Stationary waves store energy; progressive waves transfer energy
How does the amplitude of individual points differ between a progressive wave and a stationary wave? | Progressive wave: all points have the same amplitude. Stationary wave: each point has a different amplitude depending on its position
What is a node on a stationary wave? | A point of no vibration (zero displacement at all times)
A point of no vibration on a stationary wave — what is this called? | A node
What is an antinode on a stationary wave? | A point of maximum amplitude on a stationary wave
A point of maximum amplitude on a stationary wave — what is this called? | An antinode
How do nodes and antinodes behave in terms of position on a stationary wave? | Nodes are fixed in position. Antinodes oscillate vertically but do not move along the string
How can you determine whether two points on a stationary wave are in phase or out of phase? | Count the nodes between them: an even number (including zero) means in phase, an odd number means out of phase
What distance separates adjacent nodes (or adjacent antinodes) on a stationary wave? | Half a wavelength ($\frac{\lambda}{2}$)
State the principle of superposition | When two or more waves with the same frequency arrive at a point, the resultant displacement is the sum of the displacements of each wave
The resultant displacement at a point equals the sum of the displacements of the individual waves arriving there — what principle is this? | The principle of superposition
To what types of waves does the principle of superposition apply? | All types: transverse and longitudinal, progressive and stationary
What happens when two waves of the same frequency and amplitude superpose in phase? | Constructive interference occurs and the resultant wave has double the amplitude
What happens when two waves of the same frequency and amplitude superpose in anti-phase? | Destructive interference occurs and the resultant wave has zero amplitude
What conditions are required for a stationary wave to form? | Two waves with the same frequency, same wavelength, and similar amplitude must travel in opposite directions along the same line
What type of interference produces nodes on a stationary wave? | Destructive interference (the waves are in anti-phase at those points)
What type of interference produces antinodes on a stationary wave? | Constructive interference (the waves are in phase at those points)
What do stationary waves on a vibrating stretched string produce? | Sound (this is how stringed instruments such as guitars and violins work)
How can stationary waves be demonstrated with microwaves? | Place a microwave source in line with a metal reflector and move a probe detector between them to locate nodes (signal minima) and antinodes (signal maxima)
How can stationary sound waves be demonstrated in a tube? | Place fine powder inside a tube with a loudspeaker at the open end. At resonant frequencies the powder collects in evenly spaced heaps at the nodes
For a stationary sound wave in a tube closed at one end, where are the node and antinode? | Node at the closed end, antinode at the open end (loudspeaker end)
What is a resonant frequency in the context of a vibrating string? | A frequency at which a whole number of half wavelengths fits exactly on the length of the string, forming a stationary wave
What are harmonics? | Different stationary wave patterns that can form at specific resonant frequencies, depending on the frequency of vibration and the boundary conditions
What is the first harmonic on a string fixed at both ends? | The simplest stationary wave pattern: a single loop with two nodes (at the ends) and one antinode
What is another name for the first harmonic? | The fundamental frequency
What is the wavelength of the first harmonic on a string of length $L$? | $\lambda = 2L$
What is the frequency of the first harmonic on a string of length $L$ with wave speed $v$? | $f_1 = \frac{v}{2L}$
How many nodes and antinodes does the second harmonic have on a string? | Three nodes and two antinodes
What is the wavelength of the second harmonic on a string of length $L$? | $\lambda = L$
What is the frequency of the second harmonic in terms of $f_1$? | $f_2 = 2f_1$
How many nodes and antinodes does the third harmonic have on a string? | Four nodes and three antinodes
What is the wavelength of the third harmonic on a string of length $L$? | $\lambda = \frac{2L}{3}$
What is the frequency of the third harmonic in terms of $f_1$? | $f_3 = 3f_1$
For the $n$th harmonic on a string, how many antinodes and nodes are there? | $n$ antinodes and $n + 1$ nodes
What is the frequency of the $n$th harmonic in terms of $f_1$? | $f_n = n \times f_1$
What is the equation for the speed of a wave travelling along a string under tension? | $v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$, where $T$ is tension (N) and $\mu$ is mass per unit length (kg m$^{-1}$)
What is mass per unit length ($\mu$) and what is its SI unit? | The mass of the string divided by its length, measured in kg m$^{-1}$
What is the equation for the frequency of the first harmonic in terms of $L$, $T$, and $\mu$? | $f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$
What is the aim of the required practical on stationary waves? | To investigate how the frequency of the first harmonic depends on the length, tension, or mass per unit length of a string
In the stationary waves required practical (varying length), what are the independent, dependent, and control variables? | IV: length of string. DV: frequency of first harmonic. CVs: tension (same hanging mass) and same string (constant mass per unit length)
Describe the setup for the stationary waves required practical | A string is attached to a vibration generator at one end, passes over a pulley at the other end, and has masses hung from it to provide tension. A moveable bridge sets the vibrating length $L$
In the stationary waves practical, how is the first harmonic identified? | The frequency of the vibration generator is gradually increased until a single stationary wave loop (one antinode, two nodes at the ends) is observed
When plotting $f$ against $\frac{1}{L}$ for the stationary waves practical, what does the gradient represent? | The gradient equals $\frac{v}{2}$, so the wave speed is $v = 2 \times \text{gradient}$
How can the experimental wave speed be verified in the stationary waves practical? | By comparing it with $v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$ calculated from the measured tension and mass per unit length
What is the main source of random error in the stationary waves practical? | The sharpness of resonance makes it difficult to judge exactly when the first harmonic frequency is reached
How can the random error in identifying the first harmonic be minimised? | Adjust the frequency while watching a node to find the largest response, then overshoot the frequency and reduce it back. Average the readings from multiple approaches
Name two ways to reduce systematic error in the stationary waves practical | Use an oscilloscope to verify the signal generator frequency readings, and let the signal generator stabilise for about 20 minutes before taking measurements
State two safety precautions for the stationary waves practical | Use a rubber string instead of metal wire in case it snaps under tension (wear goggles if metal wire is used). Place a crash mat under the hanging masses and stand well away from them
