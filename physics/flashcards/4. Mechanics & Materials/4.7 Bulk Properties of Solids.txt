What is density? | The mass per unit volume of an object
The mass per unit volume of an object -- what quantity is this? | Density
What is the equation for density? | $\rho = \frac{m}{V}$
What are the SI units of density? | $\text{kg m}^{-3}$
How do you convert $\text{mm}^3$ to $\text{m}^3$? | Divide by $(1000)^3 = 10^9$, so $1\;\text{mm}^3 = 1 \times 10^{-9}\;\text{m}^3$
How do you convert $\text{cm}^3$ to $\text{m}^3$? | Divide by $(100)^3 = 10^6$, so $1\;\text{cm}^3 = 1 \times 10^{-6}\;\text{m}^3$
When converting a larger unit to a smaller unit, what operation do you use? | Multiply
When converting a smaller unit to a larger unit, what operation do you use? | Divide
State Hooke's Law | The extension of a material is directly proportional to the applied force, up to the limit of proportionality
What is the equation for Hooke's Law? | $F = k\Delta L$
What does the spring constant $k$ measure? | The stiffness of a material; a larger $k$ means a stiffer material
What are the units of the spring constant? | $\text{N m}^{-1}$
How is extension $\Delta L$ calculated? | Stretched length minus unstretched length
What two types of deformation does Hooke's Law apply to? | Extensions (increase in length) and compressions (decrease in length)
On a force-extension graph, how is the Hooke's Law region shown? | A straight line through the origin
What is the gradient of a force-extension graph equal to in the Hooke's Law region? | The spring constant $k$
What is the gradient of an extension-force graph equal to in the Hooke's Law region? | $\frac{1}{k}$
What is the limit of proportionality? | The point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to the applied force
The point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to the applied force -- what term is this? | Limit of proportionality
What is the elastic limit? | The point beyond which a material will no longer return to its original length when the force is removed
The point beyond which a material will no longer return to its original length when the force is removed -- what term is this? | Elastic limit
How does the limit of proportionality differ from the elastic limit? | Limit of proportionality: extension stops being proportional to force. Elastic limit: material stops returning to its original shape. The limit of proportionality is reached first
Define tensile stress | The force exerted per unit cross-sectional area of a material
The force exerted per unit cross-sectional area of a material -- what quantity is this? | Tensile stress
What is the equation for tensile stress? | $\sigma = \frac{F}{A}$
What are the units of tensile stress? | Pascals (Pa)
What Greek letter represents tensile stress? | $\sigma$ (lower-case sigma)
Define tensile strain | The extension per unit length
The extension per unit length -- what quantity is this? | Tensile strain
What is the equation for tensile strain? | $\text{strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L}$
Why is tensile strain dimensionless? | It is the ratio of two lengths, so the units cancel
How is strain expressed as a percentage? | Multiply the strain value by 100
When calculating strain, do the extension and original length need to be in metres? | No, they can be in any units as long as both use the same units, since strain is a ratio
What is the ultimate tensile stress (UTS)? | The maximum stress that a material can withstand, shown at the highest point on a stress-strain graph
The maximum stress that a material can withstand -- what term is this? | Ultimate tensile stress (UTS)
What is the breaking stress? | The stress at the point where a material fractures
The stress at the point where a material fractures -- what term is this? | Breaking stress
How does the UTS differ from the breaking stress on a stress-strain graph? | UTS is the maximum stress (peak of the curve); breaking stress is the stress at which the material actually fractures (final point), which can be lower than UTS
What is the yield stress? | The stress at which a material begins to deform plastically for no or a small increase in stress
The stress at which a material begins to deform plastically for no or a small increase in stress -- what term is this? | Yield stress
What does the area under the straight-line region of a stress-strain graph represent? | The elastic strain energy stored per unit volume
What does the area under a force-extension graph represent? | The work done on the material (elastic strain energy stored)
When a material obeys Hooke's Law, what shape is the area under the force-extension graph? | A right-angled triangle
What is the equation for elastic strain energy when Hooke's Law is obeyed? | $E = \frac{1}{2}F\Delta L$
Write the elastic strain energy equation in terms of the spring constant | $E = \frac{1}{2}k(\Delta L)^2$
Before a material reaches its elastic limit, where is all the work done on it stored? | As elastic strain energy
How is work done calculated from a force-extension graph when Hooke's Law is not obeyed? | Find the total area under the curve by splitting it into separate geometric shapes and adding the individual areas
What is elastic deformation? | Deformation where the material returns to its original shape when the load is removed
Deformation where the material returns to its original shape when the load is removed -- what type of deformation is this? | Elastic deformation
What is plastic deformation? | Permanent deformation where the material does not return to its original shape or length when the load is removed
Permanent deformation where the material does not return to its original shape when the load is removed -- what type of deformation is this? | Plastic deformation
How does elastic deformation differ from plastic deformation? | Elastic: material returns to original shape when force is removed. Plastic: material is permanently deformed and does not return to its original shape
On a force-extension graph, what divides the elastic and plastic regions? | The elastic limit
What characterises a brittle material on a stress-strain graph? | A straight line through the origin with no or negligible curved region before fracture
Give two examples of brittle materials | Glass and concrete
What characterises a ductile material on a stress-strain graph? | A straight line through the origin followed by a significant curved region (large plastic region) before fracture
Give two examples of ductile materials | Copper and rubber
How does a brittle material differ from a ductile material? | Brittle: fractures with little or no plastic deformation. Ductile: undergoes significant plastic deformation (stretches into a new shape) before fracturing
When a metal wire is unloaded after being stretched beyond its elastic limit, how does the unloading line compare to the loading line? | It is parallel (same gradient, since $k$ has not changed) but does not pass through the origin, due to permanent extension
Why is the unloading line parallel to the loading line for a plastically deformed metal wire? | The intermolecular forces between bonds are identical, so the spring constant and gradient remain the same
What does the area between the loading and unloading lines on a force-extension graph represent? | The work done to permanently deform the material
On a rubber band's force-extension graph, where does the unloading curve lie relative to the loading curve? | Below the loading curve
After stretching and releasing a rubber band, what is its permanent extension? | Zero -- the rubber band deforms elastically and returns to its original length
What does the area between the loading and unloading curves of a rubber band represent? | The energy converted to thermal energy (heat) in the rubber band
On a rubber band's force-extension graph, what does the total area under the loading curve represent? | The total work done in stretching the rubber band
Why does a rubber band become warm when repeatedly stretched and released? | Some strain energy is converted to thermal energy during each loading-unloading cycle, as shown by the area between the curves
What three forms of energy interconvert in a vertical spring-mass system? | Elastic potential energy (EPE), kinetic energy (KE), and gravitational potential energy (GPE)
In a vertical spring-mass oscillation, at which point is kinetic energy at a maximum? | At the equilibrium position, where the mass moves at maximum speed
In a vertical spring-mass oscillation, at which point is elastic potential energy at a maximum? | At the lowest point, where the spring has maximum extension
In a vertical spring-mass oscillation, at which point is GPE at a maximum? | At the highest point
For a horizontal spring-mass system, which energy forms interconvert? | Only kinetic energy and elastic potential energy (no change in GPE)
What is the role of shock absorbers in a vehicle suspension system? | To dampen the compression and rebound of the springs by converting kinetic energy into thermal energy
How does the resistance of a shock absorber vary with the speed of spring movement? | The faster the springs move, the greater the resistance the shock absorber provides
