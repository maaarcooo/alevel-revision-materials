What is damping? | The loss of energy from an oscillating system through resistive forces, which reduces the amplitude of its oscillations
The loss of energy from an oscillating system through resistive forces, which reduces the amplitude of its oscillations - what term is this? | Damping
In which direction does a damping force act on an oscillator? | Opposite to its velocity
How does a resistive force on an oscillator differ from the restoring force? | Resistive force: opposes the velocity and removes energy. Restoring force: acts towards the equilibrium position and keeps the system oscillating
What is light damping? | Damping in which the system oscillates many times while its amplitude gradually decreases
How does the amplitude of a lightly damped oscillator vary with time? | It decreases exponentially
How does light damping affect the period of an oscillator as its amplitude falls? | The period stays almost constant
What is critical damping? | Damping that returns a displaced system to its equilibrium position in the shortest possible time without oscillating
Damping that returns a displaced system to its equilibrium position in the shortest possible time without oscillating - what term is this? | Critical damping
What is heavy damping? | Damping greater than critical damping, so the system returns to equilibrium slowly without oscillating
Give an example of a critically damped system | A car's suspension, which settles quickly after a bump without bouncing
Give an example of a heavily damped system | A door fitted with a closer, which shuts slowly without slamming
What is a free vibration? | An oscillation with no external periodic force acting and no energy supplied after it is set going
An oscillation with no external periodic force acting and no energy supplied after it is set going - what term is this? | Free vibration
What is the natural frequency of a system? | The frequency at which it oscillates when it vibrates freely
The frequency at which a system oscillates when it vibrates freely - what term is this? | Natural frequency
What is a forced vibration? | An oscillation produced by an external periodic driving force acting on the system
An oscillation produced by an external periodic driving force acting on the system - what term is this? | Forced vibration
At what frequency does a system in forced vibration oscillate? | The frequency of the driving force (the driving frequency)
Why must a periodic force be applied to keep a damped system oscillating at constant amplitude? | It does work on the system that replaces the energy removed by damping
What is resonance? | The large increase in the amplitude of a forced vibration when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system
The large increase in the amplitude of a forced vibration when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system - what term is this? | Resonance
Explain why the amplitude of a driven system is greatest at resonance | Energy is transferred from the driver to the system at the greatest rate when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency
What does a resonance curve show? | The amplitude of a driven system against the driving frequency, with a peak at the natural frequency
How does increasing the damping affect the maximum amplitude of a driven system at resonance? | The maximum amplitude decreases
How does increasing the damping affect the sharpness of a resonance peak? | The peak becomes broader and less sharp
How does heavier damping affect the frequency at which a driven system has its maximum amplitude? | It falls slightly below the natural frequency
What is the phase difference between the displacement of a driven system and its driver at resonance? | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ rad, with the system lagging the driver
How does the phase of a driven system compare with its driver when the driving frequency is well below the natural frequency? | They are almost in phase
How does the phase of a driven system compare with its driver when the driving frequency is well above the natural frequency? | They are almost in antiphase, a phase difference of $\pi$ rad
What do Barton's pendulums demonstrate? | Resonance: of several pendulums driven by one heavy pendulum, the one with the same length as the driver swings with the largest amplitude
In Barton's pendulums, why does the pendulum of the same length as the driver respond most? | Its natural frequency equals the driving frequency
Give an example of resonance that involves stationary waves | The air column in an organ pipe, or a stretched string, driven at one of its natural frequencies
Give an example of unwanted resonance in a mechanical system | A bridge swaying with a large amplitude when footsteps or the wind drive it near its natural frequency
State two ways of reducing unwanted resonance in a structure | Increase its damping, or change its mass or stiffness so its natural frequency is far from the driving frequency
