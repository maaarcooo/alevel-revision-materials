In Rutherford's scattering experiment, what were alpha particles fired at? | A thin sheet of gold foil
What two things were measured in Rutherford's scattering experiment? | The angles through which alpha particles were deflected and the number deflected at each angle
Why was the alpha source in Rutherford's experiment placed in a lead container with a narrow opening? | To produce a narrow, collimated beam of alpha particles
Why did the gold foil in Rutherford's experiment need to be extremely thin? | So each alpha particle was scattered by at most one nucleus, and was not absorbed
Why was Rutherford's scattering experiment carried out in an evacuated chamber? | So the alpha particles were not absorbed or scattered by air molecules on the way to the foil
What did the observation that most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil suggest? | The atom is mostly empty space
What did the observation that some alpha particles were deflected through small angles suggest? | The positive charge of the atom is concentrated in a central nucleus that repels the positive alpha particles
What did the observation that a very small number of alpha particles were deflected through more than 90° suggest? | The nucleus is extremely small and contains most of the atom's mass
In Rutherford scattering, how does the number of alpha particles detected per unit time vary with deflection angle? | It is greatest at small angles and falls rapidly as the angle increases
Which particle did J.J. Thomson discover in 1897? | The electron
What did Thomson's plum pudding model propose? | A sphere of uniformly spread positive charge with electrons embedded in it, neutral overall
Who carried out the gold foil experiments that tested the plum pudding model? | Geiger and Marsden, working under Rutherford
Which result of the gold foil experiment could the plum pudding model not explain? | A small number of alpha particles were deflected through large angles, some bouncing back
Which model of the atom replaced the plum pudding model after the gold foil experiment? | The Rutherford (nuclear) model
Who discovered the neutron, and when? | James Chadwick, 1932
What are the charge and mass of an alpha particle? | Charge $+2e$, mass $4u$
What is gamma radiation? | High-energy electromagnetic radiation (photons) emitted from a nucleus
Why do nuclei emit gamma radiation? | To lose excess energy
What is the nuclear notation for gamma radiation? | ${}^{0}_{0}\gamma$
How is the ionising ability of a radiation quantified? | By the number of ion pairs it produces per cm of air
Why is ionising radiation harmful to living cells? | Ionisation causes chemical changes that damage cells
Which type of radiation is the most ionising? | Alpha
Why is alpha radiation the most ionising? | It has the largest charge, $+2e$, and moves relatively slowly, so it interacts strongly with the atoms it passes
Roughly how many ion pairs per cm of air does alpha radiation produce? | About $10^4$
Roughly how many ion pairs per cm of air does beta radiation produce? | About 100
Roughly how many ion pairs per cm of air does gamma radiation produce? | About 1
Which type of radiation is the least ionising? | Gamma
Why is gamma radiation the least ionising? | Gamma photons have no charge
Which type of radiation is the most penetrating? | Gamma
Which type of radiation is the least penetrating? | Alpha
How are ionising ability and penetrating power related across alpha, beta and gamma? | Inversely: the more ionising the radiation, the less penetrating it is
What is the range of alpha particles in air? | About 3 to 7 cm
What is the range of beta particles in air? | About 20 cm to 3 m, depending on their energy
How does the intensity of gamma radiation in air vary with distance from a point source? | It has no definite range; intensity falls according to the inverse-square law
What absorbs alpha particles? | A sheet of paper
What absorbs beta particles? | A few millimetres of aluminium
What significantly reduces the intensity of gamma radiation? | Several centimetres of lead or several metres of concrete
Towards which plate are alpha particles deflected when passing between charged plates? | The negative plate
Towards which plate are beta-minus particles deflected when passing between charged plates? | The positive plate
Why is gamma radiation not deflected by electric or magnetic fields? | It is uncharged
Why are beta-minus particles deflected much more than alpha particles in the same magnetic field? | Their far smaller mass gives a much smaller momentum per unit charge, so their path has a much smaller radius
Which radioactive source is used in smoke detectors? | Americium-241, an alpha emitter
Explain how a smoke detector uses alpha radiation | Alpha particles ionise the air between two plates so a small current flows. Smoke absorbs the alpha particles, the current falls and the alarm sounds
Why is an alpha source suitable for a smoke detector inside a home? | Its short range means it poses little hazard to occupants
Why does the americium-241 in a smoke detector need a long half-life? | So its activity does not fall significantly over the detector's lifetime and it need not be replaced
Which type of radiation is used to monitor the thickness of paper or aluminium foil during manufacture? | Beta
Why is alpha radiation unsuitable for monitoring the thickness of aluminium foil? | It would be completely absorbed by the foil
Why is gamma radiation unsuitable for monitoring the thickness of thin foil? | It passes through almost unabsorbed, so small changes in thickness barely change the count rate
In beta thickness monitoring, how is the roller pressure adjusted if the detected count rate falls? | It is increased, because a falling count rate means the material is too thick
Why does a thickness-monitoring source need a long half-life? | So it does not need to be replaced often
Which type of radiation is used to sterilise medical equipment? | Gamma, e.g. from cobalt-60
Why is gamma radiation suitable for sterilising medical equipment? | It is penetrating enough to reach all sides of the instruments without removing their sealed packaging
Why does gamma sterilisation not make the equipment radioactive? | Gamma radiation does not affect the nuclei of the equipment's atoms, and only a change to a nucleus can make it radioactive
How does gamma radiotherapy deliver a high dose to a tumour while limiting damage to surrounding tissue? | Beams are concentrated on the tumour from many directions as the machine rotates around the patient
How are parts of a radiotherapy patient's body that are not being treated protected? | They are covered with lead
Which type of radiation can treat skin cancer by direct application to the affected area? | Beta
What is a radioactive tracer? | A radioisotope put into the body to monitor processes occurring in it
Why are radioactive tracers with short half-lives preferred? | They decay quickly, so the patient's dose is lower
Why is only a small sample of a short-half-life tracer needed? | Its initial activity is very high
Why is a gamma emitter ideal as a medical tracer? | Gamma penetrates out of the body to an external detector and is weakly ionising, so causes minimal damage
Which organ takes up iodine-131? | The thyroid gland
State the inverse-square law for gamma radiation | The intensity of gamma radiation from a point source is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source
What is the equation for the inverse-square law for gamma radiation? | $I = \frac{k}{x^2}$
Why does the intensity of gamma radiation from a point source follow an inverse-square law? | The radiation spreads out uniformly over a sphere whose area is proportional to $x^2$
What is the unit of intensity? | $\text{W m}^{-2}$
By what factor does gamma intensity change when the distance from a point source doubles? | It falls to one quarter
A gamma source gives a corrected count rate of 200 counts s⁻¹ at 0.2 m. What is the corrected count rate at 0.5 m? | $C_2 = C_1\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right)^2 = 200 \times \left(\frac{0.2}{0.5}\right)^2 = 32\ \text{s}^{-1}$
How can count rates at different distances be tested against the inverse-square law? | Check that $Cx^2$ is constant for at least three distances, where C is the corrected count rate
Why does the inverse-square law not apply to alpha and beta radiation in air? | They are absorbed by the air within a short distance
What is background radiation? | The ionising radiation present in the environment at all times, from natural and artificial sources
The ionising radiation present in the environment at all times, from natural and artificial sources - what term is this? | Background radiation
What is the largest source of background radiation in the UK? | Radon gas
Where does radon gas come from? | The decay of uranium found naturally in rocks, soil and building materials such as stone and brick
Which type of radiation does radon emit? | Alpha
Why is radon gas particularly hazardous? | It is inhaled, so its alpha particles are emitted inside the lungs
Where do cosmic rays come from? | The Sun and high-energy events in space such as supernovae
Which radioactive isotope is found in all organic matter? | Carbon-14
What is the largest artificial source of background radiation? | Medical uses, e.g. X-rays and CT scans
Besides medical uses, what artificial sources add to background radiation? | Nuclear waste, and fallout from weapons tests and accidents such as Chernobyl
What is the corrected count rate? | The count rate from a source with the background count rate subtracted
The count rate from a source with the background count rate subtracted - what term is this? | Corrected count rate
How is the background count rate measured? | By recording counts with a GM tube and counter with no source present
A GM tube records 24 counts per minute with no source and 285 counts per minute with a source. What is the corrected count rate? | $285 - 24 = 261$ counts per minute
Why are repeat readings and averages essential when measuring count rates? | Radioactive decay is random
How does the percentage uncertainty in a count N depend on N? | It is proportional to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$
Why should counts be recorded over as long a time as possible? | A larger count has a smaller percentage uncertainty
Why are radioactive sources stored in lead-lined boxes when not in use? | The lead absorbs the radiation, reducing exposure
Why are radioactive sources handled with long tongs? | It increases the distance from the source, reducing the intensity received
Why should exposure time to radioactive sources be kept short? | Less time exposed means a smaller dose received
Which safe-handling rule applies to the open end of a sealed source? | Never point it at anyone, including yourself
How is the radiation exposure of workers such as radiographers monitored? | With detector badges
What is the aim of the inverse-square law required practical? | To verify the inverse-square law for gamma radiation
What is the independent variable in the gamma inverse-square law practical? | The distance x between the source and the GM tube
What is the dependent variable in the gamma inverse-square law practical? | The count rate
What is measured first in the gamma inverse-square law practical, before the source is brought in? | The background count, averaged over several readings
Why is a 2 to 3 mm aluminium sheet placed in front of the GM tube in the gamma inverse-square law practical? | The source also emits alpha and beta, which the sheet absorbs so only gamma is counted
What graph is plotted to verify the inverse-square law for gamma radiation? | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}$ against $x$, where C is the corrected count rate
Why is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}$ plotted against x in the gamma inverse-square law practical? | If $C \propto \frac{1}{x^2}$, then $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}} \propto x$, giving a straight line
If $C = \frac{k}{x^2}$, what is the gradient of a graph of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}$ against x? | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$
In the gamma inverse-square law practical, why is the measured distance usually smaller than the true source-detector distance? | The radioactive material and the detecting region sit inside their casings, behind the points x is measured from
In the gamma inverse-square law practical, what does a graph of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}$ against x that cuts the negative x-axis show? | A systematic error in distance, equal in size to the intercept
What is the dead time of a GM tube? | The short time (about 100 μs) after a count during which the tube cannot register another
What effect does dead time have on recorded counts? | They are lower than the true counts
How would alpha or beta radiation reaching the detector affect the graph of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}$ against x? | It would make the graph curve slightly
