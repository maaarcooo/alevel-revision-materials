## Cathode Rays

### Discharge Tubes

- A **cathode** is a negatively charged electrode. An **anode** is a positively charged electrode.
- A **discharge tube** is a glass tube of low-pressure gas with an anode at one end and a cathode at the other, connected to a high-voltage supply. When the pd is applied, the gas conducts and glows.
- **Cathode rays** are beams of electrons emitted from the cathode.

### Evidence about Cathode Rays

| Observation | What it showed |
|---|---|
| A magnetic field deflected the rays in the direction expected for a negative charge | Cathode rays are **negatively charged particles** |
| In Crookes' tube, a small paddle wheel in the beam turned and moved towards the anode | Cathode rays are particles carrying **momentum and energy** from the cathode towards the anode |

### How the Tube Conducts

1. The strong electric field between the electrodes **ionises** some gas atoms, forming positive ions and free electrons.
2. The **positive ions** accelerate towards the cathode. When they strike it, they release electrons from its surface.
3. The **electrons** are accelerated towards the anode. These are the cathode rays.

The current is carried by electrons moving towards the anode and positive ions moving towards the cathode.

The gas must be at **low pressure**, so its particles are widely spaced. Charged particles can then accelerate to high kinetic energy between collisions.

### Why the Gas Glows

Light is produced in two ways:

- **Recombination:** positive ions and electrons collide and recombine into atoms in **excited states**. These de-excite to the ground state, emitting photons.
- **Excitation by collision:** fast electrons collide with gas atoms and excite them. The atoms then de-excite, emitting photons.

## Thermionic Emission

**Thermionic emission** is the emission of electrons from a heated metal surface. Heating a metal filament gives its electrons enough kinetic energy to escape the surface.

A current through the filament heats it. It is the **high temperature**, not the current itself, that gives electrons enough energy to escape.

### The Electron Gun

An **electron gun** emits electrons from a heated cathode and accelerates them towards an anode to form a beam.

- A **low pd**, a few volts, heats the filament.
- A **high pd**, hundreds of volts or more, between the cathode and the anode accelerates the electrons.
- Electrons accelerated towards the anode pass through a **small hole** in its centre, forming a narrow beam.
- The filament and anode are in a **vacuum or very low-pressure gas**, so the electrons are not stopped or scattered by gas molecules.

### Speed of the Electrons

The work done on an electron accelerated through a pd $V$ is $eV$. If it starts almost at rest, this all becomes kinetic energy:

$$\frac{1}{2}m_e v^2 = eV \qquad \text{so} \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}}$$

**Inline example:** with an anode pd of 500 V, $v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 500}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 1.33 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Specific Charge of the Electron

**Specific charge** is charge per unit mass, in C kg⁻¹. For the electron it is $\frac{e}{m_e}$. The specification needs any one method described in full, but calculations can be set on all three.

### Method 1: Magnetic Field Only (Fine Beam Tube)

An electron beam moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field follows a **circular path**, because the magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it provides the centripetal force.

$$Bev = \frac{m_e v^2}{r} \qquad \text{so} \qquad v = \frac{Ber}{m_e}$$

Combining this with $eV_A = \frac{1}{2}m_e v^2$ for the anode pd $V_A$ gives

$$\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{2V_A}{B^2 r^2}$$

**Method:**

1. Accelerate electrons from an electron gun through a measured anode pd $V_A$.
2. Apply a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the beam, usually from Helmholtz coils, and find $B$ from the coil current.
3. Measure the radius $r$ of the circular path.

The **fine beam tube** contains a low-pressure gas. The electrons excite the gas atoms, which emit light, making the circular path visible so its radius can be measured.

**Inline example:** electrons accelerated through 200 V move in a circle of radius $4.8 \times 10^{-2}$ m in a $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T field, so $\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{2 \times 200}{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})^2 \times (4.8 \times 10^{-2})^2} = 1.7 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$.

### Method 2: Crossed Fields (Thomson's Method)

A uniform magnetic field from **Helmholtz coils** and a uniform electric field between parallel plates act on the beam at the same time, perpendicular to each other and to the beam.

1. Adjust the pd $V$ across the plates, separation $d$, until the beam travels in a **straight line**. The electric force is then equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the magnetic force, so the resultant force is zero:
$$\frac{eV}{d} = Bev \qquad \text{so} \qquad v = \frac{V}{Bd}$$
2. **Either** switch off the electric field and measure the radius $r$ of the circular path in the magnetic field alone. Combining $v = \frac{Ber}{m_e}$ with $v = \frac{V}{Bd}$ gives
$$\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{V}{B^2 rd}$$
3. **Or** use the anode pd $V_A$: $\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{v^2}{2V_A}$, with $v = \frac{V}{Bd}$ from the balanced fields.

**Inline example:** electrons pass undeflected between plates 5.0 cm apart with a pd of 200 V in a $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T field. Their speed is $v = \frac{200}{2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.050} = 2.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

### Method 3: Electric Field Only

An electron entering the field between parallel plates at speed $v$, parallel to the plates, follows a parabola, like a projectile.

| Direction | Motion |
|---|---|
| Parallel to the plates (perpendicular to the field) | **Constant** velocity $v$. Time between plates of length $w$: $t = \frac{w}{v}$ |
| Perpendicular to the plates (along the field) | Constant acceleration from rest: $a = \frac{eV}{dm_e}$ |

The vertical deflection on leaving the plates is

$$y = \frac{1}{2}at^2 = \frac{1}{2}a\frac{w^2}{v^2}$$

Measuring $y$ and $w$, with $v$ found from the anode pd, gives $a$. Then $m_e a = \frac{eV}{d}$ gives

$$\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{ad}{V}$$

### Significance of Thomson's Result

- Thomson's specific charge for cathode-ray particles, about $1.76 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$, was **much larger**, about 1800 times, than that of the hydrogen ion, the largest then known (about $9.6 \times 10^{7}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$).
- This meant the electron had either a **much smaller mass** or a **much larger charge** than the hydrogen ion.
- Specific charge is a ratio, so a measurement of $\frac{e}{m_e}$ alone could not separate charge and mass. Measuring $e$ itself needed a further experiment, Millikan's.
- Thomson found the same specific charge whatever gas or cathode metal was used, so the electron is a constituent of all atoms.

## Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment

Millikan's oil drop experiment determined the **charge of the electron**.

### Apparatus

- A fine spray of oil drops enters the space between two horizontal parallel metal plates. The drops become charged as they are sprayed or by ionising radiation.
- Oil is used rather than water because it does not evaporate quickly, so each drop's **mass stays constant**.
- The drops are lit from the side and observed through a **microscope**.

### Holding a Drop Stationary

With a pd $V$ across plates a distance $d$ apart, a drop of charge $Q$ feels an electric force $\frac{QV}{d}$. The **pd is adjusted** until the drop is held stationary, when the electric force balances the weight:

$$\frac{QV}{d} = mg$$

**Inline example:** an oil drop of mass $1.6 \times 10^{-15}$ kg is held stationary between plates 10 mm apart with a pd of 980 V. $Q = \frac{mgd}{V} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.81 \times 0.010}{980} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

To find $Q$, the drop's mass is needed. It is too small to measure directly, so it is found from the drop's fall.

### Stokes' Law

**Stokes' law:** the viscous drag on a sphere of radius $r$ moving at speed $v$ through a fluid of viscosity $\eta$ is

$$F = 6\pi\eta rv$$

It applies to a **small, spherical** object moving at **low speed**. The unit of viscosity is Pa s ($\text{N s m}^{-2}$).

**Inline example:** a sphere of radius $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m moving at $1.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ through air of viscosity $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa s feels a drag of $6\pi \times 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 1.0 \times 10^{-4} = 3.4 \times 10^{-14}$ N.

### Motion without the Electric Field

With the field switched off, a drop quickly reaches **terminal velocity**, measured by **timing the drop as it falls a known distance**. Weight and viscous drag then balance:

$$mg = 6\pi\eta rv$$

The mass of a spherical drop of density $\rho$ is $m = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho$. Substituting:

$$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho g = 6\pi\eta rv \qquad \text{so} \qquad r = \sqrt{\frac{9\eta v}{2\rho g}}$$

This gives the radius, and so the mass. Two **assumptions** are made: the drop is **spherical**, and the **upthrust** from the air is negligible.

### Motion with the Electric Field

If the pd is increased beyond the balancing value, the drop rises. At constant speed $v$, the drag acts downwards with the weight:

$$\frac{QV}{d} = mg + 6\pi\eta rv$$

### Finding the Charge

Two measurements on each drop give its charge:

1. its **terminal velocity** with the field off, which gives $r$ and so $m$
2. the **pd needed to hold it stationary**, which gives $Q = \frac{mgd}{V}$

### Significance of Millikan's Results

Millikan measured the charge on many drops and found that **every charge was an integer multiple of $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ C**.

- This showed the **quantisation of electric charge**: charge exists only in integer multiples of the electron charge $e$.
- A charged drop always carries an integer multiple of $e$ because it has gained or lost a whole number of electrons.
- With $e$ known, Thomson's $\frac{e}{m_e}$ gave the mass of the electron, about $\frac{1}{1800}$ of the mass of a hydrogen atom. The electron's large specific charge was due to its small mass.
