# 5.4 Electromotive Force & Internal Resistance

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.5.1.6):** electromotive force, ε = E/Q; ε = I(R + r); terminal PD and EMF; applications in circuits of EMF and internal resistance; required practical 6 (investigation of the EMF and internal resistance of electric cells and batteries).

## Electromotive Force

When charge passes through a power supply such as a battery, it **gains** electrical energy.

**Electromotive force (EMF)** is defined as the amount of chemical energy converted to electrical energy per coulomb of charge when passing through a power supply:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{E}{Q}$$

where $\varepsilon$ = EMF (V), $E$ = energy transferred (J), and $Q$ = charge (C).

EMF is represented by the symbol $\varepsilon$ (Greek letter epsilon). Despite its name, it is not a force. It is measured in **volts (V)**.

EMF is equal to the potential difference across the cell when no current is flowing. It can be measured by connecting a high-resistance voltmeter across the terminals of the cell in an open circuit (voltmeter in parallel with the cell, no external load connected).

### Terminal Potential Difference

The **terminal potential difference (PD)** is the potential difference across the terminals of a cell. It is given by Ohm's law:

$$V = IR$$

where $V$ = terminal PD (V), $I$ = current (A), and $R$ = load resistance ($\Omega$).

If there were no internal resistance, the terminal PD would equal the EMF. In practice, since a cell has internal resistance, the terminal PD is always **lower** than the EMF.

### Lost Volts

In a closed circuit, current flows through the cell and a potential difference develops across the internal resistance. Since resistance opposes current, this reduces the energy per unit charge (voltage) available to the rest of the external circuit. This difference is called the **lost volts**, represented by lowercase $v$.

Lost volts is defined as the work done per unit charge to overcome the internal resistance inside the battery when current flows. It is the voltage lost in the cell due to internal resistance.

From conservation of energy:

$$v = \varepsilon - V = Ir$$

where $v$ = lost volts (V), $I$ = current (A), $r$ = internal resistance ($\Omega$), $\varepsilon$ = EMF (V), and $V$ = terminal PD (V). Rearranged, the terminal PD is $V = \varepsilon - Ir$, so it falls as the current drawn from the cell increases.

This explains why a lamp dims when another component is connected in parallel with it. The total resistance falls, so the current from the cell rises, the lost volts increase, and the terminal PD across the lamp falls. The reverse also holds: increasing the resistance of a component in parallel with the lamp reduces the current, the lost volts fall, and the lamp gets brighter. Assume a real cell has internal resistance unless a question says otherwise.

### The EMF Equation

The EMF is the sum of the terminal PD and the lost volts:

$$\varepsilon = IR + Ir = I(R + r)$$

where $R$ = resistance of the external load ($\Omega$) and $r$ = internal resistance ($\Omega$). The term $IR$ represents the terminal PD (voltage across the load) and $Ir$ represents the lost volts (voltage across the internal resistance).

EMF can therefore be defined as the **total**, or **maximum**, voltage available to the circuit.

**Inline example:** A battery has $\varepsilon = 7.3$ V and $r = 0.3\;\Omega$, connected to a $9.5\;\Omega$ load. The current is $I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r} = \frac{7.3}{9.5 + 0.3} = 0.74\text{ A}$ (2 s.f.). The lost volts are $v = Ir = 0.74 \times 0.3 = 0.22$ V (2 s.f.).

> **Exam tip:** If a question states "a battery of negligible internal resistance", the EMF equals the terminal voltage and no internal resistance calculations are needed. If the circuit diagram explicitly includes internal resistance, use the EMF equations.

## Internal Resistance

All power supplies have some resistance between their terminals. This is called **internal resistance** ($r$), defined as the resistance of the materials within the battery.

Internal resistance causes the charge circulating to dissipate some electrical energy from the power supply itself. This is why a cell becomes warm after a period of use. Over time, internal resistance causes a **loss of voltage** (energy loss) in the power supply. Combining cells changes both the EMF and the internal resistance. For $n$ identical cells in **series**, both add: the EMF is $n\varepsilon$ and the internal resistance is $nr$. For $n$ identical cells in **parallel**, the EMF stays $\varepsilon$ and the internal resistance is $\frac{r}{n}$, so the lost volts are smaller for the same current.

In terms of power, $\varepsilon I = I^2R + I^2r$: the power supplied by the cell equals the power delivered to the load plus the power wasted in the internal resistance.

### Circuit Model

A cell can be modelled as a source of EMF with an internal resistance connected in series. In such a circuit:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $R$ | Load resistor (external resistance) |
| $r$ | Internal resistance of the cell |
| $\varepsilon$ | EMF of the cell |
| $V_r$ | Lost volts (PD across $r$) |
| $V_R$ | PD across the load resistor (= terminal PD) |

The cell and internal resistance are enclosed within a dashed box in circuit diagrams to show they are part of the same component. The voltmeter reads the terminal PD $V_R$ across the external load, while the lost volts $V_r$ drop across $r$ inside the cell.

## Required Practical: Investigating EMF & Internal Resistance

### Aims

Investigate the relationship between EMF and internal resistance by measuring the variation of current and voltage using a variable resistor.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Resistance, $R$ ($\Omega$) |
| Dependent | Voltage, $V$ (V) and current, $I$ (A) |
| Control | EMF of the cell, internal resistance of the cell |

### Equipment

| Apparatus | Purpose |
|---|---|
| 1.5 V Cell | Provide an EMF to the circuit |
| Resistor (unknown value) | Act as internal resistance |
| 100 $\Omega$ Variable Resistor | Change the values of current and voltage in the circuit |
| Fixed resistor in series (optional) | Limits the current when the variable resistor is at zero, so the cell does not overheat or run down |
| Voltmeter (0--2 V range) | Measure voltage |
| Ammeter (0--200 mA range) | Measure current |
| Wires (at least 6 leads) | Make electrical connections |
| Switch | Open between readings to avoid running down the battery |

**Resolution of measuring equipment:** Voltmeter = 1 mV, Ammeter = 0.1 mA.

### Method

The circuit consists of the cell and the resistor labelled $r$ connected in series (treated as a single cell), in series with a switch, an ammeter, and a variable resistor $R$. A voltmeter is connected in parallel across $R$.

1. With the switch open, record the voltmeter reading $V$ (this gives the EMF directly since no current flows).
2. Set the variable resistor to its maximum value, close the switch, and record $V$ and $I$ from the ammeter. Open the switch between readings.
3. Vary the resistance of the variable resistor to obtain a minimum of 8--10 pairs of readings for $V$ and $I$, covering the whole range of the variable resistor.
4. Take 3 repeat readings at each resistance setting to calculate mean values of $V$ and $I$.

### Analysing the Results

Starting from $\varepsilon = I(R + r)$, expand and rearrange for $V$:

$$\varepsilon = IR + Ir = V + Ir$$

$$V = -rI + \varepsilon$$

Comparing to $y = mx + c$:

| Straight-line term | Physical quantity |
|---|---|
| $y$ | $V$ (V) |
| $x$ | $I$ (A) |
| Gradient ($m$) | $-r$ ($\Omega$) |
| $y$-intercept ($c$) | $\varepsilon$ (V) |

Plot a graph of $V$ (y-axis) against $I$ (x-axis) and draw a line of best fit. The $y$-intercept gives the EMF directly. The gradient is the negative of the internal resistance, so $r = -\text{gradient}$.

**Inline example:** From a dataset where the line of best fit passes through $(0,\;1.60)$ and $(66 \times 10^{-3},\;0.10)$, the gradient is $\frac{1.6 - 0.1}{0 - 66 \times 10^{-3}} = -22.7\;\Omega$ (3 s.f.). Therefore $\varepsilon = 1.60$ V and $r = 22.7\;\Omega$.

### Evaluating the Experiment

**Systematic errors:**
Only close the switch for as long as it takes to record each pair of readings. This prevents the internal resistance of the battery from changing during the experiment (prolonged current flow heats the cell and alters $r$).

**Random errors:**
Use fairly new cells, since the EMF and internal resistance of run-down batteries can vary during the experiment. Wait for the voltmeter and ammeter readings to stabilise before recording. Take at least 3 repeat readings for each voltage and current, and calculate a mean to reduce random errors.

### Safety Considerations

This is a low-risk experiment. Electrical components can get hot when used for prolonged periods. Switch off the power supply immediately if burning is smelled. Keep liquids away from the electrical equipment.

## Key Equations Summary

| Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\varepsilon = \dfrac{E}{Q}$ | EMF = energy transferred per unit charge |
| $V = IR$ | Terminal PD (Ohm's law for external circuit) |
| $v = \varepsilon - V = Ir$ | Lost volts across internal resistance |
| $\varepsilon = IR + Ir = I(R + r)$ | EMF equation (conservation of energy in full circuit) |
| $V = -rI + \varepsilon$ | Rearranged form for straight-line graph analysis |
