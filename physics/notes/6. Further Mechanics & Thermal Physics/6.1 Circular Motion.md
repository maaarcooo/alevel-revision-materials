# 6.1 Circular Motion

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.6.1.1):** motion in a circular path at constant speed implies an acceleration and requires a centripetal force; magnitude of angular speed ω = v/r = 2πf; radian measure of angle; centripetal acceleration a = v²/r = ω²r; centripetal force F = mv²/r = mω²r. The direction of angular velocity and the derivation of the centripetal acceleration formula are not examined.

## Uniform Circular Motion

An object in **uniform circular motion** moves round a circle at constant speed. At every instant its velocity points along the **tangent** to the circle, which is perpendicular to the radius at that point.

Velocity is a vector, so a change of direction is a change of velocity even when the speed stays the same. The object's velocity is therefore continuously changing, so it is **accelerating**, since acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

This acceleration is the **centripetal acceleration**. It is directed towards the centre of the circle, perpendicular to the velocity.

### Why a Resultant Force Must Act

The chain of reasoning examiners look for:

1. The direction of motion keeps changing, so the velocity changes.
2. A changing velocity means the object is accelerating.
3. By Newton's second law ($F = ma$), an acceleration requires a resultant force, acting in the same direction as the acceleration.
4. So a resultant force acts on the object, towards the centre of the circle.

An object moving in a circle is therefore **not in equilibrium**. If the resultant force were removed, Newton's first law says the object would carry on in a straight line at constant speed, along the tangent at the point where the force stopped.

## Radians and Angular Displacement

In circular motion, angles are measured in **radians** rather than degrees.

A **radian** (rad) is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius of the circle.

An angle in radians is the ratio of arc length to radius:

$$\theta = \frac{s}{r}$$

where $\theta$ = angle (rad), $s$ = arc length, or distance travelled round the circle (m), and $r$ = radius (m). Both lengths must be in the same unit, so the radian is a ratio with no dimensions.

The **angular displacement** $\Delta\theta$ of a body in circular motion is the angle, in radians, through which it has turned about the centre of its circle.

For a complete circle the arc length is the circumference, so the angle is:

$$\frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi \text{ rad} = 360°$$

### Converting Between Degrees and Radians

One radian is $\frac{360°}{2\pi} = \frac{180°}{\pi} \approx 57.3°$.

| Conversion | Method |
|---|---|
| Radians to degrees | Multiply by $\frac{180}{\pi}$ |
| Degrees to radians | Multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ |

| Degrees | Radians |
|---|---|
| 30° | $\frac{\pi}{6}$ |
| 45° | $\frac{\pi}{4}$ |
| 60° | $\frac{\pi}{3}$ |
| 90° | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ |
| 180° | $\pi$ |
| 270° | $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ |
| 360° | $2\pi$ |

> **Exam tip:** A calculator has a degree mode and a radian mode. The mode only matters when using sin, cos or tan, but using the wrong one is a common way to lose marks. In SHM (6.2), $\cos\omega t$ always needs radian mode.

## Angular Speed

The **angular speed** $\omega$ is the angle turned through per unit time, the rate of change of angular displacement:

$$\omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}$$

Angular speed is measured in $\text{rad s}^{-1}$. Angular velocity is the same quantity treated as a vector, but its direction is not examined.

One complete circle is an angle of $2\pi$ in one period $T$, and the distance travelled is $2\pi r$, so $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$. These give:

$$\omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$$

where $v$ = linear speed ($\text{m s}^{-1}$), $r$ = radius (m), $T$ = period (s) and $f$ = frequency (Hz). The linear speed is sometimes called the tangential speed. Rearranged, $v = \omega r$.

For points on the same rigid rotating object, such as a wheel, every point turns through the same angle in the same time, so **angular speed is the same for every point**. Linear speed is proportional to the distance from the axis, since $v = \omega r$.

For objects moving at the **same linear speed** on different radii, $\omega = \frac{v}{r}$, so the one on the larger radius has the smaller angular speed.

**Inline example:** A fairground ride turns once every 8.0 s, carrying a seat 5.0 m from the axis. Its angular speed is $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{8.0} = 0.79\;\text{rad s}^{-1}$, and the seat's linear speed is $v = \omega r = 0.785 \times 5.0 = 3.9\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Centripetal Acceleration

**Centripetal acceleration** is the acceleration of an object moving in a circle at constant speed, directed towards the centre of the circle:

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$

Substituting $v = \omega r$ into either form also gives $a = v\omega$.

The derivation of $a = \frac{v^2}{r}$ is not examined.

**Inline example:** A ball on a string moves in a horizontal circle of radius 1.5 m at $3.5\;\text{rad s}^{-1}$, so $a = \omega^2 r = 3.5^2 \times 1.5 = 18\;\text{m s}^{-2}$. If both the radius and the angular speed double, $a = \omega^2 r$ increases by $2^2 \times 2 = 8$ times, to $150\;\text{m s}^{-2}$ (2 s.f.).

## Centripetal Force

**Centripetal force** is the resultant force towards the centre of the circle that keeps a body moving in uniform circular motion. Applying $F = ma$ to the centripetal acceleration:

$$F = \frac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$$

where $F$ = centripetal force (N) and $m$ = mass (kg).

The centripetal force and the centripetal acceleration act in the **same direction**, towards the centre, as Newton's second law requires. Both are perpendicular to the velocity.

At a fixed radius, $F \propto v^2$, so doubling the speed needs four times the centripetal force.

### Centripetal Force Is Not a Separate Force

Centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is the name for the **resultant** of the real forces acting, whatever provides it. To solve a problem, find which forces act, take their resultant towards the centre, and equate it to $\frac{mv^2}{r}$.

| Situation | What provides the centripetal force |
|---|---|
| Car going round a roundabout on a level road | Friction between the tyres and the road |
| Ball on a rope whirled in a horizontal circle | Tension in the rope |
| Earth orbiting the Sun, or a satellite orbiting a planet | Gravitational force |
| Car on a track banked towards the centre | The horizontal component of the normal reaction force, together with any friction |

**Banked tracks.** On a level road only friction provides the centripetal force, so the maximum speed round a bend is limited by the friction available. On a track sloped towards the centre, the normal reaction force is tilted, and its horizontal component points towards the centre. This adds to the centripetal force, so a car can take a bend of the same radius at a greater speed.

### No Work Is Done

A centripetal force does **no work** on an object in uniform circular motion. The force is always perpendicular to the direction of motion, so the object never moves in the direction of the force ($W = Fs\cos 90° = 0$). The kinetic energy, and so the speed, stays constant. Only the direction of motion changes.

**Inline example:** A 300 g ball is whirled on a string in a circle of radius 0.8 m. The string breaks at 60 N. Rearranging $F = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ gives the maximum speed $v = \sqrt{\frac{rF}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.8 \times 60}{0.300}} = 12.6\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Vertical Circles

For a mass $m$ on a string moving in a vertical circle, the tension $T$ and the weight $mg$ act along the same line at the top and the bottom, and their resultant towards the centre is the centripetal force:

| Position | Forces towards the centre | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom of the circle | Tension acts up, towards the centre, weight acts away | $T - mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ |
| Top of the circle | Tension and weight both act down, towards the centre | $T + mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ |

At the same speed, the tension is greatest at the bottom, where it must support the weight as well as supply the centripetal force, and least at the top, where the weight supplies part of the centripetal force.
