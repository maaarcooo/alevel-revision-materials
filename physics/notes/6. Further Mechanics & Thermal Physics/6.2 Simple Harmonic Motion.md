## What Simple Harmonic Motion Is

An oscillator moves back and forth about an **equilibrium position**, the position at which the resultant force on it is zero. Its **displacement** $x$ is its distance from equilibrium in a stated direction, and its **amplitude** $A$ is the maximum displacement. The maximum displacement is the same on both sides.

When the oscillator is displaced, a **restoring force** acts on it: the resultant force, directed back towards the equilibrium position.

**Simple harmonic motion (SHM)** is an oscillation in which the acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement from the equilibrium position and is always directed towards that position:

$$a \propto -x$$

Equivalently, the restoring force is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it. Every complete oscillation takes the same time, and the period of an object in SHM does **not depend on its amplitude**. A larger amplitude means a longer path, but the object moves faster, so the time is unchanged.

SHM is a particularly simple kind of oscillation, easy to analyse mathematically, and many more complicated oscillations can be treated as combinations of SHMs. Examples of oscillators that move with SHM, or very nearly so, include a mass on a spring, a pendulum swinging through small angles, a ruler vibrating off the edge of a table, a guitar string, and liquid oscillating in a U-tube.

**Not SHM: a trampoline.** A person bouncing on a trampoline is not in SHM. While they are in the air the only force on them is their weight, which is constant and always downwards, so the restoring force is not proportional to the displacement.

### Showing That a System Moves with SHM

To show that an oscillator moves with SHM:

1. Write the resultant force on it at displacement $x$ from equilibrium.
2. Equate this to $ma$.
3. Show that the acceleration is proportional to $x$, because every other quantity in the expression is constant, with a minus sign because the force acts towards the equilibrium position.

For a mass on a spring the resultant force is $F = -kx$, so $ma = -kx$ and $a = -\frac{k}{m}x$. Since $k$ and $m$ are constant, $a \propto -x$, which is SHM with $\omega^2 = \frac{k}{m}$.

## The Defining Equation

$$a = -\omega^2 x$$

where $a$ = acceleration ($\text{m s}^{-2}$), $x$ = displacement (m) and $\omega$ = the **angular frequency** ($\text{rad s}^{-1}$), related to the period and frequency by $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$.

The minus sign shows that the acceleration is always opposite to the displacement. When the object is displaced to the right it accelerates to the left, and the reverse.

A graph of acceleration against displacement is a **straight line through the origin with a negative gradient**, running from $x = -A$ to $x = +A$. Its gradient is $-\omega^2$.

### Motion Through One Oscillation

Taking a pendulum bob released from one side:

- It speeds up as it moves towards the equilibrium position.
- Its velocity is greatest as it passes through the equilibrium position, where the acceleration is zero.
- It slows down as it moves out to the other side.
- It stops momentarily at $x = \pm A$, where it changes direction and the acceleration is greatest.

## Displacement and Velocity Equations

If the oscillator is released from its maximum displacement at $t = 0$:

$$x = A\cos\omega t$$

since $\cos 0 = 1$ gives $x = A$ at the start. If instead the oscillator passes through its equilibrium position at $t = 0$, the displacement is $x = A\sin\omega t$, since $\sin 0 = 0$. The two equations describe the same motion with a different starting point. In both, $\omega t$ is an angle in radians, so the calculator must be in radian mode.

The velocity at displacement $x$ is:

$$v = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$$

The $\pm$ shows that at any displacement the object may be moving in either direction. For a speed, take the positive value. A larger amplitude gives a greater speed at the same displacement, since the object covers a longer path in the same period.

**Inline example (displacement):** A 55 g mass on a spring is pulled down 4.3 cm and released at $t = 0$. It oscillates with period 0.80 s. Then $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{0.80} = 7.85\;\text{rad s}^{-1}$, and at $t = 0.30$ s, $x = 4.3\cos(7.85 \times 0.30) = -3.0$ cm. The minus sign means the mass is 3.0 cm on the other side of equilibrium from where it started. Keep the sign, because displacement is a vector.

**Inline example (speed):** A mass-spring system oscillates with amplitude 0.15 m at 6.7 Hz. At 0.12 m from equilibrium, $v = 2\pi \times 6.7 \times \sqrt{0.15^2 - 0.12^2} = 3.8\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Maximum Speed and Maximum Acceleration

The speed is greatest at the equilibrium position. Putting $x = 0$ into the velocity equation:

$$v_{\text{max}} = \omega A$$

The speed is zero at $x = \pm A$.

The acceleration is greatest at maximum displacement. Putting $x = A$ into $a = -\omega^2 x$ gives the magnitude:

$$a_{\text{max}} = \omega^2 A$$

The acceleration is zero at the equilibrium position. Take care not to confuse lower-case $a$ (acceleration) with capital $A$ (amplitude).

| Position | Displacement | Speed | Acceleration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equilibrium | $0$ | Maximum, $\omega A$ | $0$ |
| Maximum displacement | $\pm A$ | $0$ | Maximum, $\omega^2 A$, towards equilibrium |

**Inline example:** An oscillator has a maximum speed of $12\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ and an amplitude of 1.4 m. From $v_{\text{max}} = 2\pi f A$, $f = \frac{12}{2\pi \times 1.4} = 1.4$ Hz. Another has period 0.40 s and amplitude 2.8 m, so $a_{\text{max}} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{0.40}\right)^2 \times 2.8 = 690\;\text{m s}^{-2}$ (2 s.f.).

## Graphs of Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

Undamped SHM graphs against time are all sine or cosine curves with the same period.

- The **velocity-time** graph is the **gradient** of the displacement-time graph.
- The **acceleration-time** graph is the **gradient** of the velocity-time graph.

| Starting point at $t = 0$ | Displacement-time | Velocity-time | Acceleration-time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maximum positive displacement | Cosine, amplitude $A$ | Negative sine, amplitude $\omega A$ | Negative cosine, amplitude $\omega^2 A$ |
| Equilibrium, moving in the positive direction | Sine | Cosine | Negative sine |

Key features:

- The amplitude of the displacement graph is $A$, and its period is $T$.
- The velocity is greatest where the displacement graph is steepest, which is where $x = 0$, and zero where $x = \pm A$.
- The acceleration graph is the displacement graph reflected in the time axis, since $a = -\omega^2 x$. It is greatest where the velocity graph is steepest, at $x = \pm A$.

**Phase differences.** Velocity is a quarter of a cycle ($\frac{\pi}{2}$ rad, 90°) out of phase with displacement, and acceleration is a quarter of a cycle out of phase with velocity. Displacement and acceleration are therefore in **antiphase** ($\pi$ rad, 180°).

**Inline example:** A swing is pulled back 5 cm and released, and its displacement-time graph is a cosine curve that first crosses zero at 0.2 s. Its velocity is first at its maximum at 0.2 s, where $x = 0$.

## Simple Harmonic Systems

### Mass-Spring System

A mass $m$ on a spring of spring constant $k$ feels a restoring force given by Hooke's law:

$$F = -kx$$

As shown above, this gives SHM with $\omega^2 = \frac{k}{m}$, so the period is:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

where $T$ = period (s), $m$ = mass (kg) and $k$ = spring constant ($\text{N m}^{-1}$).

- The equation applies to both horizontal and vertical mass-spring systems.
- $T \propto \sqrt{m}$: four times the mass doubles the period.
- $T \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$: a stiffer spring gives a shorter period.
- The period does **not depend on $g$**, so the same system has the same period on the Moon as on Earth.

**Inline example:** A 2.0 kg mass on a spring with $k = 0.90\;\text{N m}^{-1}$ has $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2.0}{0.90}} = 9.4$ s, so its frequency is $f = \frac{1}{T} = 0.11$ Hz.

### Simple Pendulum

A **simple pendulum** is a small, dense bob on a light, inextensible string hanging from a fixed point. The bob is treated as a point mass, and the string stays taut throughout.

The restoring force is the **component of the bob's weight along the arc**, towards the equilibrium position, $mg\sin\theta$ for a string at angle $\theta$ to the vertical. For **small angles**, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ with $\theta$ in radians, and the arc is almost a horizontal straight line. The restoring force is then proportional to the displacement, so the motion is SHM with period:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$

where $l$ = length from the point of suspension to the centre of mass of the bob (m) and $g$ = gravitational field strength ($\text{N kg}^{-1}$).

- The equation holds only for small angles, less than about 10°, because it relies on the **small-angle approximation**. At larger angles the motion is no longer simple harmonic.
- $T \propto \sqrt{l}$: four times the length doubles the period.
- The mass of the bob has no effect on the period.
- The period **does depend on $g$**. On the Moon, where $g$ is about $\frac{1}{6}$ of Earth's, a pendulum with period 7.0 s on Earth has period $7.0 \times \sqrt{6} = 17$ s.

### Other Harmonic Oscillators

Any system whose restoring force is proportional to its displacement oscillates with SHM. An exam question may describe one, such as liquid oscillating in a U-tube, and will give the information needed. Model it on a spring or a pendulum: find the restoring force, show $a \propto -x$, and read off $\omega$.

**Inline example:** Liquid oscillating in a U-tube has period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{2g}}$, where $L$ is the total length of the liquid column, so it behaves like a pendulum of length $\frac{L}{2}$. For $L = 0.40$ m, $f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2 \times 9.81}{0.40}} = 1.1$ Hz.

## Energy in SHM

An oscillator continually exchanges kinetic energy with potential energy:

| System | Energy exchange |
|---|---|
| Mass oscillating horizontally on a spring | Kinetic energy and elastic potential energy |
| Mass oscillating vertically on a spring | Kinetic energy, elastic potential energy and gravitational potential energy |
| Simple pendulum | Kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy |

At maximum displacement the potential energy is greatest and the kinetic energy is zero. As the oscillator moves towards equilibrium it speeds up, so potential energy is transferred to kinetic energy, which is greatest at the equilibrium position. Past equilibrium the kinetic energy is transferred back to potential energy.

With no energy losses, the **total energy stays constant**, equal to the sum of the kinetic and potential energies, as conservation of energy requires:

$$E = E_k + E_p$$

with $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, elastic $E_p = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ and gravitational $\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h$. The total energy equals the maximum kinetic energy, so from $v_{\text{max}} = \omega A$:

$$E = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 A^2$$

### Energy Against Displacement

| Curve | Shape |
|---|---|
| Potential energy | U-shaped (a parabola), zero at $x = 0$, greatest at $x = \pm A$ |
| Kinetic energy | Inverted U-shape, greatest at $x = 0$, zero at $x = \pm A$ |
| Total energy | Horizontal straight line touching the top of both curves |

At every displacement the two curves add up to the total.

### Energy Against Time

Kinetic and potential energy both vary periodically, in opposite senses: when one is greatest the other is zero. Each reaches its maximum **twice per oscillation**, since the oscillator passes through equilibrium twice and reaches maximum displacement twice in each cycle. So the energy curves have half the period of the oscillation. Energy is never negative, and the total energy is a horizontal line at the maximum value of either curve.

**Inline example:** A 0.50 kg object in SHM has a kinetic energy graph that peaks at 60 mJ at $x = 0$ and falls to zero at $x = \pm 2.0$ cm. The total energy is 60 mJ and the amplitude is 2.0 cm. The maximum speed is $v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.060}{0.50}} = 0.49\;\text{m s}^{-1}$. Where the graph shows $E_k = 50$ mJ, the potential energy is $60 - 50 = 10$ mJ.

> **Exam tip:** When sketching energy graphs, draw smooth symmetrical curves and use a ruler for the total-energy line.

## Required Practical 7: Investigating SHM

### Mass-Spring System

**Aim:** find how the period depends on the mass, and from it the spring constant.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Mass, $m$ |
| Dependent | Period, $T$ |
| Control | The spring, the initial displacement, the number of oscillations timed |

**Method:**

1. Hang a mass hanger from a spring held in a clamp, with a fiducial marker (such as a pin) at the equilibrium position.
2. Pull the mass down 2 to 5 cm and release it.
3. Start the stopwatch as the mass passes the marker, and time 10 complete oscillations. Divide by 10 for the period.
4. Add 50 g and repeat, for 8 to 10 masses, pulling down by the same distance each time.

**Analysis:** squaring $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$ gives $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{k}m$. A graph of $T^2$ against $m$ is a straight line through the origin with gradient $\frac{4\pi^2}{k}$, so $k = \frac{4\pi^2}{\text{gradient}}$. This can be compared with the $k$ found from a force-extension graph using Hooke's law.

**Inline example:** A graph of $T^2$ against $m$ passes through $(0.06, 0.42)$ and $(0.40, 0.84)$ on its line of best fit. The gradient is $\frac{0.84 - 0.42}{0.40 - 0.06} = 1.24\;\text{s}^2\,\text{kg}^{-1}$, so $k = \frac{4\pi^2}{1.24} = 32\;\text{N m}^{-1}$.

### Simple Pendulum

**Aim:** find how the period depends on the length, and from it a value for $g$.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Length, $l$ |
| Dependent | Period, $T$ |
| Control | Mass of the bob, the small release angle, the number of oscillations timed |

**Method:**

1. Clamp the string between two wooden blocks so it hangs from a fixed point, with a fiducial marker behind the equilibrium position. Start with $l = 0.20$ m, measured from the point of suspension to the centre of mass of the bob.
2. Pull the bob to one side through a small angle and release it.
3. Start timing as the bob passes the marker. One complete oscillation is from the marker to one side, to the other side, and back to the marker moving in the same direction.
4. Time 10 oscillations and divide by 10. Repeat for 8 to 10 lengths.

**Analysis:** squaring $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$ gives $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{g}l$. A graph of $T^2$ against $l$ is a straight line through the origin with gradient $\frac{4\pi^2}{g}$, so $g = \frac{4\pi^2}{\text{gradient}}$. Compare the result with $9.81\;\text{m s}^{-2}$. A gradient of $4.0\;\text{s}^2\,\text{m}^{-1}$ gives $g = \frac{4\pi^2}{4.0} = 9.9\;\text{m s}^{-2}$.

### Evaluating the Experiments

- Timing 10 or more oscillations shares the reaction-time error between them and reduces the percentage uncertainty in the period.
- Start and stop timing as the oscillator passes the fiducial marker at equilibrium. It moves fastest there, so the moment it passes can be judged most precisely.
- View the marker at eye level to avoid parallax error.
- Keep the amplitude small. The pendulum's period equation only holds for small angles, and large swings may move in an ellipse rather than a plane. A spring pulled too far may oscillate sideways. Repeat any reading where the motion is not in a straight line or a plane.
- Release from the same displacement each time.
- A light gate or motion sensor with a data logger gives a more accurate period, since it removes the reaction-time error of a stopwatch.

**Safety:** put a soft surface under the apparatus in case the masses or bob fall, only pull the spring down a few centimetres so the masses do not come off, and clamp the wooden blocks tightly so the string cannot slip.
