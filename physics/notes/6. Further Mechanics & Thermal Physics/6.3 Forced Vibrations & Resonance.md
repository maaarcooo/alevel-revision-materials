# 6.3 Forced Vibrations & Resonance

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.6.1.3 and 3.6.1.4):** effects of damping on oscillations; qualitative treatment of free and forced vibrations; resonance and the effects of damping on the sharpness of resonance; examples of these effects in mechanical systems and situations involving stationary waves.

## Damping

In practice every oscillator eventually stops. **Resistive forces**, such as friction and air resistance, act on it in the direction opposite to its velocity. They do work against the motion, removing energy from the oscillating system, so the amplitude falls.

**Damping** is the loss of energy from an oscillating system through resistive forces, which reduces the amplitude of its oscillations. It continues until the oscillator comes to rest at its equilibrium position.

Do not confuse the two forces on a damped oscillator:

| Force | Direction | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Resistive (damping) force | Opposite to the velocity | Removes energy, so the amplitude decreases |
| Restoring force | Towards the equilibrium position | Keeps the system oscillating about equilibrium |

When the damping is light, the period of the oscillations stays almost constant as the amplitude falls. A child on a swing completes each swing in about the same time, whether the swing is high or low.

### Degrees of Damping

| Degree of damping | What happens when the system is displaced and released | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Light damping** | It oscillates many times, with an amplitude that decreases gradually | A pendulum swinging in air |
| **Critical damping** | It returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time **without oscillating** | A car's suspension, which settles quickly after a bump without bouncing |
| **Heavy damping** | It returns to equilibrium without oscillating, but more slowly than with critical damping | A door closer, which shuts the door slowly so it does not slam |

### Displacement-Time Graphs

- **Light damping:** a sine or cosine curve whose peaks and troughs shrink on both sides of the time axis. The amplitude decreases **exponentially**, falling by the same fraction each cycle. The peaks stay equally spaced, because the period does not change.
- **Critical damping:** no oscillation. The displacement falls steeply to zero without crossing the axis and then stays at zero.
- **Heavy damping:** no oscillation. The displacement falls slowly towards zero, without crossing the axis, and takes longer to reach equilibrium than with critical damping.

**Inline example:** The needle of a mechanical weighing scale sometimes oscillates before settling, making it hard to read. It should not oscillate, so critical or heavy damping is needed. It should also settle as quickly as possible so the reading can be taken straight away, which heavy damping would not give. Critical damping is the right choice.

## Free and Forced Vibrations

### Free Vibrations

A **free vibration** is an oscillation with no external periodic force acting and no energy supplied after it is set going. Only the system's internal forces act. A system displaced and then left alone vibrates freely.

A freely vibrating system oscillates at its **natural frequency**, $f_0$: the frequency at which it oscillates when it vibrates freely. Strictly, a free vibration loses no energy, which only happens in a vacuum, but a system vibrating in air is still treated as free as long as no external periodic force acts on it.

> **Exam tip:** "A free vibration is not forced" earns no marks. Mark schemes look for the absence of an external force and of an energy input.

### Forced Vibrations

A **forced vibration** is an oscillation produced by an external periodic **driving force** acting on the system. The system oscillates at the frequency of the driving force, the **driving frequency**, not at its own natural frequency.

To keep a damped system oscillating at constant amplitude, a periodic force must be applied. It does work on the system that replaces the energy removed by damping. A child on a swing is pushed once each cycle. Without the pushes, air resistance and friction would damp the swing to a stop.

**Inline example:** A tuning fork struck and left to ring is a free vibration at its natural frequency. A glass shattered by a high-pitched note, a car interior buzzing at a particular road speed, and the air column of a clarinet sustained by the vibrating reed are all forced vibrations, each driven at the frequency of an external source.

## Resonance

**Resonance** is the amplitude of a forced vibration reaching its **maximum** when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system.

At resonance, energy is transferred from the driver to the oscillating system at the greatest rate, so the amplitude builds up to its largest value. If the driving frequency is close to, but not equal to, the natural frequency, the amplitude still increases, but not as much.

> **Exam tip:** A definition of resonance must say the amplitude is a **maximum**. "The amplitude increases" or "large oscillations" alone does not earn the mark.

### The Resonance Curve

A **resonance curve** is a graph of the amplitude of a driven system against the driving frequency $f$:

- At low driving frequencies the amplitude is small.
- As $f$ rises towards $f_0$, the amplitude increases.
- The amplitude is greatest at the peak, where $f = f_0$.
- Above $f_0$ the amplitude decreases again.

### Effect of Damping on Resonance

As the damping of a driven system increases:

- The maximum amplitude decreases, so the peak is lower. The amplitude at every other driving frequency decreases as well, because more energy is lost from the system.
- The peak becomes **broader**, so the resonance is less sharp.
- The frequency at which the amplitude is greatest falls slightly below the natural frequency. The natural frequency itself does not change.

A lightly damped system therefore has a tall, sharp resonance peak, and a heavily damped one has a low, flat peak.

### Phase of a Driven System

The displacement of a driven system is not in step with the driver:

| Driving frequency | Phase difference between system and driver |
|---|---|
| Well below the natural frequency | Almost in phase |
| At resonance | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ rad, with the system lagging the driver |
| Well above the natural frequency | Almost in antiphase, $\pi$ rad |

### Barton's Pendulums

Barton's pendulums demonstrate resonance. Several light pendulums of different lengths hang from a horizontal string, together with one heavy driving pendulum of length $L$. When the heavy pendulum swings, it pulls the string back and forth and drives all the others at its own frequency.

The light pendulum with the same length $L$ as the driver swings with by far the largest amplitude, because its natural frequency equals the driving frequency. The others swing with small amplitudes. Pendulums shorter than $L$ swing almost in phase with the driver, the one of length $L$ lags it by a quarter of a cycle, and those longer than $L$ swing almost in antiphase.

## Examples of Resonance

**Useful resonance:**

- **Stationary waves.** The air column in an organ pipe, a flute or a clarinet resonates at one of its natural frequencies, setting up a stationary sound wave. A stretched string driven at one of its natural frequencies forms a stationary wave in the same way.
- **Radio tuning.** A radio's tuning circuit is adjusted so that it resonates at the frequency of the wanted broadcast.
- **A swing** pushed at its natural frequency swings higher with each push.

**Unwanted resonance in mechanical systems:**

- A bridge can sway with a large amplitude when footsteps or the wind drive it near its natural frequency, which can damage the structure.
- A glass can shatter when a sound at its natural frequency drives it.
- Parts of a car or a machine can vibrate violently at particular engine speeds.

Unwanted resonance is reduced by **increasing the damping**, which lowers the resonance peak, or by **changing the mass or stiffness** of the structure so that its natural frequency is far from any likely driving frequency.

## Key Terms Summary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Damping | Loss of energy from an oscillating system through resistive forces, which reduces its amplitude |
| Light damping | The system oscillates with gradually, exponentially, decreasing amplitude |
| Critical damping | The system returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating |
| Heavy damping | The system returns to equilibrium without oscillating, more slowly than with critical damping |
| Free vibration | Oscillation with no external periodic force and no energy input after it starts |
| Natural frequency | The frequency at which a system oscillates when it vibrates freely |
| Forced vibration | Oscillation produced by an external periodic driving force, at the driving frequency |
| Resonance | The amplitude of a forced vibration reaching its maximum when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency |
| Resonance curve | Amplitude against driving frequency, with a peak at the natural frequency |
| Effect of more damping | Lower peak, lower amplitude at every frequency, broader peak, peak slightly below $f_0$ |
