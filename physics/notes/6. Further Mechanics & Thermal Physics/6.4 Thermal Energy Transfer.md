# 6.4 Thermal Energy Transfer

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.6.2.1):** internal energy as the sum of the randomly distributed kinetic and potential energies of the particles in a body; internal energy increased by heating or by doing work on the system, and the reverse (a qualitative treatment of the first law of thermodynamics); during a change of state the potential energies of the particles change but not their kinetic energies; Q = mcΔθ, including continuous flow; Q = ml for a change of state.

## Internal Energy

The particles of every substance have both kinetic and potential energy:

- **Kinetic energy** comes from their **speed**, as they move or vibrate.
- **Potential energy** comes from the **intermolecular forces** between them, and depends on the separation of the particles.

**Internal energy** is the sum of the randomly distributed kinetic and potential energies of the particles in a body. It is the total of all the particles' kinetic energies plus the total of all their potential energies. Its symbol is $U$ and its unit is the joule.

"Randomly distributed" means the particles do not all share the same energy: at any moment they have a spread of speeds and separations.

> **Exam tip:** A definition of internal energy must say **sum** or **total** of the kinetic and potential energies of the particles. Internal energy is never an average. Mentioning the random distribution of the energies makes the definition complete.

The internal energy of a body depends on:

- Its **temperature**: a higher temperature means a greater mean kinetic energy of the particles.
- Its **state**: for the same substance and mass, a gas has the most internal energy and a solid the least, because the particles are further apart and their potential energy is higher.
- The **intermolecular forces** between its particles. An ideal gas has no intermolecular forces, so its particles have no potential energy and its internal energy is entirely kinetic (see 6.6).

## The First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is conservation of energy applied to internal energy:

> The internal energy of a system is increased when energy is transferred to it by heating or when work is done on it.

| Internal energy increases when | Internal energy decreases when |
|---|---|
| Energy is transferred **to** the system by heating | Energy is transferred **away from** the system by heating |
| Work is done **on** the system, such as compressing it | The system does work **on** its surroundings, such as expanding |

Only a qualitative understanding is required. No equation for the first law is examined.

### Work Done On or By a Gas

Mechanical work is done when a distance is moved against a force. For a gas in a cylinder with a moveable piston:

- When the gas is **compressed**, the surroundings do work **on** the gas. If the cylinder is insulated, so no energy leaves by heating, this work increases the internal energy, and the particles' mean kinetic energy, and so the temperature, rises.
- When the gas **expands**, the gas does work **on** the surroundings, pushing the piston out. If the cylinder is insulated, the energy for this work comes from the internal energy, so the particles' mean kinetic energy, and the temperature, falls.

The work done by a gas at constant pressure, $p\Delta V$, is covered in 6.5.

## Specific Heat Capacity

**Specific heat capacity** $c$ is the thermal energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K) without a change of state. Its unit is $\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$, or equivalently $\text{J kg}^{-1}\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}^{-1}$.

The thermal energy needed to change the temperature of a mass $m$ by $\Delta\theta$ is:

$$\Delta Q = mc\Delta\theta$$

where $\Delta Q$ = thermal energy transferred (J), $m$ = mass (kg), $c$ = specific heat capacity and $\Delta\theta$ = change in temperature (K or °C).

A temperature **difference** has the same value in kelvin and in degrees Celsius, so $\Delta\theta$ never needs converting.

A substance with a low specific heat capacity heats up and cools down quickly, because little energy changes its temperature. A substance with a high specific heat capacity, such as water, heats up and cools down slowly.

**Inline example (finding $c$):** A 1.7 kW kettle takes 3.5 minutes to bring 650 g of a liquid from 25 °C to its boiling point of 100 °C. The energy supplied is $E = Pt = 1.7 \times 10^3 \times 210 = 3.57 \times 10^5$ J, so $c = \frac{\Delta Q}{m\Delta\theta} = \frac{3.57 \times 10^5}{0.650 \times 75} = 7300\;\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$, assuming no energy is lost to the surroundings.

**Inline example (time to heat):** A 1200 W kettle holds 0.50 kg of water ($c = 4200\;\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$) at 22 °C. The energy needed to reach 100 °C is $Q = 0.50 \times 4200 \times 78 = 1.64 \times 10^5$ J, so the time is $t = \frac{Q}{P} = \frac{1.64 \times 10^5}{1200} = 137$ s.

### Measuring Specific Heat Capacity

**Electrical method.** An electric heater is placed in a hole in a block of the material, or in a known mass of liquid, with a thermometer. The energy supplied is $E = IVt$ from an ammeter, a voltmeter and a stopwatch, and $c = \frac{IVt}{m\Delta\theta}$. Insulating the block reduces energy losses to the surroundings, a systematic error that makes $c$ too large. Oil in the thermometer hole improves thermal contact.

**Method of mixtures.** A hot object of known mass and temperature is placed in a known mass of cold water in an insulated container, and the final temperature is measured. The energy lost by the hot object equals the energy gained by the water, so the object's specific heat capacity can be found.

Random errors in these experiments come from reading the thermometer and timing. Systematic errors come mainly from energy lost to the surroundings and from the energy needed to heat the container and heater. Insulation and a lid reduce them.

### Continuous Flow

In a **continuous-flow** problem a fluid flows steadily past a heater. In each second, a mass of fluid equal to the flow rate passes the heater and is warmed by $\Delta\theta$, so:

$$P = \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} c\Delta\theta$$

**Inline example:** Water flows at $0.50\;\text{kg s}^{-1}$ past a 9000 W heater. Each second 9000 J heats 0.50 kg, so $\Delta\theta = \frac{9000}{0.50 \times 4200} = 4.3$ K.

**Continuous-flow calorimeter.** The specific heat capacity of a fluid can be measured with a continuous-flow calorimeter. The fluid flows through a glass tube past an electric heating wire, and electric thermometers measure its temperature at the inlet, $T_1$, and the outlet, $T_2$, so $\Delta\theta = T_2 - T_1$. The mass of fluid heated is the flow rate (mass per second) multiplied by the time $t$. The current and PD of the heater are recorded.

For one run, the electrical energy supplied in time $t$ heats the fluid and some is lost to the surroundings:

$$IVt = mc\Delta\theta + E_{\text{lost}}$$

The flow rate is then changed, and the PD adjusted until the temperature rise $\Delta\theta$ is the **same** as before. With the same temperatures, the energy lost to the surroundings is assumed to be the same in both runs. Subtracting the two equations cancels $E_{\text{lost}}$:

$$c = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(m_2 - m_1)\Delta\theta}$$

where $Q = IVt$ for each run.

**Inline example:** Both runs last 40 s with a current of 3.0 A and a temperature rise from 15 °C to 26 °C, so $\Delta\theta = 11$ K. Run 1 has PD 14.0 V and heats 136 g, and run 2 has PD 9.0 V and heats 73 g. Then $Q_1 = 3.0 \times 14.0 \times 40 = 1680$ J and $Q_2 = 3.0 \times 9.0 \times 40 = 1080$ J, so $c = \frac{1080 - 1680}{(0.073 - 0.136) \times 11} = 870\;\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$.

## Changes of State

The changes of state are:

| Change | Name |
|---|---|
| Solid to liquid | Melting |
| Liquid to solid | Freezing |
| Liquid to gas | Boiling, evaporation or vaporisation |
| Gas to liquid | Condensation |
| Solid to gas | Sublimation |

While a substance changes state, its **temperature stays constant**, even though energy is being supplied or removed.

On a graph of temperature against energy supplied for a substance heated steadily from solid, the temperature rises, then stays constant along a horizontal section while the solid melts, rises again through the liquid, stays constant along a second, longer horizontal section while the liquid boils, and then rises through the gas. The lower horizontal section is melting (or freezing, if energy is removed) and the higher one is boiling (or condensation).

### Energy Changes During a Change of State

During a change of state, the **potential** energies of the particles change but their **kinetic** energies do not:

- The mean kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature. The temperature is constant, so the mean kinetic energy is unchanged.
- Energy **absorbed** during melting and boiling does work against the intermolecular forces of attraction, moving the particles further apart and increasing their potential energy.
- Energy **released** during freezing and condensation lets the particles move closer together, where the intermolecular attraction is stronger, so their potential energy falls.

The internal energy therefore increases during melting and boiling even though the temperature does not change.

## Specific Latent Heat

**Specific latent heat** $l$ is the thermal energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without any change of temperature. Its unit is $\text{J kg}^{-1}$.

$$Q = ml$$

where $Q$ = energy to change the state (J), $m$ = mass changing state (kg) and $l$ = specific latent heat ($\text{J kg}^{-1}$).

There are two specific latent heats:

| Quantity | Definition | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific latent heat of fusion** | Thermal energy required to convert 1 kg of solid to liquid with no change in temperature | Melting and freezing |
| **Specific latent heat of vaporisation** | Thermal energy required to convert 1 kg of liquid to gas with no change in temperature | Boiling and condensing |

The energy released when a mass of substance freezes equals the energy absorbed when the same mass melts, and likewise for condensing and boiling.

> **Exam tip:** A definition of specific latent heat must include "without a change of temperature", or words to that effect, for full marks.

For water, the specific latent heat of fusion is about $330\;\text{kJ kg}^{-1}$ and of vaporisation about $2.26\;\text{MJ kg}^{-1}$, so vaporising 1 kg of water needs roughly seven times more energy than melting 1 kg of ice. When ice melts, the molecules only need to move far enough apart to flow past each other. When water boils, the molecules must be separated completely, against all the intermolecular attraction, and the vapour must also do work pushing back the atmosphere as it expands.

**Inline example:** Boiling 530 g of a liquid needs 0.60 MJ, so $l = \frac{Q}{m} = \frac{0.60 \times 10^6}{0.530} = 1.1 \times 10^6\;\text{J kg}^{-1}$. This is a latent heat of vaporisation, since the change is from liquid to gas.

## Mixing and Thermal Equilibrium

When a warmer and a colder material are mixed, energy is transferred from the warmer to the colder until they reach **thermal equilibrium**, the state in which all the materials in the system have the same temperature. With no energy transferred to or from the surroundings:

$$\Delta Q_{\text{colder}} = -\Delta Q_{\text{warmer}}$$

The minus sign shows that energy is transferred **to** the colder material and **away from** the warmer one. Equivalently, energy gained by the colder material equals energy lost by the warmer one.

If the colder material changes state, include every stage. For ice below 0 °C added to a warm drink, the ice gains energy in three steps: warming the ice to 0 °C ($mc_{\text{ice}}\Delta\theta$), melting it ($ml$), and warming the meltwater to the final temperature ($mc_{\text{water}}\Delta\theta$).

**Inline example:** Six 14.5 g ice cubes at −5.0 °C (total 0.087 kg) are added to 0.20 kg of coffee at 90 °C. With $c_{\text{ice}} = 2090$, $c_{\text{water}} = 4190$, $c_{\text{coffee}} = 4180\;\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$ and $l = 334\;\text{kJ kg}^{-1}$, the energy balance for a final temperature $T$ is:

$$0.087 \times 2090 \times 5.0 + 0.087 \times 334\,000 + 0.087 \times 4190 \times T = 0.20 \times 4180 \times (90 - T)$$

$$909 + 29\,058 + 364.5T = 75\,240 - 836T$$

so $T = \frac{45\,273}{1200.5} = 37.7$ °C. A quick check: the answer lies between the starting temperatures of the ice and the coffee.
