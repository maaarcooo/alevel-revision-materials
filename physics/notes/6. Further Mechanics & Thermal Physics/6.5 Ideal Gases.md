# 6.5 Ideal Gases

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.6.2.2):** gas laws as experimental relationships between p, V, T and the mass of the gas; concept of absolute zero of temperature; ideal gas equation pV = nRT for n moles and pV = NkT for N molecules; work done = pΔV; Avogadro constant N_A, molar gas constant R, Boltzmann constant k; molar mass and molecular mass; required practical 8 (investigation of Boyle's law at constant temperature and Charles's law at constant pressure).

## Absolute Zero and the Kelvin Scale

**Absolute zero** is the temperature at which the particles of a substance have **zero kinetic energy**. It is the lowest temperature possible, $0$ K or $-273.15$ °C. No more energy can be removed from a system at absolute zero, so no temperature is lower and a kelvin temperature is never negative.

The gas laws give another way to see it. If the volume of a gas at constant pressure, or its pressure at constant volume, is plotted against temperature and the straight line is extrapolated, the volume or pressure reaches zero at absolute zero.

> **Exam tip:** Absolute zero needs "zero kinetic energy" of the particles, or "the temperature at which the pressure or volume of a gas would be zero". "The lowest possible temperature" alone does not earn the mark.

The **kelvin** (absolute, or thermodynamic) scale starts at absolute zero. Its divisions are the same size as those of the Celsius scale, so a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, and a temperature **difference** has the same value on both scales:

$$T/\text{K} = \theta/{}^{\circ}\text{C} + 273.15$$

Using 273 in place of 273.15 is accepted in exams. To remember which way to convert, recall that 0 °C is 273 K, so add 273 to go from Celsius to kelvin. For example, a room temperature of 300 K is $300 - 273.15 = 26.85$ °C.

**All gas law calculations must use temperatures in kelvin.**

## The Gas Laws

The three gas laws are **experimental** (empirical) relationships between the pressure $p$, volume $V$ and absolute temperature $T$ of a gas. Each holds for a **fixed mass of gas**, so the number of molecules is constant, with one of the three quantities held constant.

| Law | Held constant | Relationship | Two-state form | Graph |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Boyle's law** | Temperature | Pressure is inversely proportional to volume, $p \propto \frac{1}{V}$, so $pV$ = constant | $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$ | $p$ against $V$ is a curve approaching both axes. $p$ against $\frac{1}{V}$ is a straight line through the origin |
| **Charles's law** | Pressure | Volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature, $V \propto T$ | $\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2}$ | $V$ against $T$ in kelvin is a straight line through the origin |
| **Pressure law** | Volume | Pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature, $p \propto T$ | $\frac{p_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2}{T_2}$ | $p$ against $T$ in kelvin is a straight line through the origin |

For Boyle's law, a curve for a higher constant temperature has the same shape but lies further from the origin, since $pV$ is larger.

If the temperature axis is in degrees Celsius, the Charles's law and pressure law lines do not pass through the origin. Extrapolated back, they meet the temperature axis at $-273$ °C, absolute zero.

The three laws combine into one relation for a fixed mass of gas:

$$pV \propto T \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$$

**Inline example (pressure law):** A tyre's air is at $5.10 \times 10^5$ Pa at 279 K. After a ride it is at 299 K with the volume unchanged, so $p_2 = \frac{p_1T_2}{T_1} = \frac{5.10 \times 10^5 \times 299}{279} = 5.47 \times 10^5$ Pa, to 3 s.f. like the data.

**Inline example (Boyle's law):** A fixed mass of gas at constant temperature has pressure $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa at volume $3.0 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^3$. At $1.5 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^3$, $p_2 = \frac{p_1V_1}{V_2} = 2.0 \times 10^5$ Pa.

### Explaining the Gas Laws with Molecules

The molecules of a gas move randomly at high speeds and collide with the walls of the container. Each collision changes a molecule's momentum, and **force is the rate of change of momentum**, so the collisions exert a force on the walls. **Pressure is force per unit area.** The temperature of a gas is related to the mean kinetic energy, and so the mean speed, of its molecules.

| Change | Molecular explanation |
|---|---|
| Heating at constant volume (pressure law) | The molecules move faster, so they hit the walls more frequently and with a greater change of momentum each time. The force on each unit area of wall increases, so the pressure rises |
| Reducing the volume at constant temperature (Boyle's law) | The molecules' speed is unchanged, but with less space they hit each unit area of wall more frequently, so the force per unit area, the pressure, rises |
| Heating at constant pressure (Charles's law) | The molecules' mean speed rises, so they hit the walls more often and with a greater change of momentum. The gas must expand so that collisions are less frequent over a larger wall area, keeping the pressure the same |

> **Exam tip:** Explanations of pressure must refer to the **rate of change of momentum** (force) and to **force per unit area**. "More collisions" alone is not enough. Say collisions per second, or the frequency of collisions.

## The Ideal Gas Equation

An **ideal gas** is a gas that obeys the equation of state $pV = nRT$ at all pressures, volumes and temperatures. Its molecules:

- have negligible volume compared with the volume of the gas,
- collide elastically,
- exert no forces on each other except during collisions,

so an ideal gas obeys Boyle's law, Charles's law and the pressure law, and could never be liquefied. Real gases behave nearly ideally at low pressures and at temperatures well above their boiling points.

The ideal gas equation for $n$ moles:

$$pV = nRT$$

where $p$ = pressure (Pa), $V$ = volume ($\text{m}^3$), $n$ = number of moles, $R$ = molar gas constant $= 8.31\;\text{J mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$ and $T$ = absolute temperature (K).

For $N$ molecules:

$$pV = NkT$$

where $k$ = the Boltzmann constant $= 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\;\text{J K}^{-1}$.

Both constants are on the data and formulae sheet. Volumes given in $\text{cm}^3$ must be converted, multiplying by $10^{-6}$ to get $\text{m}^3$.

**Inline example:** A cylinder of volume $8.3 \times 10^3\;\text{cm}^3 = 8.3 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^3$ holds gas at $4.5 \times 10^7$ Pa and 15 °C (288 K). The amount of gas is $n = \frac{pV}{RT} = \frac{4.5 \times 10^7 \times 8.3 \times 10^{-3}}{8.31 \times 288} = 160$ mol.

**Inline example (keeping the ratio):** A gas in a fixed container is at $6.2 \times 10^5$ Pa and 30 °C (303 K). Heated to 40 °C (313 K), its pressure becomes $6.2 \times 10^5 \times \frac{313}{303} = 6.4 \times 10^5$ Pa. Keep unrounded values on the calculator between steps.

## Work Done by a Gas

When a gas expands it pushes on the walls of its container, so it does work on its surroundings, as when expanding steam pushes a piston in an engine. For a change in volume at **constant pressure**:

$$W = p\Delta V$$

where $W$ = work done (J), $p$ = pressure (Pa) and $\Delta V$ = change in volume ($\text{m}^3$).

For a gas pushing a piston of cross-sectional area $A$ a distance $s$, the force is $F = pA$ and the work is $W = Fs = pAs$. Since $\Delta V = As$, this is $W = p\Delta V$.

- When the gas **expands**, work is done **by** the gas.
- When the gas is **compressed**, work is done **on** the gas.

On a graph of pressure against volume, the work done is the **area under the graph** between the two volumes. This also applies when the pressure changes.

**Inline example:** A balloon is blown up from $0.015\;\text{m}^3$ to $0.030\;\text{m}^3$ at a constant $1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa. The work done is $W = 1.0 \times 10^5 \times 0.015 = 1500$ J.

## Moles, Molar Mass and Molecular Mass

### The Avogadro Constant

The **atomic mass unit** u is approximately the mass of a proton or neutron, $1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, so an atom's mass in u is roughly its number of nucleons. A carbon-12 atom has mass $12\;\text{u} = 1.99 \times 10^{-26}$ kg.

The **Avogadro constant** $N_A$ is the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12, $6.02 \times 10^{23}\;\text{mol}^{-1}$. One **mole** of any substance contains $N_A$ particles, so the number of particles in $n$ moles is:

$$N = nN_A$$

For example, 2.0 mol of helium contains $2.0 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.20 \times 10^{24}$ atoms.

### Molar Mass and Molecular Mass

The **molar mass** of a substance is the mass of one mole of it. One mole of an element has a mass equal to its relative atomic mass in grams, so 1 mol of helium-4 is 4 g. For a compound, add the relative atomic masses: water, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, has molar mass $2 \times 1 + 16 = 18\;\text{g mol}^{-1}$.

In physics questions, molar mass is given in $\text{kg mol}^{-1}$, so water's molar mass is $0.018\;\text{kg mol}^{-1}$. The number of moles in a sample is:

$$n = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{molar mass}}$$

The **molecular mass** is the mass of one molecule:

$$m = \frac{\text{molar mass}}{N_A}$$

with the molar mass in $\text{kg mol}^{-1}$ to give $m$ in kg.

**Inline example:** 6.0 g of magnesium-24 is $\frac{6.0}{24} = 0.25$ mol, which is $0.25 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.51 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. An argon atom, molar mass $4.0 \times 10^{-2}\;\text{kg mol}^{-1}$, has mass $\frac{4.0 \times 10^{-2}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 6.6 \times 10^{-26}$ kg.

### The Boltzmann Constant

The Boltzmann constant is the molar gas constant per molecule:

$$k = \frac{R}{N_A} = \frac{8.31}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\;\text{J K}^{-1}$$

Substituting $n = \frac{N}{N_A}$ into $pV = nRT$ gives $pV = \frac{N}{N_A}RT = NkT$.

The Boltzmann constant links the microscopic properties of particles, such as the kinetic energy of a molecule, to macroscopic properties, such as temperature, which is why its unit is $\text{J K}^{-1}$. Its value is tiny because the kinetic energy of a single molecule rises by a very small amount for each kelvin.

## Required Practical 8: Investigating the Gas Laws

### Boyle's Law

**Aim:** investigate how the pressure of a fixed mass of air varies with its volume at constant temperature.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Mass hung from the plunger, which sets the pressure |
| Dependent | Volume of air |
| Control | Temperature, cross-sectional area of the syringe |

**Method:**

1. Remove the plunger and measure the internal diameter $d$ of the syringe with a vernier calliper, averaging at least three readings.
2. Replace the plunger, push out as much air as possible, then seal the nozzle with rubber tubing and a pinch clip close to the nozzle so the mass of air is fixed.
3. Clamp the syringe vertically, nozzle up, with a loop of string from the plunger to a mass hanger below. Use a counterweight or G-clamp so the stand cannot topple.
4. Record the volume from the syringe scale with no masses, then add masses 200 g at a time, so each change of volume is significant, for 8 to 10 readings.
5. Wait a few seconds after adding each mass before reading the volume, so the air returns to room temperature after work is done on or by it.

**Analysis:**

- The cross-sectional area is $A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$.
- The hanging masses pull the plunger down with force $F = mg$, reducing the pressure of the trapped air by $\frac{mg}{A}$.
- The pressure of the air is atmospheric pressure ($101$ kPa) minus $\frac{mg}{A}$.
- Plot $p$ against $\frac{1}{V}$. A straight line through the origin confirms $pV$ = constant. A straight line that misses the origin indicates a systematic error.

**Errors:** friction between the plunger and the syringe is the main systematic error, reduced by using a low-friction or lubricated syringe. Taking repeat readings reduces the effect of random errors.

### Charles's Law

**Aim:** investigate how the volume of a gas varies with temperature at constant pressure.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Temperature of the water |
| Dependent | Height (length) of the trapped air column |
| Control | Pressure, which stays atmospheric |

**Method:**

1. A capillary tube, sealed at the bottom and open at the top, contains a drop of concentrated sulfuric acid that traps a column of air below it. The open top keeps the trapped air at atmospheric pressure. The tube is fixed to a ruler.
2. Stand the tube in a large beaker filled with freshly boiled water, with the water level above the acid drop so all the trapped air is at the water's temperature. Stir well.
3. As the water cools, read the height of the air column, up to the bottom of the acid drop, every 5 °C from 95 °C, for at least eight readings.

**Analysis:**

- The tube's radius is constant, so $V = \pi r^2 h$ is proportional to $h$, and the height can stand for the volume.
- Plot height against temperature in °C. A straight line shows that volume varies linearly with temperature.
- Extrapolate the line to the temperature where the height is zero, which estimates absolute zero. It must be found by extrapolation, because the water bath cannot reach such low temperatures without freezing.
- For the line $h = mT + c$, the height is zero at $T_0 = T_1 - \frac{h_1}{m}$, where $(T_1, h_1)$ is any point on the line of best fit.
- Compare the result with $-273$ °C using the relative percentage error, $\frac{\text{measured} - \text{accepted}}{\text{accepted}} \times 100\%$.

**Inline example:** A line of best fit has gradient $0.040\;\text{cm}\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}^{-1}$ and passes through $(60\;{}^{\circ}\text{C}, 13.2\;\text{cm})$. Then $T_0 = 60 - \frac{13.2}{0.040} = -270$ °C, a relative error of $\frac{-270 - (-273)}{-273} \times 100\% = -1.1\%$.

**Errors:** secure the tube close to and aligned with the ruler, or every reading will be out by the same amount (systematic). Read the ruler and thermometer at eye level to avoid parallax, and stir the water so the air and water are at the same temperature (random).

**Safety:** do not spill boiling water on skin or electrical equipment, and stand the beaker on a heat-proof mat.
