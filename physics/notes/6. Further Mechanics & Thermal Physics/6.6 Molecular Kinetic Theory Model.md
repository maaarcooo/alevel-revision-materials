## Gas Laws Versus Kinetic Theory

The **gas laws** (Boyle's law, Charles's law, the pressure law and the ideal gas equation) are **empirical**: they are based on experimental observation and evidence of how a gas responds to changes in pressure, volume and temperature.

**Kinetic theory** arises from **theory**. It starts from assumptions about the molecules of a gas and derives their behaviour using Newton's laws. It links the **microscopic** properties of the molecules, their mass and speed, to the **macroscopic** properties of the gas, its pressure, volume and temperature, and so explains why gases obey the gas laws. The molecular explanations of the three gas laws are set out in 6.5.

## Brownian Motion

**Brownian motion** is the random, erratic motion of small particles, such as smoke or pollen, suspended in a liquid or gas. It is usually observed as smoke particles in air, lit from the side and viewed under a microscope, where they appear as specks of light that jitter about.

Random motion means the particles have a range of speeds and no preferred direction of movement.

The visible particles are much larger and heavier than the air molecules that cause the motion. The air molecules move much faster than the smoke particles and collide with them unevenly from different sides. At any instant the collisions on one side outweigh those on the other, giving a resultant force that changes the particle's speed and direction at random.

Brownian motion is evidence for the **existence of atoms and molecules**: the motion can only be explained by collisions with invisible, fast-moving particles of the fluid.

## Assumptions of Kinetic Theory

The kinetic theory of an ideal gas rests on these assumptions:

- The gas contains a **very large number** of molecules, so their average behaviour can be used.
- The molecules are **identical**, all with the same mass.
- The molecules are hard spheres that collide **perfectly elastically**, so no kinetic energy is lost in collisions.
- The **volume of the molecules is negligible** compared with the volume of the container.
- The **duration of a collision is negligible** compared with the time between collisions.
- There are **no intermolecular forces**, except during collisions, so the molecules move in straight lines at constant speed between collisions.
- The molecules are in **continuous random motion**.
- The motion of the molecules obeys **Newton's laws**.
- External forces such as gravity are ignored.

> **Exam tip:** Questions often ask for two or three assumptions by name, so learn the list. A question on the derivation may ask which assumption justifies a particular step.

## Deriving $pV = \frac{1}{3}Nm(c_{\text{rms}})^2$

Molecules colliding with a wall change momentum, so the wall exerts a force on them and they exert an equal and opposite force on the wall. The force is the rate of change of momentum, and the pressure is the total force divided by the area of the wall.

Consider a cube of side $L$ and volume $V = L^3$ containing $N$ molecules, each of mass $m$.

**1. Change of momentum in one collision.** One molecule moves with velocity component $c_1$ perpendicular to one face. It hits the face and rebounds elastically with velocity $-c_1$, so its change of momentum is:

$$\Delta p = -mc_1 - mc_1 = -2mc_1$$

The momentum given to the wall is $+2mc_1$.

**2. Time between collisions with the same wall.** The molecule travels to the opposite face and back, a distance $2L$, before it hits the same face again:

$$\Delta t = \frac{2L}{c_1}$$

**3. Average force on the wall.** By Newton's second law, force equals rate of change of momentum:

$$F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{2mc_1}{2L/c_1} = \frac{mc_1^2}{L}$$

**4. Pressure from one molecule.** The face has area $L^2$, so:

$$p = \frac{F}{L^2} = \frac{mc_1^2}{L^3} = \frac{mc_1^2}{V}$$

**5. Many molecules.** For $N$ molecules with velocity components $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_N$ perpendicular to this face:

$$p = \frac{m}{V}(c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \ldots + c_N^2)$$

**6. Three dimensions.** The molecules move randomly in all directions. For each molecule, by Pythagoras in three dimensions, $c^2 = c_x^2 + c_y^2 + c_z^2$. Because the motion is random, with no preferred direction, the mean squares of the three components are equal, so each is one third of the mean square speed:

$$\overline{c_x^2} = \overline{c_y^2} = \overline{c_z^2} = \frac{1}{3}\overline{c^2}$$

The pressure on one face therefore uses one third of the sum of the squared speeds:

$$p = \frac{1}{3}\frac{m}{V}(c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \ldots + c_N^2)$$

**7. Root-mean-square speed.** The molecules have a range of speeds, and every one contributes to the pressure. The **root-mean-square speed** $c_{\text{rms}}$ is the square root of the mean of the squares of the molecular speeds:

$$c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \ldots + c_N^2}{N}}$$

so $c_1^2 + c_2^2 + \ldots + c_N^2 = N(c_{\text{rms}})^2$. The mean square speed $(c_{\text{rms}})^2$ has unit $\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2}$. Substituting and multiplying by $V$ gives the **kinetic theory equation**:

$$pV = \frac{1}{3}Nm(c_{\text{rms}})^2$$

where $p$ = pressure (Pa), $V$ = volume ($\text{m}^3$), $N$ = number of molecules, $m$ = mass of **one** molecule (kg) and $c_{\text{rms}}$ = root-mean-square speed ($\text{m s}^{-1}$). Here $c$ is a molecular speed, not the speed of light.

> **Exam tip:** In a derivation, explain each step in words, naming the physics used (elastic collision, Newton's second law, pressure = force / area, random motion in three dimensions). Equations alone do not earn full marks.

### In Terms of Density

The density of the gas is $\rho = \frac{Nm}{V}$, so dividing the kinetic theory equation by $V$ gives:

$$p = \frac{1}{3}\rho(c_{\text{rms}})^2$$

**Inline example:** An ideal gas of density $4.5\;\text{kg m}^{-3}$ is at a pressure of $9.3 \times 10^5$ Pa. Its r.m.s. speed is $c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3p}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 9.3 \times 10^5}{4.5}} = 790\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Average Molecular Kinetic Energy

Both $pV = NkT$ (from experiment) and $pV = \frac{1}{3}Nm(c_{\text{rms}})^2$ (from theory) describe an ideal gas, so their right-hand sides are equal:

$$\frac{1}{3}Nm(c_{\text{rms}})^2 = NkT$$

Cancelling $N$ and multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ gives the **average kinetic energy of one molecule**:

$$\frac{1}{2}m(c_{\text{rms}})^2 = \frac{3}{2}kT = \frac{3RT}{2N_A}$$

using $k = \frac{R}{N_A}$. The temperature must be in kelvin.

Key consequences:

- The mean kinetic energy of a molecule is **directly proportional to the absolute temperature**, $\bar{E}_k \propto T$. This is what temperature means at the molecular level.
- At the same temperature, the molecules of **any** two ideal gases have the **same mean kinetic energy**, because $\frac{3}{2}kT$ depends only on temperature, not on the mass of the molecules. Lighter molecules therefore have a higher $c_{\text{rms}}$.
- For a given gas, $(c_{\text{rms}})^2 \propto T$, so $c_{\text{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}$.

The equation gives the kinetic energy of **one** average molecule. Multiply by $N$ for the whole gas.

**Inline example:** At 300 K the mean kinetic energy of an ideal gas molecule is $\frac{3}{2} \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 = 6.21 \times 10^{-21}$ J.

**Inline example:** Helium has $c_{\text{rms}} = 720\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ at 45 °C (318 K). At 80 °C (353 K), $c_{\text{rms}} = 720 \times \sqrt{\frac{353}{318}} = 760\;\text{m s}^{-1}$. Converting to kelvin is essential, since $c_{\text{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}$ only for absolute temperature.

## Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

Molecules in solids and liquids have both kinetic and potential energy, because they are close together and bound by intermolecular forces. When a solid is heated its molecules vibrate more, and when a gas is heated its molecules move faster.

An ideal gas has **no intermolecular forces**, so its molecules have **no potential energy**. Its internal energy is therefore the **sum of the kinetic energies of all its molecules**:

$$U = N\bar{E}_k = \frac{3}{2}NkT = \frac{3}{2}nRT$$

The internal energy of a fixed amount of ideal gas is **directly proportional to its absolute temperature**, $U \propto T$.

**Inline example:** 3.00 mol of an ideal gas contains $N = 3.00 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.806 \times 10^{24}$ molecules. If their mean kinetic energy is $6.53 \times 10^{-21}$ J, the internal energy is $U = 1.806 \times 10^{24} \times 6.53 \times 10^{-21} = 1.18 \times 10^4$ J.

**Inline example (moles to molecules):** A bottle holds 128 g of oxygen (molar mass $0.032\;\text{kg mol}^{-1}$) at 330 K. That is $\frac{0.128}{0.032} = 4.0$ mol, or $4.0 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.41 \times 10^{24}$ molecules. Each has mean kinetic energy $\frac{3}{2} \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 330 = 6.83 \times 10^{-21}$ J, so the total kinetic energy is $2.41 \times 10^{24} \times 6.83 \times 10^{-21} = 1.6 \times 10^4$ J.

## How Ideas About Gases Developed

Knowledge of gases built up over many centuries, and scientific models change as new evidence appears:

| Who and when | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Democritus, about 2000 years ago | Proposed that matter is made of indivisible pieces, *atomos*. Aristotle and Plato rejected the idea, and their influence kept it out of favour for almost 2000 years |
| Robert Boyle, 1662 | Pressure and volume at constant temperature: Boyle's law |
| Guillaume Amontons, 1699, and later Joseph Gay-Lussac | Pressure and temperature at constant volume: the pressure law |
| Jacques Charles, 1787 | Volume and temperature at constant pressure: Charles's law |
| Daniel Bernoulli, 18th century | Proposed that gases are made of tiny moving particles, the start of kinetic theory, which was not widely accepted for a long time |
| Robert Brown, 1827 | Observed Brownian motion, the random movement of tiny particles in a fluid |
| Albert Einstein, 1905 | Used kinetic theory to predict Brownian motion quantitatively. After this, the atomic and kinetic theories became widely accepted |

The names and dates do not need to be learned. What matters is that scientific ideas are rarely accepted at once. A new theory must be **validated**: other scientists repeat the experiments and test its predictions, and the model is accepted, refined or replaced in light of the evidence.
