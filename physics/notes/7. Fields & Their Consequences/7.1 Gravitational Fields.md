## Force Fields

A **force field** is a region in which a body experiences a **non-contact force**, a force that acts between bodies without physical contact. Pushing a trolley is a contact force. A magnet attracting a steel fridge door is a non-contact force.

Force fields arise from interactions between bodies or particles:

| Field | What experiences a force in it |
|---|---|
| Gravitational | Anything with **mass** |
| Electric | **Charged** particles, whether stationary or moving |
| Magnetic | **Moving** charged particles, moving across the field lines |

A force field can be represented by a vector at each point, showing the direction of the force a body would feel there. The direction is found by inspection. The field is drawn with **field lines** (lines of force):

- The **direction** of a field line, shown by its arrow, is the direction of the force on a body placed at that point.
- The **spacing** of the lines shows the field strength: closer lines mean a stronger field.

The gravitational fields of the Moon and the Sun reach the Earth, where they cause the tides.

## Gravitational Fields

A **gravitational field** is a region of space where a mass experiences a force due to the gravitational attraction of another mass.

Gravity is a **universal** force: it acts between all matter with mass. It is **always attractive**, never repulsive, and has an **infinite range**, although it is very weak between small masses such as atoms and only becomes large near very massive bodies such as planets and stars. The **weight** of an object is the gravitational force acting on it, and the Earth's field is responsible for the weight of every object on Earth.

### Gravitational Field Lines

| Field | Field lines | Field strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Radial** field, around a point mass or a uniform sphere | Radial lines pointing **inwards**, towards the centre of the mass | Not uniform. The lines spread out with distance, so $g$ decreases with the inverse square of the distance from the centre |
| **Uniform** field | Parallel, equally spaced lines | The same at every point |

The Earth's field is radial, but near its surface, over distances small compared with the Earth's radius, the lines are almost parallel and equally spaced, so the field is approximately **uniform**.

When drawing field lines, always add arrows. Gravitational field lines point towards the mass: towards the centre of a sphere in a radial field, and towards the surface of the Earth in a uniform field near it.

### Point Masses

A **uniform sphere** is a sphere whose mass is distributed evenly. For any point **outside** a uniform sphere, its gravitational field is identical to that of a point mass at its centre, so its field lines are drawn converging on the centre.

Stars and planets can be treated as point masses when applying Newton's law of gravitation, because they are approximately uniform spheres and their separation is much larger than their radii.

## Newton's Law of Gravitation

Newton's law of gravitation states that the gravitational force between two point masses is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation:

$$F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$$

where $F$ = gravitational force (N), $G$ = the gravitational constant $= 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\;\text{N m}^2\,\text{kg}^{-2}$, $m_1$ and $m_2$ = the two masses (kg) and $r$ = the distance between their centres (m).

The force acts on each mass, towards the other, and the two forces are equal and opposite, as Newton's third law requires.

A relationship in which a quantity is proportional to $\frac{1}{r^2}$ is an **inverse square law**. When the separation doubles, the force falls to $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$ of its value. When it triples, the force falls to $\frac{1}{9}$.

**Distances are measured from the centre of each body.** For a satellite at height $h$ above a planet of radius $R$:

$$r = R + h$$

Forgetting to add the planet's radius is a common exam mistake.

**Inline example:** Two 1000 kg point masses 2.0 m apart attract each other with $F = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1000 \times 1000}{2.0^2} = 1.7 \times 10^{-5}$ N, a tiny force, which shows how weak gravity is between everyday objects.

**Inline example:** A 6500 kg satellite orbits 2000 km above the Earth's surface, where the gravitational force on it is 37 kN. With $R = 6400$ km, $r = 6400 + 2000 = 8400$ km $= 8.4 \times 10^6$ m, and the Earth's mass is $M = \frac{Fr^2}{Gm} = \frac{37 \times 10^3 \times (8.4 \times 10^6)^2}{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6500} = 6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg.

## Gravitational Field Strength

The **gravitational field strength** $g$ at a point is the gravitational force per unit mass on a mass placed at that point:

$$g = \frac{F}{m}$$

Its unit is $\text{N kg}^{-1}$, which is equivalent to $\text{m s}^{-2}$. Gravitational field strength is a **vector**, directed towards the centre of mass of the body creating the field, the same direction as the field lines.

For an object falling freely, the only force is its weight $mg$, so its acceleration is $g$. This is why $g$ is also called the acceleration due to gravity. Near the Earth's surface $g = 9.81\;\text{N kg}^{-1}$, and any object falling freely there accelerates at $9.81\;\text{m s}^{-2}$.

An object's **mass** is the same everywhere, but its **weight** depends on $g$. On a planet with a larger surface $g$, such as Jupiter, the same object weighs much more.

Do not confuse $g$, the gravitational field strength, which varies from place to place, with $G$, the universal gravitational constant.

**Inline example:** A 2.0 kg mass experiences a gravitational force of 19.6 N, so $g = \frac{19.6}{2.0} = 9.8\;\text{N kg}^{-1}$. An object weighing 10 N on Earth has mass $\frac{10}{9.81} = 1.0$ kg.

### Field Strength in a Radial Field

Placing a test mass $m$ at distance $r$ from a point mass $M$ and dividing Newton's law by $m$:

$$g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$$

where $M$ = mass of the body producing the field (kg) and $r$ = distance from its centre (m).

Outside a planet of radius $R$, $g \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$, so the field strength falls rapidly with distance. At $r = 3R$ it is $\frac{g}{9}$, where $g$ is the surface value.

On a graph of $g$ against $r$ for a uniform planet:

- From the centre to the surface ($r < R$), $g$ increases in proportion to $r$, from zero at the centre to its greatest value at the surface.
- Outside the planet ($r > R$), $g$ follows an inverse-square curve, falling steeply at first and then levelling towards zero.

The surface field strength depends on the planet's **mass and radius**. Writing the mass of a uniform planet as $M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\rho$:

$$g = \frac{GM}{R^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho R$$

so the surface field strength is proportional to the product of the planet's density and radius.

**Inline example:** A planet of mass $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg and radius $6.4 \times 10^6$ m has surface field strength $g = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24}}{(6.4 \times 10^6)^2} = 9.8\;\text{N kg}^{-1}$.

**Inline example:** The Moon's mean density is $\frac{3}{5}$ of the Earth's, and its surface $g$ is $\frac{1}{6}$ of the Earth's. Since $g \propto \rho R$, $\frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{R_M}{R_E}$, so $\frac{R_M}{R_E} = \frac{5}{18} = 0.28$.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Force field | A region in which a body experiences a non-contact force |
| Gravitational field | A region where a mass experiences a force due to the gravitational attraction of another mass |
| Field lines | Direction shows the force on a body, spacing shows the field strength |
| Uniform field | Parallel, equally spaced field lines, $g$ the same everywhere |
| Radial field | Lines pointing to the centre, $g \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$ |
| $F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$ | Newton's law of gravitation, $r$ between centres |
| Gravitational field strength | Force per unit mass, $g = \frac{F}{m}$, in $\text{N kg}^{-1}$ |
| $g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$ | Field strength in a radial field |
| $g = \frac{4}{3}\pi G\rho R$ | Surface field strength of a uniform planet |
| $r = R + h$ | Distance from a planet's centre at height $h$ |
