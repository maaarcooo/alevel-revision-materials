# 7.10 Alternating Currents & Transformers

## Alternating Current

An **alternating current** (a.c.) is a current that periodically reverses direction, varying between positive and negative values with time. Its direction reverses every **half cycle**. A coil rotating in a magnetic field produces an alternating EMF, $\varepsilon = BAN\omega\sin(\omega t)$ (7.9), which is why generators produce a.c.

At A-level only **sinusoidal** a.c. is studied: the current or voltage follows a sine curve, and the rms equations below apply to sinusoidal waveforms only. As in SHM, the period and frequency are related by $f = \frac{1}{T}$.

### Peak and Peak-to-Peak Values

- The **peak** value, $I_0$ or $V_0$, is the maximum value measured from zero, equal to the amplitude of the waveform.
- The **peak-to-peak** value is the difference between the maximum positive and maximum negative values, so for a sinusoidal a.c. it is twice the peak value:

$$V_0 = \frac{\text{peak-to-peak voltage}}{2}$$

### Root-Mean-Square Values

The **rms value** of an alternating current is the steady direct current that delivers the same average power in a resistor as the alternating current. It is calculated as the square root of the mean of the squares of all the current values in one cycle. The rms voltage is defined in the same way.

The rms value lets an a.c. be compared fairly with a d.c.: an rms current of 2 A has the same heating effect in a resistor as a steady 2 A.

For a sinusoidal a.c.:

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} \qquad V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$$

so the rms value is about 0.707, roughly 70%, of the peak value.

The average power delivered to a resistor is:

$$P = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}$$

**Inline example:** A 230 V rms supply delivering an rms current of 2.0 A to a resistor supplies $P = 2.0 \times 230 = 460$ W on average.

### Mains Electricity

UK mains electricity is supplied by the National Grid as a.c. at **230 V rms** and **50 Hz**. The quoted 230 V is the rms value, so:

- Peak voltage: $V_0 = \sqrt{2} \times 230 = 325$ V.
- Peak-to-peak voltage: $2 \times 325 = 650$ V.

Many devices, such as computers, run on d.c., so they contain a step-down transformer and circuitry to convert mains a.c. to a low d.c. voltage.

## The Oscilloscope

An oscilloscope (cathode-ray oscilloscope, CRO) displays voltage on the y-axis against time on the x-axis. It can be used as an a.c. and d.c. voltmeter, to measure time intervals and frequencies, and to display a.c. waveforms.

| Control | What it sets | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Y-gain** (voltage sensitivity) | The voltage represented by each vertical division | $\text{V div}^{-1}$ |
| **Time-base** | The time represented by each horizontal division | for example $\text{ms div}^{-1}$ |

**Taking measurements:**

- **Period:** count the horizontal divisions spanned by one complete cycle and multiply by the time-base. To reduce uncertainty, measure the time for as many complete cycles as fit on the screen and divide by the number of cycles. Then $f = \frac{1}{T}$.
- **Peak voltage:** count the vertical divisions from the zero line to a peak and multiply by the y-gain.
- **Peak-to-peak voltage:** count the vertical divisions from the minimum to the maximum and multiply by the y-gain.
- Watch the units: a time-base given in ms or μs per division, or per mm or cm, must be converted.

**Inline example:** One cycle spans 4 divisions at a time-base of $5\;\text{ms div}^{-1}$, so $T = 4 \times 5 = 20$ ms and $f = \frac{1}{0.020} = 50$ Hz.

**What the traces look like:**

| Input | Time-base on | Time-base off |
|---|---|---|
| d.c. voltage | A horizontal line, whose height above the zero line in divisions times the y-gain gives the voltage | A single dot at that height |
| a.c. voltage | A sinusoidal waveform across the screen, from which the period and peak voltage can be measured | A vertical line whose length represents the peak-to-peak voltage |

For a d.c. trace the time-base setting does not matter, since there is no period, but the y-gain is needed to read the voltage.

## Transformers

A **transformer** is a device that changes a high alternating voltage at low current to a low alternating voltage at high current, or the reverse. It consists of:

- a **primary coil**, connected to the input,
- a **secondary coil**, connected to the output,
- an **iron core** that both coils are wound around.

### How a Transformer Works

1. An alternating current in the primary coil produces an **alternating magnetic flux** in the core.
2. The core concentrates the flux and links it through the secondary coil.
3. The flux linkage of the secondary coil therefore keeps changing, so by Faraday's law an alternating EMF is induced in the secondary.

The output has the **same frequency** as the input. The input must be **alternating**, because only a changing current produces the changing flux linkage needed to induce an EMF. A steady d.c. input would induce nothing once switched on.

The core is made of **soft iron**, which is easily magnetised and demagnetised, so little energy is lost each time its magnetisation reverses.

### The Transformer Equation

$$\frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{V_s}{V_p}$$

where $N_s$ and $N_p$ = numbers of turns on the secondary and primary coils, and $V_s$ and $V_p$ = secondary (output) and primary (input) voltages.

| Type | Turns | Effect on voltage | Use in the National Grid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Step-up** | $N_s > N_p$ | Increases it | Between power stations and the transmission lines |
| **Step-down** | $N_p > N_s$ | Decreases it | Between the transmission lines and homes or other buildings |

**Inline example:** A transformer with 1000 primary turns and 50 secondary turns on a 230 V supply gives $V_s = 230 \times \frac{50}{1000} = 11.5$ V. A step-down transformer turning 500 V into 100 V with 3000 primary turns needs $N_s = 3000 \times \frac{100}{500} = 600$ turns.

### Ideal Transformers and Efficiency

An ideal transformer is 100% efficient, so the power in equals the power out:

$$I_pV_p = I_sV_s \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{I_s}{I_p} = \frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}$$

Stepping the voltage up steps the current down by the same factor, and the reverse.

Real transformers are not 100% efficient. The **efficiency** is the ratio of output power to input power:

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{I_sV_s}{I_pV_p}$$

as a decimal, or multiplied by 100 as a percentage.

**Inline example:** An ideal transformer with turns ratio $N_p : N_s = 20 : 1$ and a primary current of 0.50 A has $I_s = 0.50 \times 20 = 10$ A. A transformer with $V_p = 200$ V, $I_p = 0.50$ A, $V_s = 20$ V and $I_s = 4.0$ A has efficiency $\frac{20 \times 4.0}{200 \times 0.50} = 0.80$.

**Inline example:** A 15 V, 80 W lamp runs at full brightness from the secondary of a 65% efficient transformer on a 220 V supply. The input power is $\frac{80}{0.65} = 123$ W, so the primary current is $\frac{123}{220} = 0.56$ A.

### Causes of Inefficiency

| Cause | Why energy is lost | How it is reduced |
|---|---|---|
| **Eddy currents** | The changing flux induces circulating currents in the conducting core. By Lenz's law their field opposes the change that induced them, and they heat the core | **Laminate** the core: build it from thin layers of iron insulated from each other, so eddy currents are confined to thin layers and are much smaller. Use a core material of high resistivity |
| **Resistance of the coils** | Current in the wires causes heating, more so for larger currents | Use **thick wires**, which have low resistance, especially for the secondary coil of a step-down transformer, which carries the larger current |
| **Hysteresis** | Energy is needed to reverse the magnetisation of the core every half cycle | Use a **soft iron** core, which is easily magnetised and demagnetised |
| **Flux leakage** | Not all the flux from the primary passes through the secondary | Design the core so all the primary's flux links the secondary, for example winding the coils on top of each other |

## Transmitting Electrical Power

Electricity is transmitted over long distances, and the cables have resistance, so some power is lost as heat:

$$P_{\text{lost}} = I^2R$$

where $I$ = current in the cables and $R$ = their total resistance. Since $P \propto I^2$, doubling the current quadruples the power lost.

For a given power $P = IV$, a **higher voltage** means a **lower current**, and so much smaller $I^2R$ losses. Power stations therefore use step-up transformers to transmit at high voltage, and step-down transformers reduce the voltage to 230 V before it reaches homes.

The transmission voltage is not made as high as possible, because very high voltages need much better insulation, taller pylons and more expensive equipment.

**Inline example:** A cable of resistance 5.0 Ω carrying 100 A loses $100^2 \times 5.0 = 5.0 \times 10^4$ W.

**Inline example:** A power station's 25 kV output feeds a step-up transformer with 50 primary and 750 secondary turns, giving $V_s = 25\,000 \times \frac{750}{50} = 3.75 \times 10^5$ V. Transmitting 500 MW at this voltage needs $I = \frac{500 \times 10^6}{3.75 \times 10^5} = 1330$ A. Along 225 km of cable with resistance $0.20\;\Omega\,\text{km}^{-1}$, $R = 45\;\Omega$, so the power lost is $1333^2 \times 45 = 8.0 \times 10^7$ W, 16% of the power sent. At 25 kV the current would be 15 times larger and the loss 225 times larger, more than the power transmitted, which is why high voltages are essential.
