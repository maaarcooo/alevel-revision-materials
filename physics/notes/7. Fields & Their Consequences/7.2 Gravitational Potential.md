# 7.2 Gravitational Potential

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.2.3):** definition of gravitational potential, including zero value at infinity; gravitational potential difference; work done in moving mass m given by ΔW = mΔV; equipotential surfaces; no work done when moving along an equipotential surface; V = −GM/r in a radial field; significance of the negative sign; graphs of the variation of g and V with r; V related to g by g = −ΔV/Δr; ΔV from the area under a graph of g against r.

## Gravitational Potential

Near the Earth's surface, the change in gravitational potential energy of a lifted object is $\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h$, taking the surface as zero. This only works where $g$ is effectively constant. Over large distances from a planet $g$ changes, so a more general measure is needed.

**Gravitational potential** $V$ at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point. Its unit is $\text{J kg}^{-1}$.

It is the gravitational potential energy per unit mass at that point, so a mass $m$ at a point of potential $V$ has gravitational potential energy:

$$E_p = mV$$

Gravitational potential is a **scalar**, unlike gravitational field strength, which is a vector.

### Why Gravitational Potential Is Negative

- Gravitational potential is defined as **zero at infinity**.
- Gravity is attractive, so work must be done **on** a mass to move it out to infinity, against the field.
- The mass therefore has **less** potential energy at any closer point than at infinity, so its potential energy, and the potential there, is negative.

Equivalently, as a mass falls in from infinity the field does work on it, and its potential energy falls below zero.

Gravitational potential is greatest, at zero, at infinity. Moving away from a planet it **increases**, becoming less negative, towards zero. Moving towards a planet it decreases, becoming more negative.

> **Exam tip:** The definition of gravitational potential and the reason it is negative are very common questions. The definition needs "work done per unit mass", "from infinity" and "to that point".

## Potential in a Radial Field

At distance $r$ from the centre of a point mass, or outside a uniform sphere, of mass $M$:

$$V = -\frac{GM}{r}$$

where $G$ = the gravitational constant and $r$ = distance from the centre of the mass (m). The potential energy of a mass $m$ there is:

$$E_p = mV = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

**Inline example:** A planet of diameter 7600 km has mass $3.5 \times 10^{23}$ kg. 400 km above its surface, $r = 3800 + 400 = 4200$ km $= 4.2 \times 10^6$ m, so $V = -\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 3.5 \times 10^{23}}{4.2 \times 10^6} = -5.6 \times 10^6\;\text{J kg}^{-1}$. Keep the minus sign when giving a potential, and measure $r$ from the planet's centre.

## Gravitational Potential Difference and Work Done

Two points at different distances from a mass have different potentials. The **gravitational potential difference** between them is:

$$\Delta V = V_f - V_i$$

The work done in moving a mass $m$ through a gravitational potential difference $\Delta V$ is:

$$\Delta W = m\Delta V$$

The work done on a mass against a gravitational field equals its gain in gravitational potential energy. Work is done against the field when a mass moves away from a planet, against the direction of the field lines.

For a mass $m$ moving from $r_1$ to $r_2$ from the centre of a mass $M$:

$$\Delta V = -\frac{GM}{r_2} - \left(-\frac{GM}{r_1}\right) = GM\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)$$

$$\Delta E_p = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)$$

This is positive when $r_2 > r_1$, so moving away from the mass increases the potential energy.

$\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h$ can only be used when $g$ is effectively constant, for small height changes in a uniform field near a planet's surface. The general equation does not contain $g$, which falls off as $\frac{1}{r^2}$ away from a planet.

When a question asks for a "change in" potential or potential energy, calculate final minus initial consistently. A negative answer means the quantity decreased. Often only the magnitude is needed.

**Inline example:** A 300 kg spacecraft rises from the surface of Mars (radius 3400 km, mass $6.40 \times 10^{23}$ kg) to an altitude of 700 km, so $r_1 = 3.4 \times 10^6$ m and $r_2 = 4.1 \times 10^6$ m. The work done against gravity is $\Delta E_p = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.40 \times 10^{23} \times 300 \times \left(\frac{1}{3.4 \times 10^6} - \frac{1}{4.1 \times 10^6}\right) = 6.4 \times 10^8$ J.

## Equipotential Surfaces

An **equipotential surface** is a surface on which the potential is the same at every point. In two dimensions these are equipotential lines.

- **No work is done** moving a mass along an equipotential surface, since $\Delta V = 0$ and so $\Delta W = m\Delta V = 0$. Work is only done moving between equipotentials.
- Equipotentials are always **perpendicular** to the field lines.
- They are drawn as dashed lines with **no arrows**, because potential is a scalar and has no direction. Field lines are solid with arrows.

| Field | Equipotentials |
|---|---|
| Radial field around a spherical mass | Concentric spheres centred on the mass (concentric circles in 2D). For equal steps of potential, they get **further apart** with distance |
| Uniform field near the Earth's surface | Horizontal, parallel planes, **equally spaced** for equal steps of potential |

In a radial field the equipotentials spread out because the field weakens with distance, so a greater distance must be moved to give the same change in potential.

## Graphs of $V$ and $g$ Against $r$

The field strength is related to the potential gradient by:

$$g = -\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}$$

The minus sign shows that the field points in the direction of **decreasing** potential, towards the mass. The magnitude of $g$ is the steepness of the potential gradient.

**Inline example:** The gravitational potential rises by $2.0 \times 10^4\;\text{J kg}^{-1}$ over a radial distance of 10 km, so $g = \frac{2.0 \times 10^4}{1.0 \times 10^4} = 2.0\;\text{N kg}^{-1}$.

| Graph (outside a planet) | Shape | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| $V$ against $r$ | Negative values, rising gently towards zero along a $-\frac{1}{r}$ curve | $g$ at a point is the **negative of the gradient** of the tangent there |
| $g$ against $r$ | Positive values, falling steeply along a $\frac{1}{r^2}$ inverse-square curve | The **area under the graph** between two distances is the magnitude of the potential difference $\Delta V$ between them |

The area under a plotted $g$-$r$ curve can be estimated by counting squares, or by splitting it into trapeziums and adding their areas.

When sketching, make the $g$-$r$ curve start steeper and fall faster than the $V$-$r$ curve, to show the inverse-square relationship rather than $\frac{1}{r}$. Doubling $r$ quarters $g$ but only halves $V$.

### Between Two Planets

Along the line between two planets, the potential is negative everywhere. Starting from the surface of the larger planet A, it rises to a maximum, still below zero, and then falls again towards the surface of the smaller planet B. The maximum is where the resultant field strength is zero, since the gradient of the $V$-$r$ graph is zero there. It lies **closer to the less massive planet**, because a smaller mass needs a shorter distance to produce a field strength equal to that of the larger one.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gravitational potential | Work done per unit mass in bringing a test mass from infinity to the point, in $\text{J kg}^{-1}$ |
| $V = -\frac{GM}{r}$ | Potential in a radial field, zero at infinity and negative elsewhere |
| $E_p = mV = -\frac{GMm}{r}$ | Gravitational potential energy of mass $m$ |
| $\Delta V = V_f - V_i$ | Gravitational potential difference |
| $\Delta W = m\Delta V$ | Work done moving a mass through a potential difference |
| $\Delta E_p = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)$ | Change in potential energy from $r_1$ to $r_2$ |
| Equipotential surface | Surface of constant potential, perpendicular to field lines, no work done moving along it |
| $g = -\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}$ | Field strength as the negative potential gradient |
| Area under $g$-$r$ graph | Magnitude of $\Delta V$ |
