# 7.3 Orbits of Planets & Satellites

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.2.4):** orbital period and speed related to the radius of a circular orbit; derivation of T² ∝ r³; energy considerations for an orbiting satellite; total energy of an orbiting satellite; escape velocity; synchronous orbits; use of satellites in low orbits and geostationary orbits, including the plane and radius of a geostationary orbit.

## Circular Orbits

Most planets and satellites follow nearly circular orbits. For a satellite of mass $m$ orbiting a body of mass $M$ at radius $r$ with speed $v$, the **gravitational force provides the centripetal force**. It acts towards the centre of the orbit, perpendicular to the satellite's velocity.

Although the speed is constant, the direction of motion keeps changing, so the satellite has a centripetal acceleration (see 6.1).

### Orbital Speed

Equating the gravitational force to the centripetal force:

$$\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

The satellite's mass $m$ cancels, leaving:

$$v^2 = \frac{GM}{r} \quad\text{so}\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$

- Every satellite in a given orbit travels at the same speed, whatever its mass.
- Orbital speed **decreases** as the orbital radius increases, $v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}$.

**Inline example:** A satellite orbits a planet of mass $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg at radius $7.0 \times 10^6$ m. Its speed is $v = \sqrt{\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24}}{7.0 \times 10^6}} = 7.6 \times 10^3\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

### Deriving $T^2 \propto r^3$

1. Equate gravitational and centripetal force and cancel $m$: $v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$.
2. In one period $T$ the satellite travels the circumference $2\pi r$, so $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}$ and $v^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}$.
3. Substitute: $\frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{r}$.
4. Rearrange:

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$$

Since $\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}$ is constant for one central body, $T^2 \propto r^3$. This is **Kepler's third law**: for planets or satellites in circular orbits about the same central body, the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbital radius.

The derivation is examined, so learn each step and the reason for it.

**Inline example:** A satellite orbits a planet of mass $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg at radius $4.2 \times 10^7$ m. Its period is $T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 \times (4.2 \times 10^7)^3}{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24}}} = 8.5 \times 10^4$ s, close to one day.

### Graphs of $T$ Against $r$

For orbits around one central body:

- A graph of $T^2$ against $r^3$ is a straight line through the origin, with gradient $\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}$.
- Taking logs, $\log T = \frac{3}{2}\log r + \frac{1}{2}\log\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}$, so a graph of $\log T$ against $\log r$ is a straight line with **gradient 1.5**. For the planets of the Solar System, with $T$ in years and $r$ in astronomical units, it is a straight line that does not pass through the origin.

**Inline example (comparing orbits):** A low Earth orbit 250 km up has period 89 min. The Earth's radius is $6.37 \times 10^6$ m, so its orbital radius is $6.62 \times 10^6$ m. A geostationary orbit has period 1440 min, so by Kepler's third law its radius is $6.62 \times 10^6 \times \left(\frac{1440}{89}\right)^{2/3} = 4.2 \times 10^7$ m, a height of about 36 000 km.

## Energy of an Orbiting Satellite

A satellite in a circular orbit has kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy. In a given circular orbit its speed and distance from the centre do not change, so **both stay constant**, and so does its total energy.

Using $v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$:

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{GMm}{2r}$$

$$E_p = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

$$E_{\text{total}} = E_k + E_p = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$$

The total energy is negative because the satellite is bound to the planet. The kinetic energy is half the magnitude of the potential energy.

### Moving to a Different Orbit

| Quantity | Smaller orbit (closer) | Larger orbit (further out) |
|---|---|---|
| Gravitational force, $F \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$ | Larger | Smaller |
| Speed | Higher | Lower |
| Kinetic energy | Higher | Lower |
| Potential energy | Lower (more negative) | Higher (less negative) |
| Total energy | Lower (more negative) | Higher (less negative) |
| Period | Shorter | Longer |

When a satellite moves to a larger orbit, its total energy increases because the gain in potential energy is twice the loss in kinetic energy. Energy must be supplied, by the rocket engine, to raise an orbit.

A way to remember the direction: closer to the planet the gravitational pull is stronger, so the satellite must orbit faster, giving it more kinetic energy, while its potential energy is lower.

**Inline example:** Two satellites of equal mass orbit at radii $R$ and $3R$. The inner one has more kinetic energy, less potential energy, a shorter period and a higher speed. The statement "the outer one has a longer period and travels faster" is wrong, because larger orbits are slower.

## Escape Velocity

**Escape velocity** is the minimum speed an object needs to escape a gravitational field from a given point, with no further energy input. "Escape" means reaching infinity, beyond the reach of the field, not just leaving the planet's surface.

At escape velocity, the object's kinetic energy at launch equals the energy needed to move it from $r$ to infinity, where its potential energy is zero:

$$\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{GMm}{r}$$

The object's mass cancels:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$

This equation is not on the data and formulae sheet, so learn its derivation.

- Escape velocity is the same for every object in the same field: a rocket and a tennis ball need the same speed to escape from the Earth.
- The escape velocity from a planet's surface depends on the planet's **mass and radius**.
- Rockets do not need to reach escape velocity to get into orbit. Their engines supply energy continuously through thrust, and less energy is needed to reach orbit than to escape the field entirely.

> **Exam tip:** In the definition, refer to the gravitational **field**, and include "minimum speed" and "no further energy input". Avoid vague words such as "gravity" or "the pull of the planet".

**Inline example:** For a planet of mass $6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg and radius $6.4 \times 10^6$ m, $v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24}}{6.4 \times 10^6}} = 1.1 \times 10^4\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

**Inline example (from density):** The Moon has mass $7.35 \times 10^{22}$ kg and density $3340\;\text{kg m}^{-3}$. Its volume is $\frac{M}{\rho} = 2.20 \times 10^{19}\;\text{m}^3$, so from $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$ its radius is $R = 1.74 \times 10^6$ m. The escape velocity from its surface is $\sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 7.35 \times 10^{22}}{1.74 \times 10^6}} = 2.4 \times 10^3\;\text{m s}^{-1}$.

## Synchronous and Geostationary Orbits

A **synchronous orbit** is an orbit whose period equals the rotational period, the length of a day, of the body being orbited. The satellite has the same angular speed as the body. A synchronous orbit can be in any plane, and the satellite need not stay above one point. An Earth-centred synchronous orbit, with a period of 24 hours, is called geosynchronous, and it may be inclined to the equator.

A **geostationary orbit** is an orbit above the equator with a period of 24 hours in the direction of the Earth's rotation, so the satellite stays above one point on the surface. Its key features:

- Period of **24 hours**.
- In the **plane of the equator**.
- Orbits from **west to east**, the same direction as the Earth's rotation.
- Radius of about $4.2 \times 10^7$ m from the centre of the Earth, which is about 36 000 km above the surface.

The radius follows from $r^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}$ with $T = 24 \times 3600$ s and $M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg: $r^3 = 7.5 \times 10^{22}\;\text{m}^3$, so $r = 4.2 \times 10^7$ m.

**Uses:** geostationary satellites are used for **telecommunications and TV broadcasting**. A ground station sends a signal up to the satellite, which amplifies it and transmits it back down to a wide area. Because the satellite stays at the same point in the sky, receiver dishes can be fixed in position.

## Low Orbits

Satellites in **low orbits** are much closer to the Earth's surface than geostationary satellites. They travel faster and have much shorter periods, around 90 minutes. A **polar orbit** is a low orbit in which the satellite passes over the north and south poles, so as the Earth turns beneath it the satellite passes over the whole surface.

Low orbits are used for high-quality imaging of the Earth's surface, such as weather monitoring, scientific observation of remote places and military applications. Being close to the surface, they need less powerful transmitters. They can be used for communications, but each satellite is only above a region briefly, so many satellites must work together to give continuous coverage.
