# 7.4 Electric Fields

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.1, 3.7.3.1 and 3.7.3.2):** similarities and differences between gravitational and electrostatic forces; force between point charges in a vacuum F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²); permittivity of free space ε₀; air can be treated as a vacuum; the charge of a charged sphere may be considered to be at its centre; comparison of the magnitudes of gravitational and electrostatic forces between subatomic particles; representation of electric fields by field lines; E as force per unit charge, E = F/Q; E = V/d in a uniform field and its derivation from Fd = QΔV; trajectory of a charged particle entering a uniform field at right angles; E = Q/(4πε₀r²) in a radial field.

## Coulomb's Law

Every charged particle produces an electric field, which exerts a force on other charges nearby. **Coulomb's law** states that the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation:

$$F = \frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$$

where $F$ = electrostatic force (N), $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ = the charges (C), $r$ = the separation of their **centres** (m) and $\varepsilon_0$ = the **permittivity of free space**, $8.85 \times 10^{-12}\;\text{F m}^{-1}$ (on the data sheet). The constant $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$ is about $8.99 \times 10^9\;\text{N m}^2\,\text{C}^{-2}$, but it is not given, so calculate it from $\varepsilon_0$.

Coulomb's law for charges is analogous to Newton's law of gravitation for masses: both are inverse square laws.

- **Like charges** repel. The product $Q_1Q_2$ is positive, so a **positive** $F$ means repulsion.
- **Opposite charges** attract. The product is negative, so a **negative** $F$ means attraction.

### Approximations

- Charges are assumed to be in a vacuum unless stated otherwise. **Air can be treated as a vacuum**, so $\varepsilon_0$ is used for charges in air. Every other material has a larger permittivity.
- For a point outside a **uniformly charged sphere**, the charge can be treated as a **point charge at its centre**. The field lines around a charged sphere are radial, outwards from a positive sphere and inwards to a negative one, identical to those of a point charge.
- Coulomb's law applies to point charges, so it can be used for charged spheres only when $r$ is measured from their centres. It cannot be used for charge spread over irregularly shaped objects.

**Inline example:** Two charges of $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ C, 0.10 m apart in a vacuum, repel with $F = \frac{(1.0 \times 10^{-6})^2}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.10^2} = 0.90$ N.

**Inline example:** An alpha particle (charge $2e$) is 2.0 mm from a gold nucleus (charge $79e$). The force between them is $F = \frac{2 \times 79 \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3})^2} = 9.1 \times 10^{-21}$ N.

### Electrostatic Versus Gravitational Force Between Particles

Between subatomic particles the electrostatic force is enormously larger than the gravitational force, because their masses are tiny while their charges are comparatively large. For two protons $2.0 \times 10^{-12}$ m apart:

- Gravitational: $F = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times (1.67 \times 10^{-27})^2}{(2.0 \times 10^{-12})^2} = 4.65 \times 10^{-41}$ N
- Electrostatic: $F = \frac{(1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times (2.0 \times 10^{-12})^2} = 5.75 \times 10^{-5}$ N

The electrostatic force is about $10^{36}$ times larger, so the gravitational force between charged particles is negligible. Since both forces follow the inverse square law, the ratio is the same at any separation.

## Electric Field Strength

An **electric field** is a region of space in which a charged object experiences a force.

**Electric field strength** at a point is the force per unit charge experienced by a small positive test charge placed at that point:

$$E = \frac{F}{Q}$$

Its unit is $\text{N C}^{-1}$, equivalent to $\text{V m}^{-1}$. Electric field strength is a **vector**.

Specifying a **positive** test charge sets the convention for the field's direction:

- A positive charge feels a force $EQ$ **in the direction** of the field.
- A negative charge feels a force $EQ$ **opposite** to the direction of the field.

So electric fields point away from positive charges and towards negative charges.

**Inline example:** A 2.0 μC charge feels a force of 0.050 N, so $E = \frac{0.050}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.5 \times 10^4\;\text{N C}^{-1}$. A particle feeling 0.30 N where $E = 3.5 \times 10^4\;\text{N C}^{-1}$ carries $Q = \frac{F}{E} = 8.6 \times 10^{-6}$ C.

## Electric Field Lines

Field lines show both the **direction** and the **magnitude** of an electric field:

- The arrows show the direction of the force on a positive charge, so the lines run **from positive to negative**.
- **Closer** lines mean a **stronger** field.
- Lines never cross, and they start or end on the surface of a charge or a plate, meeting it at right angles.

| Arrangement | Field pattern |
|---|---|
| Positive point charge | Radial, pointing outwards. The lines spread out, so the field weakens with distance |
| Negative point charge | Radial, pointing inwards |
| Charged conducting sphere | The same as a point charge at its centre, outside the sphere. Inside a conducting sphere the field is zero |
| Two opposite charges | Lines run from the positive charge to the negative charge, connecting them, which shows attraction |
| Two like charges | Lines do not connect the two charges, which shows repulsion. For two equal charges there is a **neutral point** at the midpoint, where the resultant field is zero |
| Two oppositely charged parallel plates | **Uniform** between the plates: parallel, equally spaced lines from the positive plate to the negative plate. Beyond the edges the field is weaker and non-uniform |
| Point charge near a charged plate | Like the field between two opposite charges, with the lines meeting the plate at right angles |

In a **uniform** field the field strength is the same in magnitude and direction at every point, so a test charge feels the same force everywhere. In a **radial** field the field strength decreases with distance.

## Uniform Fields Between Parallel Plates

The electric field strength between two parallel plates with a potential difference $V$ across them is:

$$E = \frac{V}{d}$$

where $V$ = potential difference between the plates (V) and $d$ = separation of the plates (m).

- A greater PD gives a stronger field. A greater separation gives a weaker field.
- The field points from the positive plate to the negative plate.
- An **earthed** plate is at 0 V.
- $E = \frac{V}{d}$ only applies to the uniform field between parallel plates. It cannot be used for the radial field around a point charge.

### Derivation

When a charge $Q$ moves from one plate to the other, a distance $d$, the field does work $W = Fd$ on it. The work done moving a charge through a potential difference is also $W = Q\Delta V$. Equating:

$$Fd = Q\Delta V \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{F}{Q} = \frac{\Delta V}{d}$$

and $\frac{F}{Q}$ is the field strength $E$, so $E = \frac{V}{d}$.

**Inline example:** Plates 0.020 m apart with a PD of 500 V have $E = \frac{500}{0.020} = 2.5 \times 10^4\;\text{V m}^{-1}$. With plates 3.5 cm apart at 7.9 kV, a charge of $2.6 \times 10^{-15}$ C feels $F = \frac{QV}{d} = \frac{2.6 \times 10^{-15} \times 7900}{0.035} = 5.9 \times 10^{-10}$ N.

## Charged Particles in a Uniform Field

A charged particle **starting at rest** in a uniform field accelerates in a straight line parallel to the field lines: along the field if positive, against it if negative.

A charged particle that **enters the field at right angles** to the field lines, for example by passing between charged plates, follows a **parabola**. It feels a constant force in a fixed direction perpendicular to its initial velocity, so, like a projectile, its velocity component along the field increases steadily while its component across the field stays constant.

- A **positive** particle is deflected towards the **negative** plate.
- A **negative** particle is deflected towards the **positive** plate.
- An uncharged particle, such as a neutron, feels no force and passes straight through undeflected.

The deflection depends on the particle's acceleration, $a = \frac{EQ}{m}$, and on how long it spends between the plates:

| Property | Effect of increasing it on the deflection |
|---|---|
| Mass | Decreases (smaller acceleration) |
| Charge | Increases (larger force) |
| Speed | Decreases (less time in the field) |

A smaller deflection means a gentler curve, so the particle hits the plate further along, or leaves the plates without hitting them.

**Inline example:** A boron-11 nucleus enters the same field as a proton with the same velocity. Its charge is 5 times larger, so the force on it is 5 times larger, but its mass is 11 times larger, so its acceleration is only $\frac{5}{11}$ of the proton's. It curves the same way but is deflected less.

## Radial Fields of Point Charges

Combining $E = \frac{F}{q}$ with Coulomb's law for a small test charge $q$ at distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$:

$$E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$$

The field strength of a point charge follows an **inverse square law**, $E \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$, and is not constant.

For a charged conducting sphere of radius $R$, a graph of $E$ against $r$ is zero inside the sphere, jumps to its greatest value at the surface, and then falls along an inverse-square curve outside.

**Inline example:** 0.30 m from a charge of $1.0 \times 10^{-9}$ C, $E = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-9}}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.30^2} = 1.0 \times 10^2\;\text{N C}^{-1}$.

**Inline example:** A metal sphere of diameter 15 cm has a field strength of $1.5 \times 10^5\;\text{V m}^{-1}$ at its surface. Treating it as a point charge at its centre, with $r = 7.5 \times 10^{-2}$ m, its charge is $Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 E r^2 = 4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.5 \times 10^5 \times (7.5 \times 10^{-2})^2 = 9.4 \times 10^{-8}$ C.

### Combining Fields

Electric field strength is a vector, so the resultant field at a point due to several charges is found by **vector addition** of the individual fields. Each field points away from a positive charge and towards a negative one, as felt by a positive test charge at the point.

- Fields along the same line: add them if they point the same way, subtract if they point in opposite directions.
- Perpendicular fields $E_1$ and $E_2$: the resultant has magnitude $\sqrt{E_1^2 + E_2^2}$. For example, perpendicular fields of 3.0 and $4.0\;\text{kN C}^{-1}$ give $5.0\;\text{kN C}^{-1}$.

## Gravitational and Electric Fields Compared

| | Gravitational field | Electric field |
|---|---|---|
| Acts on | Mass | Charge |
| Nature of the force | Always attractive | Attractive or repulsive |
| Force law | $F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$, inverse square | $F = \frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$, inverse square |
| Field strength | Force per unit mass, $g = \frac{F}{m}$ | Force per unit positive charge, $E = \frac{F}{Q}$ |
| Radial field strength | $g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$ | $E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$ |
| Radial potential (7.2 and 7.5) | $V = -\frac{GM}{r}$, always negative | $V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$, positive or negative |
| Field lines around a point source | Radially inwards, like a negative charge | Outwards from a positive charge, inwards to a negative one |
| Uniform field | Parallel, equally spaced lines | Parallel, equally spaced lines |
| Equipotentials | Spheres around a point mass, parallel planes in a uniform field | Spheres around a point charge, parallel planes in a uniform field |
| Work done | $\Delta W = m\Delta V$ | $\Delta W = Q\Delta V$ |
| Relative strength between particles | Very weak | About $10^{36}$ times stronger between two protons |

The main similarity is that both are inverse-square fields with many common features: field lines, potential, equipotential surfaces and $\frac{1}{r}$ potentials. The main difference is that masses always attract, while charges may attract or repel.
