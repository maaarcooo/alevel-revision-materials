# 7.5 Electric Potential

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.3.3):** definition of absolute electric potential, including zero value at infinity, and of electric potential difference; work done in moving charge Q given by ΔW = QΔV; equipotential surfaces; no work done moving charge along an equipotential surface; V = Q/(4πε₀r) in a radial field; graphs of the variation of E and V with r; V related to E by E = ΔV/Δr; ΔV from the area under a graph of E against r.

## Electric Potential

The **electric potential** at a point is the work done per unit charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to that point. Its unit is the volt, equivalent to $\text{J C}^{-1}$.

- Electric potential is a **scalar**, but it carries a sign.
- Like gravitational potential, it is **zero at infinity**.
- The potential at a point near a point charge depends on the size of the charge and the distance from it.

It is the electric potential energy per unit charge at that point:

$$V = \frac{E_p}{Q}$$

### Positive and Negative Charges

| Around an isolated | Sign of potential | Moving a positive test charge closer |
|---|---|---|
| Positive charge | Positive | The potential **increases**. Work must be done to overcome the repulsion |
| Negative charge | Negative | The potential **decreases**. The attraction does work on the test charge, releasing energy |

Electric potential always **decreases in the direction of the field lines**, the direction a positive test charge would be pushed. This is a reliable way to decide whether the potential rises or falls.

Unlike gravitational potential, which is always negative because masses always attract, electric potential takes the sign of the charge producing it.

## Potential in a Radial Field

At distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$:

$$V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$

where $V$ = electric potential (V), $Q$ = the charge producing the potential, with its sign (C), and $r$ = distance from the centre of the charge (m). So $V \propto \frac{1}{r}$.

For a charged conducting sphere, the potential outside it is the same as for a point charge at its centre, so $r$ is measured from the centre. Inside a conducting sphere the field is zero, so the potential is **constant**, equal to its value at the surface.

### Combining Potentials

Potential is a scalar, so the total potential at a point due to several charges is found by **adding** the potentials due to each, including their signs:

$$V = \frac{Q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_1} + \frac{Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_2}$$

This is simpler than combining fields, which needs vector addition. For example, a point equidistant from two equal positive charges has twice the potential due to either one. On the perpendicular bisector of two **equal and opposite** charges, the two potentials cancel, so the potential is **0 V** all along it.

**Inline example:** A Van de Graaff dome of radius 15 cm is charged to 240 kV. Its charge is $Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 rV = 4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.15 \times 240 \times 10^3 = 4.0\;\mu\text{C}$. At 30 cm from the dome's surface, $r = 0.45$ m from the centre, so $V = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.45} = 8.0 \times 10^4$ V.

## Potential Difference and Work Done

Two points at different distances from a charge have different potentials. The **electric potential difference** between them is:

$$\Delta V = V_f - V_i$$

For a point charge $Q$, between distances $r_i$ and $r_f$:

$$\Delta V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r_f} - \frac{1}{r_i}\right)$$

The work done moving a charge $Q$ through a potential difference $\Delta V$ is:

$$\Delta W = Q\Delta V$$

This work equals the change in the charge's electric potential energy. An external force must do work on a **positive** charge to move it **against** the field lines, and on a **negative** charge to move it **with** the field lines.

> **Exam tip:** A question may give two charges: the one producing the potential and the one being moved. In $\Delta W = Q\Delta V$, $Q$ is the charge being **moved**. The other charge goes into $\Delta V$.

**Inline example:** Moving a 2.0 μC charge through 50 V takes $\Delta W = 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 50 = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ J.

**Inline example:** Points R and S are 220 mm and 150 mm from a +7.0 nC charge. Between them, $\Delta V = \frac{7.0 \times 10^{-9}}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12}}\left(\frac{1}{0.15} - \frac{1}{0.22}\right) = 134$ V. Moving a +3.0 nC charge from R to S, towards the positive charge, takes $W = 3.0 \times 10^{-9} \times 134 = 4.0 \times 10^{-7}$ J.

### Electric Potential Energy of Two Charges

The electric potential energy of two point charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ separated by $r$ is:

$$E_p = \frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$

with the charges including their signs. The potential energy is zero at infinite separation, and also wherever $V = 0$.

| Pair | Sign of $E_p$ | As the separation increases |
|---|---|---|
| Like charges | Positive | $E_p$ **decreases** towards zero, since they repel |
| Opposite charges | Negative | $E_p$ **increases**, becoming less negative, since they attract |

The force between the charges is the negative gradient of a graph of $E_p$ against $r$.

**Inline example:** Two +1.0 μC charges 0.10 m apart have $E_p = \frac{(1.0 \times 10^{-6})^2}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.10} = 0.090$ J.

## Potential Gradient

The **potential gradient** is the rate of change of electric potential with respect to displacement in the direction of the field. Its unit is $\text{V m}^{-1}$. The electric field strength equals the potential gradient in magnitude:

$$E = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}$$

The field points in the direction of **decreasing** potential, which is why the relationship is sometimes written $E = -\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}$. Between parallel plates this gives $E = \frac{V}{d}$.

**Inline example:** Where the potential changes by 200 V over 0.040 m, $E = \frac{200}{0.040} = 5000\;\text{V m}^{-1}$.

## Graphs of $V$ and $E$ Against $r$

| Graph (outside a point charge) | Shape | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| $V$ against $r$ | A $\frac{1}{r}$ curve: positive values falling towards zero for a positive charge, negative values rising towards zero for a negative charge | The **gradient** at a point gives the magnitude of $E$ there |
| $E$ against $r$ | A $\frac{1}{r^2}$ inverse-square curve, steeper than the $V$-$r$ curve | The **area** under the graph between two distances is the potential difference between them |

$E$ falls off more rapidly with distance than $V$, because $E \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$ while $V \propto \frac{1}{r}$.

For a charged conducting sphere, $V$ is constant from the centre to the surface, then falls as $\frac{1}{r}$. $E$ is zero inside and falls as $\frac{1}{r^2}$ outside.

## Equipotential Surfaces

An **equipotential surface** joins points at the same electric potential.

- **No work is done** moving a charge along an equipotential, since $\Delta V = 0$.
- Equipotentials meet field lines at **90°**.
- They are drawn as dashed lines with no arrows, since potential is a scalar.
- For equal steps of potential, the **spacing** of the equipotentials shows the field strength: **closer** spacing means a **stronger** field, because the potential gradient is steeper. Equally spaced equipotentials mean a uniform field.

| Field | Equipotentials |
|---|---|
| Point charge, or outside a charged sphere | Concentric circles (spheres in 3D) centred on the charge, becoming further apart with distance |
| Uniform field between parallel plates | Straight lines parallel to the plates, equally spaced |
| Two equal and opposite charges | Closed curves around each charge, with a straight 0 V line along the perpendicular bisector |
| Two like charges | Closed curves around each charge. Further out, curves enclose both charges. The point of zero resultant field lies between them, at the midpoint for equal charges and closer to the smaller charge for unequal ones |

To draw equipotentials for several charges, sketch curves that cross every field line at right angles. Near each charge they are almost circles. Further away, they surround the whole group.

**Inline example:** A diagram shows field lines pointing outwards from two charges, with more lines from the left charge and a point of zero field closer to the right charge. Both charges are positive, since the lines point away from them, and the left one is larger, since it has more field lines and the neutral point is further from it.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electric potential | Work done per unit charge bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to the point, in V |
| $V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ | Potential in a radial field, taking the sign of $Q$ |
| $V = \frac{E_p}{Q}$ | Potential as potential energy per unit charge |
| $\Delta V = V_f - V_i$ | Electric potential difference |
| $\Delta W = Q\Delta V$ | Work done moving charge $Q$ through $\Delta V$ |
| $E_p = \frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ | Electric potential energy of two point charges |
| Potential gradient | Rate of change of potential with displacement in the field's direction |
| $E = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}$ | Field strength from potential gradient, field towards lower potential |
| Area under $E$-$r$ graph | Potential difference |
| Equipotential surface | Points at the same potential, perpendicular to field lines, no work done along it |
