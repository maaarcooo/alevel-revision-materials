## Capacitors and Capacitance

A **capacitor** stores charge, and with it energy in the electric field between its plates. A parallel-plate capacitor is two parallel conducting plates separated by an insulator. The circuit symbol is two short parallel lines of equal length, with a wire from each.

The insulating material between the plates is the **dielectric**. It stops charge flowing directly between the plates and, as explained below, increases the capacitance.

When a capacitor is connected to a supply, charge $+Q$ builds up on one plate and an equal charge $-Q$ on the other, producing a uniform electric field between them.

**Capacitance** is the charge stored per unit potential difference between the plates:

$$C = \frac{Q}{V}$$

where $C$ = capacitance (F), $Q$ = charge on each plate (C) and $V$ = PD across the capacitor (V).

- The unit is the **farad** (F), one coulomb per volt. One farad is a very large capacitance, so values are usually quoted in μF, nF or pF.
- $Q$ is the magnitude of the charge on **each plate**. The capacitor as a whole stores no net charge, since its plates carry equal and opposite charges.
- For a given capacitor, $Q$ is directly proportional to $V$, so a graph of charge against PD is a straight line through the origin with gradient $C$.
- Take care: $C$ is the symbol for capacitance, but C is also the unit of charge, the coulomb.

**Inline example:** A capacitor storing 30 μC at a PD of 6.0 V has $C = \frac{30 \times 10^{-6}}{6.0} = 5.0\;\mu\text{F}$. A 1.0 nF capacitor connected to 0.30 kV stores $Q = CV = 1.0 \times 10^{-9} \times 300 = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}$ C.

## Permittivity and the Parallel-Plate Capacitor

**Permittivity** $\varepsilon$ describes how a material affects the electric field set up in it. Its unit is $\text{F m}^{-1}$.

**Relative permittivity** $\varepsilon_r$ is the ratio of the permittivity of a material to the permittivity of free space:

$$\varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Relative permittivity is also called the **dielectric constant**. It has **no units**, being a ratio of two quantities with the same unit. For air it is approximately 1, the same as a vacuum.

**Inline example:** A material with permittivity $1.77 \times 10^{-11}\;\text{F m}^{-1}$ has $\varepsilon_r = \frac{1.77 \times 10^{-11}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 2.0$.

The capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor is:

$$C = \frac{A\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{d}$$

where $A$ = area of overlap of the plates (m²), $d$ = plate separation (m) and $\varepsilon_r$ = relative permittivity of the dielectric.

- $C \propto A$: capacitance is directly proportional to the area of overlap. $A$ is the area of **one** plate, not of both plates added together.
- $C \propto \frac{1}{d}$: capacitance is inversely proportional to the separation.
- Filling the gap of a vacuum (or air) capacitor with a dielectric multiplies its capacitance by the dielectric's $\varepsilon_r$.
- Square plates of side $L$ have $A = L^2$.

**Inline example:** Plates of area $0.010\;\text{m}^2$, 0.20 mm apart, with a dielectric of $\varepsilon_r = 4.0$, give $C = \frac{0.010 \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 4.0}{0.20 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.77 \times 10^{-9}$ F.

**Inline example:** Capacitor 1 has square plates of side $L$, separation $d$ and a dielectric. Capacitor 2 has plates of side $3L$, separation $3d$ and air between them. Their capacitances are equal: $\frac{L^2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{d} = \frac{9L^2\varepsilon_0}{3d}$, so $\varepsilon_r = 3$.

## Dielectric Action

A dielectric is made of **polar molecules**: molecules with a positive end and a negative end.

- When the capacitor is **uncharged**, there is no field between the plates and the polar molecules point in **random** directions.
- When the capacitor is **charged**, the field between the plates exerts forces on the ends of each molecule. The molecules **rotate to align with the field**, negative ends towards the positive plate and positive ends towards the negative plate.

The aligned molecules each produce their own electric field, which **opposes** the field from the charges on the plates. For the same charge on the plates, the overall field between them is reduced, so the PD between the plates ($V = Ed$) is smaller. Since $C = \frac{Q}{V}$, a smaller PD for the same charge means a **larger capacitance**.

The larger the opposing field the molecules produce, the larger the material's relative permittivity.

## Energy Stored by a Capacitor

While a capacitor charges, the power supply pushes electrons onto one plate and pulls them off the other, so it does **work** on the electrons. This work is stored as electrical potential energy in the capacitor.

At first adding electrons to the negative plate is easy, as there is little charge to repel them. As charge builds up, the electrons already on the plate **repel** each new electron more strongly, so more work must be done to add each one. In other words, the PD rises as the charge increases.

The **area under a graph of charge against PD** is the energy stored. Since $Q \propto V$ the graph is a straight line through the origin, and the area is a triangle:

$$E = \frac{1}{2}QV$$

The energy is less than $QV$ because the PD rises from zero to $V$ as the charge is added, so on average each coulomb is moved through only $\frac{V}{2}$.

Substituting $Q = CV$ or $V = \frac{Q}{C}$:

$$E = \frac{1}{2}QV = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C}$$

All three forms are on the data sheet. Choose the one that matches the quantities given. For a fixed PD, the energy stored is directly proportional to the capacitance.

**Inline example:** A capacitor holding 4.0 mC at 50 V stores $E = \frac{1}{2} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 50 = 0.10$ J. A 100 μF capacitor at 20 V stores $\frac{1}{2} \times 100 \times 10^{-6} \times 20^2 = 0.020$ J. A 2.0 μF capacitor holding 6.0 μC stores $\frac{(6.0 \times 10^{-6})^2}{2 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 9.0 \times 10^{-6}$ J.

**Inline example (change in energy):** When the PD across a 1500 μF capacitor rises from 10 V to 30 V, the energy stored increases by $\frac{1}{2}C(V_2^2 - V_1^2) = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \times 10^{-6} \times (30^2 - 10^2) = 0.60$ J.

**Inline example (from a graph):** A graph of potential against charge for a charged metal sphere shows 1.8 μC at 100 kV. The energy stored is the area of the triangle, $\frac{1}{2} \times 1.8 \times 10^{-6} \times 100 \times 10^3 = 0.090$ J.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capacitance | Charge stored per unit PD, $C = \frac{Q}{V}$, in farads |
| Dielectric | Insulating material between the plates of a capacitor |
| Relative permittivity (dielectric constant) | $\varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}$, no units |
| $C = \frac{A\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{d}$ | Capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor |
| Polar molecule | Molecule with a positive end and a negative end, which rotates to align with a field |
