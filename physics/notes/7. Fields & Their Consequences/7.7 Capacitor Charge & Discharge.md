# 7.7 Capacitor Charge & Discharge

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.4.4):** graphs of Q, V and I against time for charging and discharging capacitors through resistors; interpretation of gradients and areas under graphs; time constant RC, including its determination from graphical data; time to halve, T½ = 0.69RC; quantitative treatment of discharge, Q = Q₀e^(−t/RC), and the corresponding equations for V and I; quantitative treatment of charging, Q = Q₀(1 − e^(−t/RC)); required practical 9 (investigation of the charge and discharge of capacitors, using log-linear plotting to find the time constant RC).

## Charging a Capacitor

A capacitor is charged by connecting it in series with a resistor to a power supply.

- Electrons are pulled off the plate connected to the **positive** terminal, leaving it positively charged.
- They travel around the circuit and are pushed onto the plate connected to the **negative** terminal, making it negatively charged. No charge crosses the dielectric.
- An equal and opposite charge builds up on the two plates, so a PD develops across the capacitor.

At first the current is at its maximum, $I_0 = \frac{V_0}{R}$, since the capacitor has no PD to oppose the supply. As charge builds up, the electrons already on the negative plate **repel** incoming electrons, so fewer are pushed on each second and the current falls. Equivalently, the PD across the capacitor rises, leaving less PD across the resistor. Charging stops when the PD across the capacitor equals the supply PD.

| Quantity while charging | Graph against time |
|---|---|
| Current $I$ | Starts at its maximum and decreases **exponentially** towards zero |
| Charge $Q$ | Rises from zero, quickly at first then more slowly, towards its maximum $Q_0 = CV_0$ |
| PD $V$ | The same shape as the charge graph, rising towards the supply PD $V_0$ |

The charge-time and PD-time graphs for charging are identical in shape, since $Q = CV$.

**Exponential decay** means the rate of decrease of a quantity is proportional to the amount of the quantity remaining.

## Discharging a Capacitor

A charged capacitor is discharged through a resistor, with no power supply in the circuit. A two-way switch often connects the capacitor either to the supply (charging) or across the resistor alone (discharging).

During discharge, electrons flow from the negative plate back round the circuit to the positive plate, until there is no PD between the plates. The current is in the **opposite direction** to the charging current. It starts large and falls to zero.

The current, PD and charge **all decrease exponentially** from their initial values $I_0$, $V_0$ and $Q_0$, with identically shaped graphs. The current is directly proportional to both the PD across the capacitor ($I = \frac{V}{R}$) and the charge on it.

A larger resistance means a smaller current, so charge flows off the plates more slowly and the capacitor takes longer to discharge.

## Gradients and Areas

Since $\Delta Q = I\Delta t$:

| Feature | What it gives |
|---|---|
| **Area** under a current-time graph | The charge that flows onto or off the plates in that time interval |
| **Gradient** of a charge-time graph, $\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}$ | The current at that time |

On a curved charge-time graph, find the current at a given time by drawing a tangent there and calculating its gradient.

## The Time Constant

The **time constant** of a capacitor-resistor circuit is:

$$\tau = RC$$

where $R$ = resistance (Ω) and $C$ = capacitance (F). Its unit is the **second**.

- For **discharge**, it is the time for the charge, current or PD to fall to $\frac{1}{e}$, about **37%**, of its **initial** value.
- For **charging**, it is the time for the charge or PD to rise to $1 - \frac{1}{e}$, about **63%**, of its **maximum** (final) value.

Check whether a question is about charging or discharging, since the definition depends on it.

A smaller time constant means a quicker decay, and a larger one a slower decay. After about $5RC$ a capacitor is effectively fully charged or discharged.

**Finding the time constant from a graph:** on a discharge graph, read off the time at which the PD has fallen to $0.37V_0$. On a charging graph, read off the time at which it has risen to $0.63V_0$.

**Inline example:** A 2 μF capacitor discharging through a 5 kΩ resistor has $RC = 5000 \times 2 \times 10^{-6} = 0.01$ s. A 7.0 nF capacitor with a time constant of $5.6 \times 10^{-3}$ s is discharging through $R = \frac{\tau}{C} = \frac{5.6 \times 10^{-3}}{7.0 \times 10^{-9}} = 8.0 \times 10^5\;\Omega$.

### Time to Halve

The **time to halve**, $T_{1/2}$, is the time for the charge, current or PD of a discharging capacitor to fall to half its initial value. Putting $Q = \frac{1}{2}Q_0$ into the discharge equation:

$$\frac{1}{2} = e^{-\frac{T_{1/2}}{RC}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad T_{1/2} = RC\ln 2$$

$$T_{1/2} = 0.69RC$$

Because the decay is exponential, the time to halve is **independent of the starting value**: however much charge there is, it always takes the same time to halve.

**Inline example:** With $RC = 10$ s, $T_{1/2} = 0.69 \times 10 = 6.9$ s. A 600 nF capacitor discharging through 100 kΩ has $T_{1/2} = 0.69 \times 600 \times 10^{-9} \times 100 \times 10^3 = 0.041$ s.

## Discharge Equations

For a capacitor discharging through a resistor:

$$Q = Q_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}} \qquad V = V_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}} \qquad I = I_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$$

where $Q_0$, $V_0$ and $I_0$ are the **initial** values and $e \approx 2.718$. Only the $Q$ equation is on the data sheet, so remember that $V$ and $I$ follow the same form.

Putting $t = RC$ gives $Q = Q_0e^{-1} = 0.37Q_0$, which is where the 37% in the time constant's definition comes from.

To find a time, take natural logs. The inverse of $e^x$ is $\ln$: if $e^x = y$, then $x = \ln y$. For example, $\frac{I}{I_0} = e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$ gives:

$$t = -RC\ln\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)$$

**Inline example:** A capacitor with initial charge 50 μC discharges with $RC = 4$ s. After 4 s, $Q = 50e^{-1} = 18\;\mu\text{C}$.

**Inline example:** A 620 μF capacitor discharges through 450 Ω with an initial current of 0.60 A. The current falls to 0.40 A after $t = -450 \times 620 \times 10^{-6} \times \ln\left(\frac{0.40}{0.60}\right) = 0.11$ s.

## Charging Equations

For a capacitor charging through a resistor:

$$Q = Q_0\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}\right) \qquad V = V_0\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}\right)$$

Here $Q_0$ and $V_0$ are the **final** (maximum) charge and PD, not the initial values. The charging current follows the same form as for discharge, $I = I_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$, since it also decays exponentially.

Putting $t = RC$ gives $Q = Q_0(1 - e^{-1}) = 0.63Q_0$.

**Inline example:** A capacitor charges towards 12 V with $RC = 2$ s. After 2 s, $V = 12(1 - e^{-1}) = 7.6$ V.

**Inline example:** A capacitor charges towards 12 V with $RC = 0.50$ s. Rearranging, $e^{-\frac{t}{RC}} = 1 - \frac{V}{V_0}$, so it reaches 10 V after $t = -RC\ln\left(1 - \frac{V}{V_0}\right) = -0.50 \times \ln\left(1 - \frac{10}{12}\right) = 0.90$ s.

## Required Practical 9: Charging and Discharging Capacitors

**Aim:** investigate the discharge of a capacitor through a resistor and determine the time constant $RC$, and from it the capacitance.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Time, $t$ |
| Dependent | PD across the capacitor, $V$ |
| Control | The resistance, the capacitor, the starting PD from the supply |

**Method:**

1. Set up a circuit with a supply (for example 10 V), a two-way switch, the capacitor and a resistor (for example 10 kΩ), with a voltmeter **in parallel** across the capacitor. Start with the capacitor fully discharged.
2. Switch the capacitor to the supply until the voltmeter reads the supply PD, so it is fully charged.
3. Move the switch so the capacitor is connected across the resistor only, starting the discharge and a stopwatch together.
4. Record the PD at regular intervals, for example every 10 s, taking 8 to 10 readings.

The same circuit can be used to study charging, recording the PD as it rises.

**Analysis:** taking natural logs of $V = V_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$:

$$\ln V = -\frac{1}{RC}t + \ln V_0$$

| Straight-line term | Quantity |
|---|---|
| $y$ | $\ln V$ |
| $x$ | $t$ |
| Gradient | $-\frac{1}{RC}$ |
| $y$-intercept | $\ln V_0$ |

Plot $\ln V$ against $t$ and draw a line of best fit. A straight line confirms exponential decay. Then $RC = -\frac{1}{\text{gradient}}$, and dividing by $R$ gives $C$. Compare it with the labelled capacitance using the percentage difference.

**Inline example:** For discharge through 10 kΩ, a $\ln V$ against $t$ graph has gradient $-0.020\;\text{s}^{-1}$. Then $RC = \frac{1}{0.020} = 50$ s and $C = \frac{50}{10\,000} = 5.0 \times 10^{-3}$ F.

**Improving the experiment:**

- Use a large resistance and capacitance, so the capacitor discharges slowly enough for readings to be timed accurately.
- A datalogger removes the reaction-time error in reading the PD at set times.
- With a digital voltmeter, record the value once the reading settles. With an analogue meter, read at eye level to avoid parallax, and check for a zero error before starting.

**Safety:** a resistor of too low a resistance gives a large current, so the wires become very hot. A capacitor can retain charge after the power is removed and give a shock, so discharge it fully before handling.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exponential decay | Rate of decrease proportional to the amount remaining |
| Area under $I$-$t$ graph | Charge that has flowed |
| Gradient of $Q$-$t$ graph | Current |
| Time constant, $\tau = RC$ | Time to fall to 37% of the initial value (discharge) or rise to 63% of the final value (charging) |
| $T_{1/2} = 0.69RC$ | Time to halve, independent of the starting value |
| $Q = Q_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$, $V = V_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$, $I = I_0e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$ | Discharge, from initial values |
| $Q = Q_0\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}\right)$, $V = V_0\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}\right)$ | Charging, towards final values |
| $\ln V = -\frac{t}{RC} + \ln V_0$ | Log-linear form for required practical 9, gradient $-\frac{1}{RC}$ |
