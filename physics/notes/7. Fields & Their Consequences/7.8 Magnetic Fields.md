# 7.8 Magnetic Fields

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.5.1 and 3.7.5.2):** force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field, F = BIl, when the field is perpendicular to the current; Fleming's left-hand rule; magnetic flux density B and the definition of the tesla; required practical 10 (how the force on a wire varies with flux density, current and length of wire, using a top pan balance); force on charged particles moving in a magnetic field, F = BQv, when the field is perpendicular to the velocity; direction of force on positive and negative particles; circular path of particles and its application in devices such as the cyclotron.

## Magnetic Fields

A **magnetic field** is a region in which a magnet, a current-carrying conductor or a moving charge experiences a force.

Magnetic fields are produced by **moving electric charges**, such as a current in a wire, and by **permanent magnets**. A **stationary** charge produces no magnetic field.

### Field Lines

Magnetic field lines (flux lines) show the direction and strength of the field:

- Outside a magnet, field lines point **from the north pole to the south pole**: out of a north pole and into a south pole.
- The direction of a field line at a point is the direction of the force a free north pole would experience there.
- Lines **closer together** show a **stronger** field. Around a bar magnet the field is strongest at the poles.
- Field lines **never cross**.
- The field around a long, straight current-carrying wire is a set of concentric circles centred on the wire.

When a field points into or out of the page, it is drawn as a grid of symbols:

| Symbol | Field direction |
|---|---|
| Dot, or circled dot | **Out of** the page (the tip of an arrow coming towards you) |
| Cross | **Into** the page (the tail feathers of an arrow going away) |

## Magnetic Flux Density

**Magnetic flux density** $B$ is the force per unit current per unit length on a current-carrying conductor at right angles to the field. It measures the strength of the field, and in a field-line diagram it corresponds to how closely packed the lines are. Its unit is the **tesla** (T).

One **tesla** is the magnetic flux density that causes a force of 1 N on a 1 m length of wire carrying a current of 1 A at right angles to the field.

## Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A current-carrying conductor produces its own magnetic field, which interacts with an external field, so the conductor feels a force. For a conductor perpendicular to the field:

$$F = BIl$$

where $F$ = force (N), $B$ = magnetic flux density (T), $I$ = current (A) and $l$ = length of the conductor **within the field** (m).

The force can be increased by increasing the flux density, the current or the length of conductor in the field.

The force depends on the angle between the conductor and the field. Only the component of the current perpendicular to the field produces a force:

- The force is **greatest** when the conductor is at 90° to the field, giving $F = BIl$.
- The force is **zero** when the conductor is parallel to the field.
- At an angle $\theta$ to the field, $F = BIl\sin\theta$.

A copper rod resting on two rails in a uniform magnetic field shows this: when a current passes through the rod, it feels a force and accelerates along the rails.

**Inline example:** A 0.50 m wire carrying 3.0 A perpendicular to a 0.20 T field feels $F = 0.20 \times 3.0 \times 0.50 = 0.30$ N. A 1.4 m wire carrying 0.87 A at 30° to an 80 mT field feels $F = 80 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.87 \times 1.4 \times \sin 30° = 0.049$ N.

### Fleming's Left-Hand Rule

When a conductor is perpendicular to the field, the force, the field and the current are **mutually perpendicular**. **Fleming's left-hand rule** gives the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor or moving charge. Hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of the left hand at right angles to each other:

| Digit | Represents |
|---|---|
| **Th**umb | **Th**rust: the force, or motion |
| **F**irst finger | **F**ield, from north to south |
| Se**c**ond finger | **C**onventional current, from positive to negative |

Point two digits along the known directions to find the third. Conventional current flows opposite to the electron flow.

**Inline example:** A horizontal wire lies in a field directed into the page and feels a downward force. With the first finger into the page and the thumb down, the second finger points left, so the current flows from right to left.

## Force on a Moving Charge

A charge moving through a magnetic field feels a force, which is why a current-carrying wire, containing moving electrons, feels one. For a charge moving perpendicular to the field:

$$F = BQv$$

where $Q$ = charge (C) and $v$ = speed (m s⁻¹). A charge moving **parallel** to the field feels **no** magnetic force. At an angle $\theta$ to the field, $F = BQv\sin\theta$.

Use $F = BIl$ for a current-carrying conductor, and $F = BQv$ for an individual moving charge, including one inside a conductor.

**Inline example:** A charge of $3.2 \times 10^{-19}$ C moving at $2.0 \times 10^6\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to a 0.50 T field feels $F = 0.50 \times 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^6 = 3.2 \times 10^{-13}$ N.

### Direction of the Force

The force is **perpendicular to both** the velocity and the field. Use Fleming's left-hand rule with the second finger along the direction of conventional current:

- For a **positive** charge, the second finger points **along** its velocity.
- For a **negative** charge, such as an electron, the second finger points **opposite** to its velocity.

So a negative particle feels a force in the opposite direction to a positive one moving the same way in the same field.

**Inline example:** A positive charge moves from left to right through a field directed into the page. First finger into the page, second finger to the right: the thumb points **upwards**. An electron moving to the left through the same field also feels an upward force, since its conventional current is to the right.

## Circular Paths in a Magnetic Field

A charged particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field follows a **circle**. The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it acts as a centripetal force towards a fixed centre. The centripetal acceleration is towards the centre, the same direction as the magnetic force.

Because the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, it does **no work**. The particle's **speed stays constant**, and only its direction changes.

Equating the magnetic force to the centripetal force:

$$BQv = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

$$r = \frac{mv}{BQ}$$

| Increase in | Effect on radius |
|---|---|
| Speed $v$ | Larger, $r \propto v$ |
| Mass $m$ | Larger, $r \propto m$ |
| Charge $Q$ | Smaller, $r \propto \frac{1}{Q}$ |
| Flux density $B$ | Smaller, $r \propto \frac{1}{B}$ |

The equations of circular motion are in the circular motion section of the data sheet, not the magnetic fields section.

**Inline example:** A particle of mass $2.0 \times 10^{-26}$ kg and charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at $4.0 \times 10^5\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to a 0.50 T field. Its path has radius $r = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-26} \times 4.0 \times 10^5}{0.50 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 0.10$ m.

**Inline example:** An electron moves at $3.0 \times 10^6\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to a 6.2 mT field. Using the specific charge $\frac{e}{m_e} = 1.76 \times 10^{11}\;\text{C kg}^{-1}$, $r = \frac{v}{(e/m_e)B} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^6}{1.76 \times 10^{11} \times 6.2 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.7$ mm.

## The Cyclotron

A **cyclotron** is a particle accelerator that uses a magnetic field to bend charged particles, such as protons, into a spiral path while accelerating them to very high speeds. Cyclotrons produce radioactive tracers for medical imaging and high-energy beams for radiotherapy.

A cyclotron has three main parts:

- Two hollow semicircular electrodes called **dees**, with a gap between them.
- A strong **uniform magnetic field** perpendicular to the dees.
- An **alternating electric field** across the gap between the dees.

**How it works:**

1. Charged particles are released from a source at the **centre**.
2. Inside a dee there is no electric field. The magnetic field makes the particles move in a **semicircle** at constant speed.
3. At the gap, the electric field **accelerates** the particles across into the other dee. Each crossing gives a particle of charge $Q$ an energy $QV$, where $V$ is the PD across the gap.
4. The particles enter the next dee faster, so they follow a semicircle of **larger radius**, since $r \propto v$.
5. The electric field **reverses** each time the particles reach the gap, so they are always accelerated towards the opposite dee.
6. The particles spiral outwards until they leave at the edge with the desired speed.

**Roles of the fields:**

| Field | Role |
|---|---|
| Uniform magnetic field | Supplies the centripetal force that keeps the particles moving in circular paths. It does no work, so it cannot speed them up |
| Alternating electric field | Accelerates the particles across the gap between the dees |

**Constant timing.** Each half-circle takes the same time even though the particles speed up. The radius increases in proportion to the speed, so the time for a half-circle, $\frac{\pi r}{v} = \frac{\pi m}{BQ}$, does not depend on speed. This lets the electric field alternate at a fixed frequency.

## Required Practical 10: Force on a Current-Carrying Wire

**Aim:** investigate how the force on a current-carrying wire varies with the current (and with length and flux density), and so find the flux density of a pair of magnets.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Current, $I$ |
| Dependent | Top-pan balance reading (mass, $m$) |
| Control | Length of wire in the field, flux density (same magnets) |

**Method:**

1. Place a magnet holder, with a pair of magnets facing each other, on a top-pan balance. Clamp a stiff wire horizontally between the magnets, **perpendicular** to the field, so the wire does not touch the magnets.
2. Measure the length of the magnets with a ruler. This is the length $l$ of wire in the field.
3. With no current flowing, **zero the balance**, so it shows only the change caused by the magnetic force.
4. Connect the wire in series with a power supply, a variable resistor and an ammeter. Set the current to 0.5 A and record the balance reading.
5. Increase the current in 0.5 A steps for 8 to 10 readings, keeping it below about 6 A. Repeat each set at least three times and take a mean mass for each current.

The magnetic force pushes the wire one way. By **Newton's third law** the magnets feel an equal and opposite force, which changes the balance reading.

**Analysis:** the force is $F = mg$, with $m$ in kg. Equating to $F = BIl$:

$$m = \frac{Bl}{g}I$$

A graph of mass against current is a straight line through the origin with gradient $\frac{Bl}{g}$, so:

$$B = \frac{g \times \text{gradient}}{l}$$

Varying the length (using different magnet arrangements) or the flux density (different magnets) at constant current shows the force is proportional to each.

**Inline example:** A line of best fit for mass against current has gradient $2.13 \times 10^{-4}\;\text{kg A}^{-1}$, and the wire's length in the field is 0.050 m. Then $B = \frac{9.81 \times 2.13 \times 10^{-4}}{0.050} = 0.042$ T, or 42 mT.

**Errors and safety:**

- Zeroing the balance avoids a zero error.
- Avoid high currents: the wire heats up, its resistance rises, and the current drifts during a reading.
- Do not touch the wire while current is flowing, since it can become hot.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Magnetic field | Region where a magnet, current-carrying conductor or moving charge feels a force |
| Magnetic flux density | Force per unit current per unit length on a conductor at right angles to the field, in teslas |
| Tesla | Flux density giving 1 N on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field |
| $F = BIl$ | Force on a conductor perpendicular to the field, $l$ in the field |
| $F = BQv$ | Force on a charge moving perpendicular to the field |
| Fleming's left-hand rule | Thumb = force, first finger = field, second finger = conventional current |
| $r = \frac{mv}{BQ}$ | Radius of a charged particle's circular path |
| Cyclotron | Magnetic field bends particles into semicircles, alternating electric field accelerates them across the gap |
| $B = \frac{g \times \text{gradient}}{l}$ | Flux density from a graph of mass against current (required practical 10) |
