# 7.9 Electromagnetic Induction

**Specification (AQA 7408 3.7.5.3 and 3.7.5.4):** magnetic flux Φ = BA where B is normal to A; flux linkage NΦ, where N is the number of turns cutting the flux; flux linkage through a rectangular coil rotated in a magnetic field, NΦ = BAN cos θ; required practical 11 (using a search coil and oscilloscope, the effect on flux linkage of varying the angle between a search coil and the field); simple experimental phenomena of induction; Faraday's and Lenz's laws; induced EMF = rate of change of flux linkage, ε = NΔΦ/Δt; applications such as a straight conductor moving in a magnetic field; EMF induced in a coil rotating uniformly in a magnetic field, ε = BANω sin ωt.

## Magnetic Flux

**Magnetic flux** $\Phi$ is the product of the magnetic flux density and the area perpendicular to the field:

$$\Phi = BA$$

where $\Phi$ = magnetic flux (Wb), $B$ = magnetic flux density (T) and $A$ = area perpendicular to the field (m²). It can be pictured as the total number of field lines passing through the area.

The unit of flux is the **weber** (Wb), where $1\;\text{Wb} = 1\;\text{T m}^2$.

**Inline example:** An area of $0.050\;\text{m}^2$ perpendicular to a 0.20 T field has flux $\Phi = 0.20 \times 0.050 = 0.010$ Wb.

## Magnetic Flux Linkage

**Magnetic flux linkage** is the product of the magnetic flux through a coil and its number of turns, $N\Phi$. Its unit is the **weber turn** (Wb turns). A coil with more turns has more flux linkage, and so a larger EMF is induced when the flux changes.

### Coil at an Angle to the Field

When the field is not perpendicular to the coil, only the component of $B$ along the normal to the coil's plane contributes:

$$N\Phi = BAN\cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the field lines and the **normal** to the plane of the coil. Drawing the normal on a diagram helps to find the right angle.

| Orientation of the coil | $\theta$ | Flux through it |
|---|---|---|
| Plane **perpendicular** to the field lines | 0° | Maximum, $BA$ per turn |
| Plane **parallel** to the field lines | 90° | Zero |

**Inline example:** A 100-turn coil of area $0.020\;\text{m}^2$ in a 0.50 T field, with its normal at 60° to the field, has $N\Phi = 0.50 \times 0.020 \times 100 \times \cos 60° = 0.50$ Wb turns.

**Inline example:** A window frame of area $0.40 \times 0.73 = 0.292\;\text{m}^2$ faces the Earth's field of $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ T when closed, so the flux through it is $5.3 \times 10^{-6}$ Wb. As it opens through 180°, the flux falls to zero at 90° and returns to its maximum at 180°.

## Electromagnetic Induction

**Electromagnetic induction** is the process by which an EMF is induced in a conductor when the magnetic flux linkage through it changes. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, a current flows.

The flux linkage $N\Phi = BAN\cos\theta$ changes, and an EMF is induced, when there is a change in:

- the flux density $B$, for example a magnet moving towards a coil, or an alternating current in a nearby coil,
- the area $A$ in the field, for example a conductor moving across the field and cutting field lines,
- the angle $\theta$, for example a coil rotating in a field.

Electromagnetic induction is used in **generators**, which convert kinetic energy into electrical energy, and in **transformers**.

### A Magnet and a Coil

A coil connected to a sensitive meter, with a bar magnet:

- With the magnet **stationary**, inside or outside the coil, the meter reads zero, because the flux linkage is not changing.
- Pushing the magnet **in** induces an EMF, shown briefly on the meter.
- Pulling the magnet **out** induces an EMF in the **opposite direction**.
- The induced EMF is larger if the magnet moves **faster**, the coil has **more turns**, or the magnet is **stronger**, since each increases the rate of change of flux linkage.

### A Wire Between Magnets

A straight wire connected to a voltmeter and moved between the poles of two magnets cuts the field lines, so an EMF is induced. It is zero when the wire is still, reverses when the direction of motion reverses, and is larger for a longer wire in the field, a faster motion or stronger magnets.

## Faraday's and Lenz's Laws

**Faraday's law:** the magnitude of the induced EMF is equal to the rate of change of flux linkage:

$$\varepsilon = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$$

where $\varepsilon$ = induced EMF (V), $N\Delta\Phi$ = change in flux linkage (Wb turns) and $\Delta t$ = time taken (s). The **gradient** of a graph of flux linkage against time is the induced EMF.

**Lenz's law:** the induced EMF acts in a direction that produces effects opposing the change that causes it.

Combining the two, $\varepsilon = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$. The **minus sign represents Lenz's law**: it gives the direction of the induced EMF. Calculations usually ask only for the magnitude, but the meaning of the minus sign may be asked for.

Lenz's law follows from conservation of energy. If the induced current helped the change instead of opposing it, the motion would speed itself up and produce electrical energy from nothing.

**Inline example:** The flux through a 50-turn coil changes by $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb in 0.10 s, so $\varepsilon = 50 \times \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}{0.10} = 1.0$ V.

**Inline example:** A 350-turn coil of area $3.5\;\text{cm} \times 1.4\;\text{cm} = 4.9 \times 10^{-4}\;\text{m}^2$ lies with its plane parallel to a horizontal 80 mT field, so its flux linkage is zero. It is turned through 40° in 0.18 s, so the angle between its normal and the field becomes 50°. Its flux linkage is now $80 \times 10^{-3} \times 4.9 \times 10^{-4} \times 350 \times \cos 50° = 8.8 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb turns, and the average EMF is $\frac{8.8 \times 10^{-3}}{0.18} = 49$ mV.

### Demonstrating Lenz's Law

Lenz's law can be shown with a bar magnet, a coil of wire and a sensitive ammeter. No cell is needed.

- Pushing a **north pole** into the coil induces a current that makes the end of the coil nearest the magnet a **north pole**, which **repels** the magnet and opposes its approach.
- Pulling the north pole **out** induces a current in the opposite direction, making the nearest end a **south pole**, which **attracts** the magnet and opposes its departure.
- The right-hand grip rule gives the coil's poles from the current: with the fingers curled in the direction of the current, the thumb points along the field inside the coil, towards the coil's north pole.

So work must always be done to move the magnet, and this work is the source of the electrical energy.

**A falling magnet.** A magnet dropped through a coil connected in a complete circuit takes longer to fall than one dropped the same height outside it. As it approaches the coil, the induced current makes a like pole that repels it. As it leaves, the induced current makes an opposite pole that attracts it back. Both magnetic forces oppose its motion and slow it down.

## EMF Induced in a Moving Conductor

A straight conductor of length $l$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to a uniform field of flux density $B$ has an EMF induced across it. The EMF is greatest when the conductor moves perpendicular to the field lines.

**Derivation from Faraday's law:**

1. In time $\Delta t$ the conductor moves a distance $v\Delta t$.
2. It sweeps out an area $A = lv\Delta t$.
3. The flux it cuts is $\Delta\Phi = BA = Blv\Delta t$.
4. With $N = 1$, $\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = \frac{Blv\Delta t}{\Delta t}$.

$$\varepsilon = Blv$$

The EMF is larger for a longer conductor in the field, a stronger field, or a faster motion.

**Inline example:** A 0.25 m rod moving at $3.0\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to a 0.40 T field has $\varepsilon = 0.40 \times 0.25 \times 3.0 = 0.30$ V.

**Inline example:** An aircraft with a wingspan of 34.5 m flies at $253\;\text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to the Earth's field of 0.060 mT. Treating the wing as a straight conductor with $l$ = the wingspan, $\varepsilon = 0.060 \times 10^{-3} \times 34.5 \times 253 = 0.52$ V between the wing tips.

## EMF Induced in a Rotating Coil

A coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ rotating at constant angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform field has a flux linkage that varies with time. Timing from when its plane is perpendicular to the field, $\theta = \omega t$, so:

$$N\Phi = BAN\cos(\omega t)$$

The induced EMF is the rate of change of flux linkage:

$$\varepsilon = BAN\omega\sin(\omega t)$$

The EMF is **alternating** and varies sinusoidally, with peak value $\varepsilon_0 = BAN\omega$.

| Plane of the coil | Flux linkage | Induced EMF |
|---|---|---|
| **Perpendicular** to the field lines | Maximum | Zero |
| **Parallel** to the field lines | Zero | Maximum |

The EMF is greatest when the flux linkage is zero because at that orientation the sides of the coil cut the field lines fastest, so the flux linkage changes at its greatest rate. Where the flux linkage is greatest, its graph is momentarily flat, so the EMF is zero. The EMF and the flux linkage are therefore **90° out of phase**.

$\omega t$ is in radians, so use radian mode when evaluating $\sin(\omega t)$.

**Inline example:** A 200-turn coil of area $0.010\;\text{m}^2$ rotating at $50\;\text{rad s}^{-1}$ in a 0.10 T field has peak EMF $\varepsilon_0 = 0.10 \times 0.010 \times 200 \times 50 = 10$ V.

### Changing Fields from Alternating Currents

If a coil sits in the field of a second coil connected to an alternating supply, the field through the first coil alternates, so an alternating EMF is induced in it with the **same frequency** as the supply. This is how a transformer works (7.10).

## Required Practical 11: Flux Linkage and a Search Coil

**Aim:** find how the flux linkage through a search coil varies with the angle between the coil and the magnetic field.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Angle $\theta$ between the normal to the search coil and the field lines |
| Dependent | Peak induced EMF |
| Control | Area and number of turns of the search coil, peak flux density and frequency of the field |

**Method:**

1. Connect a slinky spring (a large coil) to a low-voltage 50 Hz a.c. supply. The alternating current produces a changing magnetic field, so an EMF is induced in a stationary search coil placed in it.
2. Connect the search coil to a cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO). Switch the time-base off, so the trace is a vertical line whose length shows the peak-to-peak voltage. Adjust the volts per division so the whole line fits on the screen.
3. Place the search coil halfway along the slinky, with its plane perpendicular to the field, so $\theta = 0°$.
4. Record the peak-to-peak voltage and halve it to get the peak EMF $\varepsilon_0$.
5. Rotate the coil in 10° steps up to 90°, measuring the angle with a protractor, and record the peak EMF each time.

**Analysis:** the slinky's flux density varies as $B = B_0\cos(\omega t)$, so the flux linkage through the search coil is $N\Phi = B_0AN\cos(\omega t)\cos\theta$. By Faraday's law the induced EMF is $B_0AN\omega\sin(\omega t)\cos\theta$, with peak value:

$$\varepsilon_0 = B_0AN\omega\cos\theta$$

A graph of $\varepsilon_0$ against $\cos\theta$ is a straight line through the origin with gradient $B_0AN\omega$. This confirms that the flux linkage, and so the peak EMF, is proportional to $\cos\theta$.

**Errors and improvements:**

- The field is not perfectly uniform, so some flux may still link the coil at 90°, giving a non-zero intercept at $\cos\theta = 0$.
- Read the protractor from directly above and from the same point each time to avoid parallax, and fix it in place.
- Take readings over a full 360° rotation for reliability, or rotate the coil with a calibrated motor in fixed increments.

**Safety:** the slinky heats up while current flows, especially if its wire is thin, so do not leave the supply on longer than necessary, and do not exceed the coil's rated current.

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Magnetic flux | $\Phi = BA$, with $B$ normal to $A$, in webers |
| Flux linkage | $N\Phi$, in Wb turns |
