## Rutherford Scattering

Rutherford, with Geiger and Marsden, fired alpha particles at a thin gold foil and measured the **angles** through which they were deflected and the **number** deflected at each angle.

### Apparatus

| Part | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Alpha source in a lead container with a narrow opening | Lead absorbs alpha particles, so only a narrow, **collimated** beam leaves the opening |
| Very thin gold foil | Each alpha particle is scattered by at most one nucleus, and is not absorbed. Gold can be hammered into very thin sheets |
| Evacuated chamber | Alpha particles travel only a few centimetres in air, so without a vacuum they would be absorbed or scattered by air molecules before reaching the foil |
| Movable detector | A fluorescent screen viewed through a microscope, moved around the foil to count the alpha particles arriving at each angle |

### Observations and Conclusions

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most alpha particles passed straight through undeflected | The atom is mostly **empty space** |
| Some were deflected through small angles | The atom's **positive charge is concentrated** in a central nucleus, which repels the positive alpha particles |
| A very small number were deflected through more than 90°, some straight back | The nucleus is **extremely small** and contains **most of the atom's mass** |

The number of alpha particles detected per unit time is greatest at small angles and falls rapidly as the deflection angle increases.

The atom is about $10^{-10}$ m across and the nucleus about $10^{-15}$ m, so the atom is roughly 100 000 times wider than its nucleus.

### Changing Models of the Atom

- **Thomson (1897)** discovered the electron and proposed the **plum pudding model**: a sphere of uniformly spread positive charge with electrons embedded in it, neutral overall.
- The plum pudding model predicted that alpha particles would pass through the foil with at most very small deflections. It could not explain the **large-angle** deflections Geiger and Marsden observed.
- Rutherford replaced it with the **nuclear model**: a small, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the mass, surrounded by electrons, with the atom mostly empty space.
- **Chadwick (1932)** discovered the neutron, completing the picture of the nucleus as protons and neutrons.

## Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation

An unstable nucleus becomes more stable by emitting radiation. The three main types are:

| | Alpha (α) | Beta-minus (β⁻) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus: 2 protons and 2 neutrons | High-energy electron from the nucleus | High-energy electromagnetic radiation (photons) from the nucleus |
| Notation | ${}^{4}_{2}\alpha$ | ${}^{\;\,0}_{-1}\beta$ | ${}^{0}_{0}\gamma$ |
| Charge | $+2e$ | $-e$ | 0 |
| Mass | $4u$ | about $\frac{1}{2000}u$ | 0 |
| Ionising | Most, about $10^4$ ion pairs per cm of air | Moderate, about 100 ion pairs per cm | Least, about 1 ion pair per cm |
| Penetrating | Least | Moderate | Most |
| Range in air | About 3 to 7 cm | About 20 cm to 3 m, depending on energy | No definite range: intensity follows an inverse-square law |
| Absorbed by | A sheet of paper | A few millimetres of aluminium | Intensity reduced by several centimetres of lead or several metres of concrete |

A beta-plus (β⁺) particle is a high-energy positron, ${}^{\;\,0}_{+1}\beta$. Nuclei emit gamma radiation to lose excess energy, usually after an alpha or beta decay leaves them excited.

### Ionisation

Radiation **ionises** an atom when it knocks out electrons, producing an ion pair. Ionising ability is measured by the number of ion pairs produced per cm of air. Ionisation causes chemical changes that damage living cells.

- Alpha is the most ionising because it has the largest charge, $+2e$, and moves relatively slowly, so it interacts strongly with the atoms it passes.
- Gamma photons have no charge, so gamma is the least ionising.
- Ionising ability and penetrating power are inversely related: the more ionising the radiation, the faster it loses its energy, so the shorter its range.

### Identifying Radiation by Absorption

1. Measure the background count with a GM tube and counter, with no source present.
2. Place the source close to the GM tube and measure the count rate.
3. Put a sheet of paper between source and tube. A large fall in count rate shows alpha.
4. Replace the paper with a few millimetres of aluminium. A large fall shows beta.
5. Replace the aluminium with several centimetres of lead. A large fall shows gamma.

### Deflection in Electric and Magnetic Fields

- Between charged plates, alpha particles are deflected towards the **negative** plate and beta-minus particles towards the **positive** plate.
- Gamma radiation is uncharged, so electric and magnetic fields do not deflect it.
- In a magnetic field, a charged particle moving perpendicular to the field follows a circular path of radius $r = \frac{mv}{BQ}$. Beta particles have a far smaller mass, and so a much smaller momentum per unit charge, than alpha particles. Their paths have a much smaller radius, so they are deflected much more.

## Applications

### Smoke Detectors

A smoke detector contains americium-241, an alpha emitter. Alpha particles ionise the air between two plates, so a small current flows. Smoke absorbs the alpha particles, the current falls and the alarm sounds.

- Alpha is safe inside a home because its short range means it does not reach the occupants.
- Americium-241 has a long half-life, so its activity hardly falls over the detector's lifetime and it need not be replaced.

### Thickness Monitoring

A source is placed on one side of a sheet as it is made and a detector on the other. If the count rate falls, the sheet is too thick, so the pressure of the rollers is increased. If the count rate rises, the rollers are eased apart.

| Material | Radiation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Paper, aluminium foil | Beta | Partly absorbed, so small changes in thickness change the count rate. Alpha would be completely absorbed, and gamma would pass through almost unabsorbed |
| Thick steel sheet | Gamma | Only gamma penetrates far enough |

The source needs a long half-life so it does not need replacing often.

### Medicine

- **Sterilising equipment:** gamma radiation, for example from cobalt-60, is penetrating enough to reach all sides of instruments without removing their sealed packaging. The equipment does not become radioactive, because gamma radiation does not affect the nuclei of its atoms, and only a change to a nucleus makes something radioactive.
- **Radiotherapy:** beams of gamma radiation are aimed at a tumour from many directions as the machine rotates around the patient, so the tumour receives a high dose while surrounding tissue receives much less. Parts of the body not being treated are covered with lead. Beta sources can treat skin cancer by direct application.
- **Tracers:** a radioisotope put into the body to monitor processes in it. A tracer should be a **gamma emitter**, because gamma escapes the body to an external detector and is weakly ionising. Short half-lives are preferred: the tracer decays quickly, so the patient's dose is lower, and its high initial activity means only a small sample is needed. Iodine-131 is taken up by the thyroid gland. Technetium-99m is covered in 8.3.

Every medical use balances a **risk** (ionising radiation damages healthy cells) against a **benefit** (diagnosis or treatment). The dose is kept as low as the purpose allows.

## The Inverse-Square Law for Gamma Radiation

Gamma radiation from a point source spreads out uniformly over a sphere whose area is proportional to $x^2$, so its intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance:

$$I = \frac{k}{x^2}$$

where $I$ is the intensity (W m⁻²), $x$ the distance from the source and $k$ a constant. The corrected count rate $C$ is proportional to intensity, so it follows the same law. Doubling the distance reduces the intensity to a quarter.

The law does not apply to alpha and beta radiation in air, because they are absorbed by the air within a short distance.

**Inline example:** a gamma source gives a corrected count rate of 200 s⁻¹ at 0.2 m. At 0.5 m, $C_2 = C_1\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right)^2 = 200 \times \left(\frac{0.2}{0.5}\right)^2 = 32\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

To test data against the law, check that $Cx^2$ is constant for at least three distances.

## Required Practical 12: The Inverse-Square Law for Gamma Radiation

**Aim:** to verify the inverse-square law for gamma radiation.

| Variable type | Variable |
|---|---|
| Independent | Distance $x$ between source and GM tube |
| Dependent | Count rate |
| Control | Same source, same GM tube, same counting time, same aluminium sheet |

**Method:**

1. With all sources removed from the room, record the background count several times and find the mean background count rate.
2. Place a 2 to 3 mm aluminium sheet in front of the GM tube. The source also emits alpha and beta, which the sheet absorbs so only gamma is counted.
3. Place the source at a measured distance from the GM tube and record the count over a fixed time, for example 60 s. Repeat three times and find the mean.
4. Repeat for a range of distances.
5. Subtract the background count rate from each reading to give the corrected count rate $C$.

**Analysis:** if $C = \frac{k}{x^2}$, then $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{k}}$. A graph of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}$ against $x$ is a straight line with gradient $\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}$, which verifies the law.

**Errors:**

- **Distance:** the radioactive material and the detecting region sit inside their casings, behind the points $x$ is measured from, so the measured distance is smaller than the true distance. The line then cuts the negative $x$-axis, and the size of the intercept is the systematic error in distance.
- **Dead time:** after each count, a GM tube cannot register another for about 100 μs, so recorded counts are lower than the true counts, most of all at short distances.
- **Alpha or beta reaching the detector** would make the graph curve slightly.
- **Randomness:** radioactive decay is random, so repeat readings and averages are essential. The percentage uncertainty in a count $N$ is proportional to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$, so counting for as long as possible gives a smaller percentage uncertainty.

## Background Radiation

**Background radiation** is the ionising radiation present in the environment at all times, from natural and artificial sources.

**Natural sources:**

- **Radon gas**, the largest source in the UK. It comes from the decay of uranium found naturally in rocks, soil and building materials such as stone and brick. Radon emits alpha particles and is inhaled, so the alpha particles are emitted inside the lungs.
- **Cosmic rays** from the Sun and from high-energy events in space such as supernovae.
- **Carbon-14** in all organic matter, and radioactive isotopes in food and drink.
- Rocks and soil containing naturally radioactive isotopes.

**Artificial sources:**

- **Medical uses**, such as X-rays and CT scans, the largest artificial source.
- **Nuclear waste**, and **fallout** from weapons tests and nuclear accidents such as Chernobyl.

### Correcting for Background

The **corrected count rate** is the count rate from a source with the background count rate subtracted.

1. Measure the background with a GM tube and counter, with all sources removed from the room, over a long time.
2. Measure the count rate with the source present.
3. Subtract.

**Inline example:** a GM tube records 24 counts per minute with no source and 285 counts per minute with a source. The corrected count rate is $285 - 24 = 261$ counts per minute.

## Safe Handling of Radioactive Sources

| Precaution | Reason |
|---|---|
| Store sources in lead-lined boxes when not in use | The lead absorbs the radiation |
| Handle sources with long tongs | Increases the distance, so the intensity received is smaller |
| Keep exposure time short | Less time exposed means a smaller dose |
| Never point the open end of a source at anyone, including yourself | Avoids exposing anyone to the beam |
| Monitor workers such as radiographers with detector badges | Records the dose each worker receives |

A much more active source, such as a hospital source, needs the same precautions on a larger scale: remote handling instead of tongs, thicker shielding, and keeping people out of the room.
