## The Nature of Radioactive Decay

**Radioactive decay** is the spontaneous disintegration of an unstable nucleus to form a more stable nucleus, emitting alpha, beta or gamma radiation.

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Random** | It is impossible to predict which nucleus will decay next, or when a particular nucleus will decay |
| **Spontaneous** | It is not affected by external conditions such as temperature or pressure |
| **Constant decay probability** | Every nucleus of the same isotope has the same probability of decaying per unit time, and this probability does not change over time |

Because each decay is random, only the behaviour of a **large** sample can be predicted: the **proportion** of nuclei expected to decay in a given time.

**Evidence for randomness:** a GM tube near a source gives a count rate that **fluctuates irregularly and unpredictably**. Each count is one decay, so the fluctuations show the decays happen at random.

## Decay Constant and Activity

The **decay constant** $\lambda$ is the probability that an individual nucleus will decay per unit time. Its unit is $\text{s}^{-1}$, or any inverse time unit such as $\text{year}^{-1}$.

The **activity** $A$ of a sample is the number of nuclei that decay per unit time. Its unit is the **becquerel** (Bq), where 1 Bq is 1 decay per second.

The rate of change of the number of undecayed nuclei $N$ is proportional to $N$:

$$\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t} = -\lambda N$$

The minus sign shows that the number of undecayed nuclei **decreases** with time. The size of this rate is the activity:

$$A = \lambda N$$

- A larger decay constant gives a larger activity for the same number of nuclei.
- The activity falls as the number of undecayed nuclei falls.

**Inline example:** a sample of $5.0 \times 10^{12}$ undecayed nuclei with $\lambda = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ has activity $A = \lambda N = 2.0 \times 10^{-5} \times 5.0 \times 10^{12} = 1.0 \times 10^{8}$ Bq.

## Exponential Decay

Because the rate of decay is proportional to the number of nuclei left, the number of undecayed nuclei falls **exponentially**, quickly at first and then more slowly, never reaching zero:

$$N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$

where $N_0$ is the number of undecayed nuclei at $t = 0$. Activity is proportional to $N$, and the background-corrected count rate $C$ is proportional to activity, so both follow the same law:

$$A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} \qquad C = C_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$

On a graph of $N$ against $t$, every curve starts at $N_0$ on the vertical axis, and a **larger** $\lambda$ gives a **steeper** curve.

**Inline example:** $1.0 \times 10^{6}$ undecayed nuclei with $\lambda = 0.10\ \text{s}^{-1}$. After 10 s, $N = 1.0 \times 10^{6} \times e^{-0.10 \times 10} = 1.0 \times 10^{6} \times e^{-1.0} = 3.7 \times 10^{5}$.

To find a time, take natural logs: $t = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{N}{N_0} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{N_0}{N}$. The decay constant and the time must be in matching units.

### Number of Nuclei from Mass

Questions often give a mass of a radioactive isotope. The number of nuclei is

$$N = \frac{\text{mass} \times N_A}{\text{molar mass}}$$

where $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ is the Avogadro constant. Use the same mass unit in the numerator as in the molar mass.

**Inline example:** 2.0 g of a nuclide with molar mass $200\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ contains $N = \frac{2.0 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}}{200} = 6.02 \times 10^{21}$ nuclei.

## Modelling Decay with Dice

Dice model a constant decay probability. Throw a large number of dice, remove those showing a chosen face, which have "decayed", and throw the rest again, recording the number left each time.

- Every die has the same, fixed chance of being removed on each throw, just as every nucleus has the same decay probability per unit time.
- For dice with $n$ faces, one face counting as decayed, the decay constant is $\lambda = \frac{1}{n}$ per throw.
- A graph of dice remaining against number of throws is an exponential decay curve, and its scatter shows the randomness.

**Inline example:** for six-sided dice, $\lambda = \frac{1}{6}$ per throw, so the half-life is $\frac{\ln 2}{1/6} \approx 4.2$ throws.

## Half-Life

**Half-life** $T_{1/2}$ is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei of an isotope, or its activity, to halve.

Setting $N = \frac{1}{2}N_0$ at $t = T_{1/2}$ in $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ gives $\frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}$. Taking natural logs gives $\ln 2 = \lambda T_{1/2}$, so

$$T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

Half-life is **inversely proportional** to the decay constant: the larger $\lambda$, the shorter the half-life and the faster the decay.

**Inline example:** an isotope with $\lambda = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ has $T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 693$ s.

After $n$ half-lives, the fraction of the original undecayed nuclei remaining is $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$. After 3 half-lives, $\frac{1}{8}$ remains.

### Half-Life from a Decay Curve

On a graph of activity (or $N$, or corrected count rate) against time, read off the time taken for the activity to fall from $A_0$ to $\frac{A_0}{2}$. For a more reliable value, read off several successive halvings, such as $\frac{A_0}{2}$ to $\frac{A_0}{4}$, and take the mean. Each half-life is the same length whatever the starting point.

### Half-Life from a Log Graph

Taking natural logs of $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ gives

$$\ln N = \ln N_0 - \lambda t$$

which matches $y = mx + c$. A graph of $\ln N$ against $t$ is a **straight line** with:

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Gradient | $-\lambda$ |
| $y$-intercept | $\ln N_0$ |

Find $\lambda$ from the gradient, then $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$. The same works for $\ln A$ or $\ln C$ against $t$. A straight line also confirms that the decay is exponential.

## Applications

Radioactive decay is used in nuclear power, in medicine (radiotherapy, tracers and sterilising equipment, covered in 8.1), in radioactive dating and in radioisotope power systems.

### Radiocarbon Dating

Carbon-14 is radioactive, with a half-life of about 5730 years, and is made continually in the atmosphere.

- A living organism continually takes in carbon, replacing the carbon-14 that decays, so the **proportion of carbon-14** in it stays **constant**.
- When the organism dies, it no longer takes in carbon, so decayed carbon-14 is not replaced and its activity **falls**.
- The age of a once-living sample is found by comparing its current carbon-14 activity with the initial value, estimated from the ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 in living material, and solving $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t$.

| Sample age | Why the method is unreliable |
|---|---|
| Only a few hundred years | Too little carbon-14 has decayed, so the fall in activity is too small to measure accurately |
| Tens of thousands of years | The activity is too low to distinguish from background radiation |

### Uranium-Lead Dating

Uranium-238, with a half-life of about 4.5 billion years, decays through a chain of decays to the stable isotope **lead-206**.

- It is assumed that **no lead-206 was present** when the rock formed, so all of it came from uranium-238 decay.
- The ratio of lead-206 atoms to uranium-238 atoms **increases** with time, so measuring it gives the age of the rock.
- Each lead-206 atom present corresponds to one uranium-238 atom that has decayed, so $N_0 = N_U + N_{Pb}$, and $t$ follows from $N_U = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$.

### Radioisotope Power Systems

A radioisotope power system converts the **heat** released by radioactive decay into **electrical power**. They power space probes and satellites, far from the Sun and from any chance of refuelling.

- The usual fuel is **plutonium-238**, an alpha emitter. Its alpha particles are absorbed within the fuel, releasing their energy as heat, and are easily shielded. Its half-life of about 88 years gives a steady supply over a long mission.
- The power output is proportional to the activity, so it **decreases exponentially**: $P = P_0 e^{-\lambda t}$.
- The power from decays is $P = A \times E$, where $E$ is the energy released per decay. The electrical power is this multiplied by the efficiency.

### Storage of Radioactive Waste

The time for which waste must be stored depends on its half-life and initial activity. Waste containing isotopes with long half-lives stays hazardous for thousands of years, so it must be stored securely for that long. Waste with a short half-life is very active at first but becomes safe much sooner. The storage time can be estimated from $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ as the time for the activity to fall to a safe level. How waste is stored is covered in 8.4.

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Radioactive decay | Spontaneous, random disintegration of an unstable nucleus to a more stable one |
| Decay constant $\lambda$ | Probability that an individual nucleus will decay per unit time ($\text{s}^{-1}$) |
| Activity $A$ | Number of nuclei that decay per unit time (Bq) |
| $\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t} = -\lambda N$ | Rate of decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei |
| $A = \lambda N$ | Activity from decay constant and number of undecayed nuclei |
| $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ | Number of undecayed nuclei after time $t$. Also $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ and $C = C_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ |
| $N = \frac{\text{mass} \times N_A}{\text{molar mass}}$ | Number of nuclei in a sample of known mass |
| Half-life $T_{1/2}$ | Time for the number of undecayed nuclei, or the activity, to halve |
| $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$ | Half-life from decay constant |
| $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$ | Fraction remaining after $n$ half-lives |
| $\ln N$ against $t$ | Straight line, gradient $-\lambda$, intercept $\ln N_0$ |
| Dice model | Constant probability $\lambda = \frac{1}{n}$ per throw for $n$-faced dice |
