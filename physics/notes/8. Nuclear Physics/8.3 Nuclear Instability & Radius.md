## The N-Z Graph for Stable Nuclei

Plotting neutron number $N$ (vertical) against proton number $Z$ for all stable nuclei gives a band called the **line of stability**.

- **Light nuclei** (up to about $Z = 20$) lie close to the line $N = Z$.
- **Heavier stable nuclei** have $N > Z$, and the ratio $\frac{N}{Z}$ increases as $Z$ increases. The line curves above $N = Z$.

**Why heavy nuclei need more neutrons:** electrostatic repulsion between protons acts across the whole nucleus and grows with $Z$. Extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding repulsion.

**Why very heavy nuclei are unstable:** the strong force is short-range, so each nucleon is attracted only by its neighbours, while the electrostatic repulsion between protons acts across the whole nucleus. Beyond a certain size the repulsion wins.

## Decay Modes

A nucleus is unstable if it has too many neutrons, too many protons, too many nucleons or too much energy. Each decay moves it towards the line of stability.

| Problem | Decay mode | Position on the N-Z graph | Change in $N$ | Change in $Z$ | Change in $A$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Too many neutrons | β⁻ | Above (to the left of) the line, neutron-rich | $-1$ | $+1$ | 0 |
| Too many protons | β⁺ or electron capture | Below (to the right of) the line, proton-rich | $+1$ | $-1$ | 0 |
| Too many nucleons | α | Heavy nuclei, mostly $Z > 82$, beyond the end of the line | $-2$ | $-2$ | $-4$ |
| Too much energy | γ | Any excited nucleus | 0 | 0 | 0 |

On an N-Z graph, β⁻ decay is an arrow one step down and one step right, β⁺ decay and electron capture one step up and one step left, and α decay two steps down and two steps left. Check whether the vertical axis is the neutron number $N$ or the nucleon number $A$, because some questions use $A$.

### Decay Equations

| Decay | General equation |
|---|---|
| α | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \to {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\text{Y} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha$ |
| β⁻ | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \to {}^{\;\;A}_{Z+1}\text{Y} + {}^{\;\,0}_{-1}\beta + \bar{\nu}_e$ |
| β⁺ | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \to {}^{\;\;A}_{Z-1}\text{Y} + {}^{\;\,0}_{+1}\beta + \nu_e$ |
| Electron capture | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} + {}^{\;\,0}_{-1}\text{e} \to {}^{\;\;A}_{Z-1}\text{Y} + \nu_e$ |

In each equation the nucleon numbers and the proton numbers on each side balance.

**Inline example:** ${}^{239}_{94}\text{Pu}$ decays by alpha emission to ${}^{235}_{92}\text{U}$. ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ decays by β⁻ emission to ${}^{14}_{7}\text{N}$.

In **electron capture**, the nucleus takes in one of its own inner orbiting electrons, which combines with a proton to form a neutron, and an electron neutrino is emitted. This leaves a vacancy in an inner shell. As outer electrons fall to fill it, the atom emits **X-rays**. If the nucleus is left in an excited state, it also emits **gamma rays**.

## Nuclear Excited States and Gamma Emission

Like an atom's electrons, a nucleus has discrete **energy levels**. After alpha or beta decay, the daughter nucleus is often left in an **excited state**. It drops to a lower level by emitting a **gamma photon**, which removes energy but changes neither $N$ nor $Z$.

An excited nucleus usually reaches its **ground state** very quickly, either directly or via one or more lower excited states, emitting a gamma photon at each step.

A **metastable state** is an excited state that lasts much longer than a typical excited state before emitting a gamma photon. It is shown by an "m" after the nucleon number.

### Nuclear Energy Level Diagrams

- Alpha or beta decay is shown as a **diagonal line** from the parent's level down to one of the daughter's levels.
- The daughter's excited states are drawn as horizontal lines above its ground state. Gamma emission is a **vertical** arrow between them.
- The maximum kinetic energy of a β⁻ particle in a decay to a given state is the **energy difference** between the parent level and that state.
- The energy of a gamma photon is the difference between the two levels, and its wavelength is $\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E}$. The **smallest** energy gap gives the **longest** wavelength.

**Inline example:** a parent level lies 2.0 MeV above the daughter's ground state, and the daughter has excited states at 0.80 MeV and 0.30 MeV. A β⁻ decay to the 0.80 MeV state gives the beta particle at most $2.0 - 0.80 = 1.2$ MeV. The possible gamma transitions are 0.80 to 0.30, 0.80 to 0 and 0.30 to 0 MeV, so three wavelengths. The longest comes from the 0.50 MeV gap: $\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{0.50 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}} = 2.5 \times 10^{-12}$ m.

### Technetium-99m in Medical Diagnosis

Technetium-99m is a **metastable** state of technetium-99, used as a **gamma source** for medical diagnosis. It is attached to a substance taken up by the organ being studied, and a gamma camera outside the body detects the radiation.

$${}^{99}_{42}\text{Mo} \to {}^{99\text{m}}_{\;\;43}\text{Tc} + {}^{\;\,0}_{-1}\beta + \bar{\nu}_e \qquad {}^{99\text{m}}_{\;\;43}\text{Tc} \to {}^{99}_{43}\text{Tc} + \gamma$$

| Property | Why it suits diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Emits **only gamma rays** | Gamma escapes the body to the detector and is weakly ionising. There are no alpha or beta particles to damage tissue |
| Half-life of about **6 hours** | Long enough to carry out the examination, short enough to limit the radiation dose to the patient |
| Made by β⁻ decay of **molybdenum-99**, a product of nuclear reactors | Molybdenum-99 has a half-life of 66 hours, long enough to survive transport to hospitals. The technetium-99m is separated from it at the hospital as it is needed |

## Nuclear Radius

Two experiments measure the size of a nucleus: the closest approach of alpha particles, and electron diffraction.

### Closest Approach of Alpha Particles

An alpha particle fired **head-on** at a nucleus slows as the electrostatic repulsion acts on it. At the distance of closest approach $r$ it is momentarily at rest, and all its initial kinetic energy has become **electric potential energy**. Using the Coulomb equation with charges $2e$ and $Ze$:

$$E_k = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} \qquad \text{so} \qquad r = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 E_k}$$

**Inline example:** an alpha particle with $8.0 \times 10^{-13}$ J of kinetic energy fired head-on at a gold nucleus ($Z = 79$):
$r = \frac{2 \times 79 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{4\pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 8.0 \times 10^{-13}} = 4.5 \times 10^{-14}$ m. The radius of a gold nucleus is about $7 \times 10^{-15}$ m, much smaller.

**Limitations:**

- The alpha particle stops and turns back **before reaching** the nucleus, so the closest-approach distance is larger than the radius. The method only gives an **upper limit**.
- The closest-approach distance depends on the alpha particle's **initial kinetic energy**, so the method does not give a single value for the radius.
- Alpha particles are made of hadrons. A high-energy alpha particle that comes within a few fm of the nucleus feels the **strong force**, which the Coulomb calculation ignores.
- The target nucleus **recoils** as the alpha particle approaches, which the simple calculation ignores.

### Electron Diffraction

A beam of high-energy electrons is fired at a thin target. Each electron has a de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$. At high enough energies this wavelength is comparable to the size of a nucleus, so the electrons **diffract around individual nuclei**. They are not diffracted by the gaps between nuclei, since the electron wavelength is far smaller than those gaps.

The electrons needed have energies of hundreds of MeV and travel close to the speed of light, so their wavelength is found from $\lambda \approx \frac{hc}{E}$, with $E$ the electron's energy in joules.

The pattern on a screen is a **central bright spot surrounded by dimmer concentric rings**.

**Graph of intensity against angle:** the intensity falls steeply from a central maximum as the angle increases, to a **first minimum** that does not reach zero, followed by a lower secondary maximum.

The angle $\theta$ of the **first minimum** gives the radius $R$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{1.22\lambda}{2R}$$

**Inline example:** electrons of wavelength $4.0 \times 10^{-15}$ m give a first minimum at $30°$, so $R = \frac{1.22 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-15}}{2 \times \sin 30°} = 4.9 \times 10^{-15}$ m.

### Comparing the Methods

| | Closest approach | Electron diffraction |
|---|---|---|
| Result | Upper limit only | Direct, more accurate value |
| Strong force | Affects alpha particles near the nucleus | Electrons are **leptons**, so unaffected |
| Depends on | The alpha particle's energy | The electron's wavelength |

### Typical Values

Nuclear radii are of the order $10^{-15}$ m, a few femtometres (1 fm $= 10^{-15}$ m).

## Radius and Nucleon Number

Experimental data show that nuclear radius depends on nucleon number $A$:

$$R = R_0 A^{1/3}$$

where $R_0$ is a constant of about 1.2 fm. Questions give the value to use.

**Inline example:** with $R_0 = 1.2$ fm, a nucleus with $A = 64$ has $R = 1.2 \times 64^{1/3} = 1.2 \times 4 = 4.8$ fm.

| Graph | Shape | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| $R$ against $A$ | Rises with a steadily decreasing gradient | $R$ is not proportional to $A$ |
| $R$ against $A^{1/3}$ | Straight line through the origin | Gradient $R_0$ |
| $\ln R$ against $\ln A$ | Straight line, from $\ln R = \ln R_0 + \frac{1}{3}\ln A$ | Gradient $\frac{1}{3}$, $y$-intercept $\ln R_0$ |

## Nuclear Density

Treat the nucleus as a sphere. Its volume is

$$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A$$

so $V \propto A$. The mass of the nucleus is about $Au$, where $u$ is the atomic mass unit. The density is

$$\rho = \frac{Au}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{3u}{4\pi R_0^3}$$

$A$ cancels, so nuclear density is **independent of nucleon number**: all nuclei have about the same density. This suggests that nucleons are **equally spaced** whatever the size of the nucleus.

**Inline example:** with $R_0 = 1.2$ fm, $\rho = \frac{3 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27}}{4\pi \times (1.2 \times 10^{-15})^3} = 2.3 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$.

The order of magnitude is $10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$ whatever value of $R_0$ is used. Nuclear density is far greater than the density of ordinary matter, about $10^3$ to $10^4\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$, which shows that:

- almost all of an atom's **mass** is concentrated in the nucleus
- the atom's **volume** is mostly empty space, with a nucleus tiny compared with the atom

## Key Terms and Equations Summary

| Term or equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Line of stability | Band of stable nuclei on an N-Z graph, close to $N = Z$ for light nuclei, $N > Z$ for heavy ones |
| β⁻ decay | Neutron-rich nuclei: $N - 1$, $Z + 1$ |
| β⁺ decay, electron capture | Proton-rich nuclei: $N + 1$, $Z - 1$ |

| Metastable state | Excited state that lasts much longer than usual, such as technetium-99m |

| $E_k = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ | Closest approach of an alpha particle. Gives an upper limit for radius |
| $\sin\theta = \frac{1.22\lambda}{2R}$ | First minimum of electron diffraction by a nucleus |
| $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$ | Nuclear radius from nucleon number, $R_0$ about 1.2 fm |
