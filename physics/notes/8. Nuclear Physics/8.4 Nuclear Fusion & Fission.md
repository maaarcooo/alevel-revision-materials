## Mass-Energy Equivalence

Mass and energy are interchangeable: mass can be converted into energy and energy into mass. The energy equivalent of a mass $m$ is

$$E = mc^2$$

**Inline example:** a mass of $2.0 \times 10^{-28}$ kg is equivalent to $E = 2.0 \times 10^{-28} \times (3.0 \times 10^8)^2 = 1.8 \times 10^{-11}$ J.

$E = mc^2$ applies to **all** energy changes, not only nuclear ones. A stretched spring stores elastic potential energy $E$, so its mass increases by $\frac{E}{c^2}$. The change is far too small to measure, but in nuclear reactions the energies are large enough for the mass change to matter.

### The Atomic Mass Unit

The **unified atomic mass unit** (u) is one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, $1.661 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. An atom with nucleon number $A$ has a mass of about $A$ u, so uranium-235 is about 235 u.

Its energy equivalent is **1 u = 931.5 MeV**, so a mass in u multiplied by 931.5 gives the energy in MeV.

## Mass Defect and Binding Energy

The mass of a nucleus is **less** than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. The **mass defect** of a nucleus is the difference between the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons and the mass of the nucleus:

$$\Delta m = Zm_p + (A - Z)m_n - m_{nucleus}$$

**Binding energy** is the energy required to separate a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons. It equals the energy released when the nucleus forms from separate protons and neutrons, and is the energy equivalent of the mass defect, $E = \Delta m c^2$. Nuclear binding energies are usually quoted in MeV.

Binding energy is not energy stored in the nucleus. It is the energy that would have to be supplied to pull all the nucleons apart.

**Inline example:** a helium-4 nucleus has mass 4.001506 u, with $m_p = 1.007276$ u and $m_n = 1.008665$ u. $\Delta m = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) - 4.001506 = 0.030376$ u, so the binding energy is $0.030376 \times 931.5 = 28.3$ MeV.

**Why nuclear reactions release far more energy than chemical reactions:** nuclear reactions change the binding energy of nuclei, which is millions of eV, whereas chemical reactions change the binding energy of electrons, a few eV.

## Fusion and Fission

| | Fusion | Fission |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | The joining together of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus | The splitting of a heavy nucleus into two smaller nuclei |
| Nuclei | Light, $A$ less than about 56 | Heavy, $A$ greater than about 56 |
| Energy released per kg of fuel | More | Less |
| Energy needed to start it | Much greater: extremely high temperature and density | Less: absorbing a thermal neutron |

### Fusion

- Two protons fuse to form deuterium. One proton becomes a neutron by **beta-plus decay**:
$${}^1_1\text{H} + {}^1_1\text{H} \to {}^2_1\text{H} + e^+ + \nu_e$$
- In the core of a star, four hydrogen nuclei fuse, through several steps, to form one helium-4 nucleus, releasing energy.
- Fusion research on Earth concentrates on **deuterium-tritium** (D-T) fusion:
$${}^2_1\text{H} + {}^3_1\text{H} \to {}^4_2\text{He} + {}^1_0\text{n}$$

**Why fusion needs high kinetic energy:** the nuclei must overcome the electrostatic repulsion between their protons to get close enough for the short-range strong nuclear force to act. This needs extremely high temperature and density, as in the core of a star.

### Induced Fission

**Induced fission** is fission of a heavy nucleus triggered by absorbing a slow-moving (thermal) neutron. When uranium-235 absorbs a neutron it becomes uranium-236, which splits almost immediately, so it is usually left out of fission equations.

$${}^1_0\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \to {}^{144}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{89}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^1_0\text{n}$$

The number of neutrons released follows from balancing nucleon numbers: $1 + 235 = 144 + 89 + 3$. A single fission produces two smaller daughter nuclei and **two or three neutrons**, and releases energy.

**Fission fragments** have a high $\frac{N}{Z}$ ratio, lying above the line of stability on an N-Z graph, because heavy nuclei need proportionally more neutrons than medium nuclei. They usually decay by **β⁻ emission**, in which a neutron changes into a proton. This is why spent fuel is so radioactive.

### Energy Released

In both fusion and fission, the products have a **higher binding energy per nucleon** than the reactants, so their total mass is **less**. The mass difference is released as energy, mostly as the kinetic energy of the products.

There are two ways to calculate it:

1. **From masses:** energy released = (total mass before $-$ total mass after) $\times$ 931.5 MeV per u.
2. **From binding energies:** energy released = total binding energy of the products $-$ total binding energy of the reactants. The total binding energy of a nucleus is its binding energy per nucleon multiplied by $A$.

**Inline example (from masses):** with nuclear masses ${}^2_1\text{H}$ = 2.01355 u, ${}^3_1\text{H}$ = 3.01550 u, ${}^4_2\text{He}$ = 4.00151 u and neutron = 1.00867 u, one D-T fusion has $\Delta m = 2.01355 + 3.01550 - 4.00151 - 1.00867 = 0.01887$ u, so it releases $0.01887 \times 931.5 = 17.6$ MeV.

**Inline example (from binding energies):** a nucleus with $A = 56$ and a binding energy per nucleon of 8.8 MeV has a total binding energy of $56 \times 8.8 = 493$ MeV.

## The Binding Energy per Nucleon Graph

**Binding energy per nucleon** is the binding energy of a nucleus divided by the number of nucleons in it. A higher binding energy per nucleon means a more **stable** nucleus.

The graph of average binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number $A$:

- rises **steeply** from the lightest nuclei
- peaks at about **8.8 MeV** near **iron-56**, the most stable nucleus
- then falls **gradually** for heavier nuclei, to about 7.6 MeV for uranium

| Feature | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Fusion releases energy for nuclei with $A$ less than about 56 | Joining light nuclei moves the products up the rising part of the curve |
| Fission releases energy for nuclei with $A$ greater than about 56 | Splitting heavy nuclei moves the products up towards the peak |
| Fusion releases more energy per nucleon than fission | The curve rises much more steeply at low $A$ than it falls at high $A$ |
| Very heavy nuclei have a lower binding energy per nucleon than iron | Electrostatic repulsion between the many protons begins to dominate over the short-range attractive strong force |
| Helium-4, carbon-12 and oxygen-16 lie above the general trend | They are especially stable. Carbon-12 and oxygen-16 can be pictured as three and four helium-4 nuclei bound together |

When sketching the curve, do not start it at $A = 0$, label the peak near iron-56, and mark helium-4 above the line.

## Thermal Nuclear Reactors

### Thermal Neutrons and the Chain Reaction

A **thermal neutron** is a neutron in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings: slow-moving, with low kinetic energy. Its mean kinetic energy is $\frac{3}{2}kT$. Thermal neutrons are used because they are **much more likely to be absorbed** by uranium-235 nuclei than fast neutrons.

**Inline example:** at 300 K, $\frac{3}{2}kT = \frac{3}{2} \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 = 6.2 \times 10^{-21}$ J, about 0.04 eV. Fission neutrons start with energies of the order of 1 MeV.

A **chain reaction** happens when neutrons released by each fission go on to cause further fissions. The reaction is **steady and controlled** when, on average, exactly **one** neutron from each fission causes another fission.

**Critical mass** is the minimum mass of fuel required to maintain a steady chain reaction.

| Mass of fuel | What happens |
|---|---|
| Less than critical | Neutrons are lost from the surface faster than fission creates them, so the reaction dies out |
| Critical | Steady chain reaction |
| More than critical, with nothing absorbing the excess neutrons | Fission creates neutrons faster than they are lost, so the reaction rate rises and can run away |

A reactor must control two properties of the free neutrons: their **number** and their **energy**.

### Moderator

The **moderator** slows fast neutrons down to thermal speeds. The neutrons make repeated **elastic collisions** with moderator nuclei, transferring kinetic energy to them.

**Mechanical model:** in a head-on elastic collision between a moving ball and a stationary one of equal mass, the moving ball stops and passes on all its kinetic energy. If the stationary ball is much heavier, the moving ball bounces back with almost all its kinetic energy. So the closer a moderator nucleus is in mass to a neutron, the more kinetic energy the neutron loses in each collision. For a head-on collision with a nucleus of mass $M$, a neutron of mass $m$ loses a fraction $\frac{4mM}{(m + M)^2}$ of its kinetic energy.

**Inline example:** for carbon-12 nuclei, $\frac{4 \times 1 \times 12}{(1 + 12)^2} = 0.28$, so a neutron loses up to 28% of its kinetic energy per collision, and needs many collisions to become thermal.

| Requirement | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Light nuclei**, close in mass to a neutron | Each elastic collision removes a large share of the neutron's kinetic energy, so fewer collisions are needed |
| A **poor absorber** of neutrons | Neutrons must be slowed down, not removed |

Common moderators: **water** and **graphite**.

### Control Rods

**Control rods** absorb neutrons, controlling the rate of fission.

- Inserting them further into the core absorbs more neutrons, so the rate of fission **decreases**. Raising them increases it.
- Their position is adjusted so that, on average, one neutron per fission causes another fission.
- The material must absorb neutrons without itself undergoing fission or becoming dangerously unstable.

Common control rod materials: **boron** and **cadmium**.

### Coolant

The **coolant** carries thermal energy from the core to the **heat exchanger**, which also keeps the core at a safe temperature. In the heat exchanger, the hot coolant heats water in a separate circuit, producing steam that drives the turbines.

A good liquid coolant has:

- a **high specific heat capacity**, so it can carry large amounts of thermal energy
- a **low viscosity**, so it flows easily
- **little absorption of neutrons**

Water is the commonest coolant and can also act as the moderator. Other coolants are **carbon dioxide gas** and **liquid sodium**.

## Safety

| Aspect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Fuel** | **Enriched uranium**: uranium in which the proportion of uranium-235 has been increased to a few per cent. Only uranium-235 undergoes fission in a thermal reactor. Uranium-238 may absorb or scatter a thermal neutron, but does not undergo fission |
| **Remote handling** | Fuel rods are handled remotely, by machines, to protect workers |
| **Shielding** | The core is surrounded by thick **steel** and **concrete** to absorb the neutrons and gamma radiation escaping from it |
| **Emergency shut-down** | The control rods are fully inserted, absorbing the free neutrons so fission stops |

### Radioactive Waste

Some waste must be stored for a very long time because it contains isotopes with **very long half-lives**.

| Category | Examples | Treatment and storage |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-level** | Lightly contaminated clothing, gloves or tools | Encased in concrete and buried a few metres underground |
| **Intermediate-level** | Mainly from **decommissioning** (dismantling) nuclear power stations | Encased in cement in steel drums and stored securely |
| **High-level** | **Spent fuel rods** and the unusable fission products from them | See below |

High-level waste is the most dangerous: it is highly radioactive and remains radioactive for thousands of years. It is treated in stages:

1. **Cooling ponds:** spent fuel is stored in ponds of water near the reactor for several years, because it is extremely hot as well as highly radioactive. It is moved remotely.
2. **Reprocessing:** plutonium and uranium are extracted for reuse.
3. **Vitrification:** the remaining waste is mixed with molten glass and left to solidify.
4. **Final storage:** sealed in steel containers deep underground, in a geologically stable area, where it is less likely to be disturbed or released by natural disasters.

## Risks and Benefits of Nuclear Power

Knowing the physics of nuclear energy lets society make informed decisions about using it.

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| No carbon dioxide or sulphur dioxide emitted during operation, and lifetime greenhouse gas emissions significantly lower than coal or gas stations | Radioactive waste is dangerous and expensive to deal with, and some must be stored for thousands of years |
| Continuous output that does not depend on the weather, unlike wind or solar | Not renewable: its uranium fuel is mined and will eventually run out |
| Far more energy released per kilogram of fuel than fossil fuels, so only small amounts of fuel are needed | A meltdown, such as Chernobyl, can have catastrophic consequences for the environment and nearby people |
| Useful by-products such as medical isotopes, for example molybdenum-99 for technetium-99m | Building and decommissioning power stations is expensive and slow |
